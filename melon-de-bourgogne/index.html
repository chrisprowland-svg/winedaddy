What Is Melon de Bourgogne? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what Melon de Bourgogne is, how it relates to Muscadet, what its wines typically taste like, what “sur lie” means and how Australian drinkers may encounter it.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Melon de Bourgogne is a white wine-grape variety.
  • It is best known for producing Muscadet in France’s western Loire.
  • Muscadet is an appellation and wine designation; Melon de Bourgogne is the grape.
  • Wines made from the grape are commonly dry, fresh and high in acidity.
  • “Sur lie” means the wine has matured on fine lees, which can add texture and richness.
  • In Australia, you are most likely to encounter it through imported Muscadet.

Quick answer: what is Melon de Bourgogne?

Melon de Bourgogne is a white wine grape, formally listed as Melon B in international grape-variety references. It is most strongly associated with Muscadet, the dry white wines of France’s western Loire, around Nantes.

The names are related but not interchangeable:

  • Melon de Bourgogne is the grape variety.
  • Muscadet is the French appellation and wine designation principally made from that grape.
  • Muscadet sur lie is a Muscadet wine made under the appellation’s regulated “sur lie” conditions.

So, Melon de Bourgogne is not a melon-flavoured wine. Nor is it the same grape as Muscat. Its wines are typically dry, fresh and lively, often with restrained fruit or floral aromas rather than a loud, overtly fruity style.

For an Australian wine drinker, the practical takeaway is simple: if you see Muscadet, you are usually looking at a wine whose identity is closely tied to Melon de Bourgogne, even if the grape name is not displayed prominently.

Where is Melon de Bourgogne grown?

Melon de Bourgogne is most closely associated with the Muscadet vineyard in the western Loire Valley, particularly around Nantes and southern Loire-Atlantique. The wider Muscadet area also extends into parts of Maine-et-Loire and Vendée.

This is a specific part of the Loire, not a synonym for all Loire Valley wine. Muscadet is separate from better-known Loire appellations such as Sancerre and Pouilly-Fumé, which are principally associated with Sauvignon Blanc.

You may also see names such as:

  • Muscadet Sèvre et Maine
  • Muscadet Côtes de Grandlieu
  • Muscadet Coteaux de la Loire

These are appellation names within the Muscadet family. They describe place and production rules, not different grape varieties.

What does Melon de Bourgogne wine taste like?

Wines made from Melon de Bourgogne are commonly:

  • dry;
  • light- to medium-bodied;
  • high in acidity;
  • fresh and refreshing;
  • delicate in fruit or floral expression.

You might notice restrained citrus or other subtle fruit characters, floral notes and a clean, energetic finish. The grape is often less immediately aromatic than Sauvignon Blanc, but that does not mean the wine is flavourless.

Here, dry refers mainly to the amount of residual sugar left in the wine. It does not mean the wine has no aroma or personality.

The final style can vary. Vineyard site, yields, ripeness, lees contact, vessel and winemaking choices all influence the result. A wine made from Melon de Bourgogne is therefore not guaranteed to be neutral, light-bodied, unoaked or saline.

What does “sur lie” mean?

Sur lie means “on the lees”. Lees are the fine yeast sediment left after fermentation. Rather than separating the wine from all of this sediment immediately, the winemaker allows it to mature in contact with the fine lees before bottling.

In the Muscadet context, this can add texture and richness while preserving the wine’s freshness. Some examples may also have a slight natural prickle from carbon dioxide formed during fermentation.

That does not make the wine sparkling. It also does not mean that every sur lie wine will taste creamy, yeasty or the same as every other example. “Sur lie” is a useful clue about the wine’s production and likely texture, not a guarantee of one flavour profile.

How might you encounter it in Australia?

Australian drinkers are most likely to meet Melon de Bourgogne through an imported bottle labelled:

  • Muscadet;
  • Muscadet sur lie;
  • Muscadet Sèvre et Maine;
  • Muscadet Sèvre et Maine sur lie;
  • Melon de Bourgogne;
  • Melon B.

If a label says Muscadet, think of a French appellation and wine designation. If it says Melon de Bourgogne, think of the grape variety. A wine labelled with the grape name may not be a Muscadet appellation wine, so check the geographic and appellation wording separately.

Melon de Bourgogne is recognised for Australian grape-labelling purposes, but authoritative public evidence for substantial Australian plantings, production volumes or national importance was not located. It is safest to describe the variety as uncommon or niche in Australia rather than claim that it is absent.

Muscadet is the French appellation name you are most likely to encounter on imported wine. Whether that name can be used for an Australian-produced wine is a separate regulatory question and should not be assumed from the grape’s labelling recognition.

What food goes with Melon de Bourgogne?

The classic starting point is seafood. The grape’s freshness and acidity can work well with fish, shellfish and lighter vegetable dishes.

That is a suggestion, not a rule. Try a fresh Muscadet with oysters, prawns, grilled fish or a simple vegetable dish, but do not assume every bottle is made only for seafood. A richer, more textured example may suit a wider range of foods.

Serve it chilled, while remembering that very cold temperatures can mute aroma and texture. If the wine seems quiet straight from the fridge, give it a little time in the glass.

Frequently asked questions

Is Melon de Bourgogne the same as Muscadet?

No. Melon de Bourgogne is the grape variety. Muscadet is the French appellation and wine designation principally made from it.

Does Melon de Bourgogne taste like melon fruit?

No. The name refers to the grape’s historical naming, not a promise of fresh-melon flavour.

Is Melon de Bourgogne the same as Muscat?

No. Muscat is a different grape group. The similar-looking names can be confusing, but the varieties and their typical wine styles are distinct.

Is Muscadet always made from Melon de Bourgogne?

Within the Muscadet appellation, the wine is made from the grape known in French and international references as Melon B, or Melon de Bourgogne. However, not every wine labelled Melon de Bourgogne is necessarily a Muscadet appellation wine.

Is sur lie wine sparkling?

No. Sur lie means the wine has matured on fine lees. Some examples may have a slight natural prickle, but that is not the same as being sparkling wine.
