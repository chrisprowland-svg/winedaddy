What Is Sulfur Dioxide in Wine? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what sulphur dioxide and sulphites do in wine, why winemakers use them, how naturally occurring and added sulphites differ, and what “contains sulphites” means in Australia.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Sulphur dioxide, or SO₂, is used in winemaking to help protect wine from oxidation and unwanted microbial activity.
  • In Australia, you will usually see the terms sulphur dioxide and sulphites.
  • Some sulphites form naturally during fermentation; others are deliberately added by the winemaker.
  • “Contains sulphites” does not mean a wine is poor quality or unsafe for most people.
  • Free, bound and total SO₂ describe how sulphur dioxide exists in the finished wine.
  • SO₂ can help preserve freshness, colour and stability, but it does not prevent every wine fault.
  • Sulphur dioxide is not the same as carbon dioxide, elemental sulphur or hydrogen sulphide.

Quick answer: what does sulphur dioxide do in wine?

Sulphur dioxide is a wine-preservation tool. It helps limit oxidation—chemical changes caused by oxygen—and suppress some unwanted yeasts and bacteria. In simple terms, it helps protect a wine while it is being made, matured, moved and bottled.

You may also see SO₂, the chemical abbreviation, or sulphites, the Australian consumer and labelling term for sulphite compounds associated with sulphur dioxide use.

Some sulphites can form naturally during alcoholic fermentation. Winemakers may also deliberately add sulphur dioxide or sulphite-based preservatives at different stages. The finished wine can therefore contain both naturally occurring and deliberately added sulphites.

The important point is that sulphur dioxide is a tool, not a quality score. Its effect depends on the wine’s chemistry, oxygen exposure, microbial risks and the wider winemaking process.

Why do winemakers use sulphur dioxide?

To help limit oxidation

Oxygen can gradually change wine, reducing freshness and altering colour, aroma and flavour. Sulphur dioxide helps slow some of these reactions.

That does not mean it “stops oxidation”. A wine can still oxidise if it is exposed to oxygen or is not otherwise protected. Sulphur dioxide helps reduce the risk and effects of oxidation when managed appropriately.

To support microbial stability

Sulphur dioxide can inhibit or suppress some unwanted microorganisms. This may help reduce spoilage and support stability, particularly in wines with factors such as residual sugar that can make microbial control more challenging.

It is not a universal steriliser, however. Hygiene, alcohol, acidity, pH, temperature, filtration and oxygen management can all influence whether a wine remains stable. Sulphur dioxide does not guarantee that a wine will be free from every fault.

When might SO₂ be managed?

Sulphur dioxide may be managed during several parts of winemaking, including:

  • receiving or crushing grapes;
  • handling juice or must;
  • fermentation;
  • maturation and transfers;
  • blending, clarification and packaging; and
  • bottling.

The exact approach varies with the wine, grape condition, pH, oxygen exposure and microbial risk. This is why a beginner explanation should focus on the purpose of SO₂ rather than dosing formulas or cellar recipes.

Naturally occurring versus added sulphites

This distinction is easy to miss.

Fermenting yeast can produce small amounts of sulphite compounds. As a result, a wine made without deliberate sulphite additions may still contain measurable sulphites.

A useful way to think about it is:

“No added sulphites” does not automatically mean “zero sulphites”.

Deliberately added sulphur dioxide may later react with other wine compounds, become bound or dissipate. The amount present in the finished bottle is not necessarily the same as the total amount ever added.

Be cautious with the phrase sulphite-free. It can imply more certainty than the evidence or labelling rules support. “No added sulphites” is a more precise concept, but any product claim must meet the applicable Australian requirements.

Free, bound and total SO₂

These terms describe different forms of sulphur dioxide in wine:

  • Free SO₂ is the unbound fraction that is more immediately available to provide protection.
  • Bound SO₂ has reacted with other wine compounds and is less immediately available.
  • Total SO₂ is the combined amount of free and bound SO₂.

A simple analogy is to imagine free SO₂ as the available portion, bound SO₂ as the portion already attached to something else, and total SO₂ as the sum of both.

Free SO₂ is not exactly the same as molecular SO₂, a particularly active chemical form within the free fraction. That distinction matters to winemakers and laboratories, but it is not necessary for reading a wine label.

What does “contains sulphites” mean in Australia?

For Australian packaged food, FSANZ guidance says added sulphites must be declared when present at concentrations of 10 milligrams per kilogram (10 mg/kg) or more.

An Australian consumer may see wording such as:

  • “Contains sulphites”;
  • “Preservative 220”; or
  • “Preservative: sulphur dioxide”.

The exact presentation can vary according to the label and applicable requirements. The 10 mg/kg declaration threshold is not the same as saying that a wine below that level contains no sulphites. Nor is it a general medical safety threshold.

“Contains sulphites” is a declaration, not a judgement about quality. It does not mean the wine is unsafe for most people.

Are sulphites harmful?

Most people can drink wine containing declared sulphites without a sulphite-related reaction. Some sulphite-sensitive people, particularly some people with asthma, may react to sulphites.

This is different from saying that sulphites cause problems for everyone. Sulphites are often blamed for wine headaches, but the evidence does not support treating them as the universal explanation. Wine reactions can have several possible contributors, including alcohol and individual sensitivities.

If you repeatedly experience symptoms after drinking wine, speak with a qualified health professional. This article cannot diagnose sulphite sensitivity or advise on treatment. People with asthma or a suspected reaction should follow their clinician’s advice.

Sulphite sensitivity should also not be confused with an allergy to sulphonamide antibiotics. They are different substances.

What sulphur compounds are not the same as SO₂?

The word “sulphur” covers several different substances:

  • Elemental sulphur is not sulphur dioxide.
  • Hydrogen sulphide is a different compound associated with reductive, rotten-egg-like aromas.
  • Carbon dioxide is a different gas. It is the gas responsible for the bubbles in sparkling wine.

Understanding these differences helps prevent a common mix-up: a wine’s sulphur-related aroma is not automatically evidence of sulphur dioxide, and sulphur dioxide is not the same thing as carbon dioxide.

The takeaway

Sulphur dioxide is one of the winemaker’s tools for protecting wine from oxidation and unwanted microbial activity. Some sulphites occur naturally during fermentation, while others are deliberately added. Australian labels declare relevant added sulphites at or above the applicable 10 mg/kg threshold.

The phrase “contains sulphites” is useful information, but it is not a quality warning or a universal health warning. The best way to understand SO₂ is as part of a wider system of wine stability—not as the explanation for every fault, headache or difference between wines.

Frequently asked questions

Does all wine contain sulphites?

Not necessarily in the same amount. Some sulphites may form naturally during fermentation, and winemakers may also add sulphur dioxide. A wine made without added sulphites may still contain naturally occurring sulphites.

Do sulphites cause wine headaches?

Sulphites are often blamed, but they are not a proven universal explanation for wine headaches. Symptoms can have several possible causes. Repeated reactions should be discussed with a health professional.

Does “contains sulphites” mean a wine is bad?

No. The statement is a regulatory declaration about sulphite presence. It is not a judgement about the wine’s quality.

What is the difference between free and total SO₂?

Free SO₂ is the more immediately available fraction. Total SO₂ includes both free and bound SO₂.
