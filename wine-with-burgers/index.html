Wine with burgers: how to choose the right style

Learn how to choose wine with burgers by matching the patty, toppings, sauce, smoke and spice. Includes Australian Shiraz, Grenache, Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Noir, rosé, sparkling and white wine styles.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • There is no single best wine with burgers: the patty, toppings and sauce all matter.
  • For many beef burgers, start with a fresh, fruit-forward red with moderate tannin and enough acidity.
  • Australian Shiraz, Grenache, medium-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon and some Pinot Noir are useful starting points.
  • Fat and protein can make moderate tannin feel more integrated, but firm tannin may taste harsh with salty or bitter toppings.
  • Pickles, tomato, mustard and mayonnaise favour freshness, while barbecue sauce and caramelised onion call for fruitiness.
  • Chilli and high alcohol can be an uncomfortable combination; sparkling, rosé, fresher reds or wines with some sweetness may work better.
  • Dry wine can still taste richly fruity. “Dry” describes sweetness, not a lack of flavour.

Quick answer: what wine goes with burgers?

For a straightforward beef burger, try a juicy red with fresh fruit, moderate tannin and refreshing acidity. Australian Shiraz, Grenache, medium-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon and some Pinot Noir can all be sensible starting points.

The important thing is to match the whole burger, not just the meat. A smoky beef burger with bacon and barbecue sauce needs a different wine from a light chicken burger with mayonnaise, lettuce and pickles. Once you consider the patty, cooking method, cheese, sauce and spice, the choice becomes much easier.

This is an application of the broader principles explained in How Wine Pairing Works: match intensity first, then look at acidity, sweetness, tannin, body and texture.

Why burgers can be surprisingly difficult to pair

A burger brings several strong sensations together:

  • Fat and richness from the patty, cheese, bacon or mayonnaise
  • Salt from the meat, cheese, pickles and sauces
  • Umami, the savoury depth found in cooked meat, mushrooms and some plant-based patties
  • Char and smoke from a barbecue or hot grill
  • Acidity from tomato, pickles, mustard and slaw
  • Sweetness from barbecue sauce, relish or caramelised onion
  • Heat from chilli sauce or spicy seasoning

These ingredients can change how a wine tastes. Fat may make moderate tannin feel more integrated, but salty, bitter or umami-rich toppings can make some firm reds seem more bitter or metallic. A wine with good freshness can feel livelier beside rich toppings, while a very sweet sauce may make a completely dry wine seem austere.

A simple way to choose

1. Match the burger’s intensity

A plain grilled chicken or vegetable burger usually does not need a powerful red. A large beef burger with bacon, mature cheese and smoky barbecue sauce can handle more body and flavour.

Think in broad terms:

  • Lighter burger: rosé, sparkling wine, Pinot Noir or a fresh white
  • Medium-intensity burger: Grenache, moderate Shiraz, Pinot Noir or a fuller rosé
  • Rich, smoky burger: medium- to fuller-bodied Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon

These are starting points, not rankings. The style of each grape can vary considerably.

2. Adjust for the toppings

Fresh, acidic toppings such as tomato, pickles, mustard and slaw generally make brightness useful. Try a fresh red, dry rosé, sparkling wine or a high-acid white. A soft, low-acid wine may seem flat beside them.

Cheddar, bacon and mayonnaise add salt and richness. A red with moderate tannin and enough acidity may work well, but very firm tannin can exaggerate bitterness beside sharp cheese or salty toppings. For more on this interaction, see Wine and Cheese Pairing.

Sweet barbecue sauce, tomato sauce, relish and caramelised onion call for a wine with plenty of fruit character. A completely dry, austere red may taste hard beside a sweet sauce. With substantial chilli, consider a fresher, lower-alcohol style, sparkling wine, rosé or a wine with some perceptible sweetness. Alcohol can intensify the sensation of burning, so a powerful, high-alcohol red is not automatically the safest choice. Wine and Spicy Food Pairing explains this principle in more detail.

Choosing by burger style

Beef burgers

Beef is a useful default because it often combines savoury flavour, fat, salt and grill char. A fruit-forward red with moderate tannin is a reliable place to begin.

Shiraz can suit beef, particularly when the burger is smoky or generously dressed. Grenache can offer red fruit and moderate structure without feeling too heavy. Medium-bodied Cabernet Sauvignon may work with a substantial grilled patty, mushrooms or mature cheese, but heavily structured Cabernet can overwhelm a casual burger.

For a lighter beef burger with tomato, pickles or delicate toppings, Pinot Noir may be more comfortable than a powerful red.

Lamb burgers

Lamb can work with Grenache, Shiraz, Pinot Noir or dry rosé. Choose according to the seasoning and toppings. Herbs and fresh sauces may suit a fresher wine, while smoky eggplant, bacon or barbecue sauce can support more body.

The right comparison is not “lamb always needs a big red”. A lean lamb patty with yoghurt-style sauce is a different pairing from a heavily charred lamb burger with sweet sauce.

Pork burgers

Pork burgers vary widely. A lighter grilled pork burger with apple, slaw, mustard or fresh herbs may suit rosé, sparkling wine or a fresh white. A richer, smoky pork burger with barbecue sauce may work with a fruitier red.

Grenache can be a useful middle ground, while a soft red may be preferable to a very tannic Cabernet when the burger includes sweetness.

Chicken burgers

Match the preparation rather than the word “chicken”. A lightly grilled chicken burger with mayonnaise and lettuce may suit Chardonnay, sparkling wine, rosé or another fresh white. Pinot Noir or a fuller white can handle more grill char.

Crumbed, fried, smoky or spicy chicken burgers need more adjustment. Richness may favour sparkling wine, while chilli makes lower alcohol and freshness more important.

Vegetarian and plant-based burgers

Vegetarian and plant-based burgers are not one sensory category. Mushroom, lentil, bean, soy-based and vegetable patties can differ in savouriness, sweetness, bitterness, smoke and texture.

Pinot Noir, Grenache, rosé, sparkling wine and fresh whites can all work, depending on the patty and toppings. A strongly mushroomy or umami-rich burger may make a heavily oaked or tannic red taste harsh. If that happens, move towards a softer, fresher wine.

Fish burgers

A fish burger with lemon, pickles or mayonnaise generally points towards sparkling wine, rosé or a fresh white such as Sauvignon Blanc or Chardonnay. A heavily grilled fish burger with smoky toppings may need a fuller white or lighter red, but the sauce remains decisive.

What the main wine styles contribute

  • Shiraz: fruit, body and moderate structure for beef, lamb and smoky burgers. Avoid assuming every Australian Shiraz is full-bodied or high in alcohol.
  • Cabernet Sauvignon: structure for substantial beef burgers, grilled flavours, mushrooms and mature cheese. Firm tannin is not always an advantage.
  • Grenache: red fruit and generally approachable structure for beef, lamb, pork and plant-based burgers.
  • Pinot Noir: a lighter red option for chicken, pork, mushrooms, lighter beef and vegetarian burgers.
  • Dry rosé: freshness and versatility for chicken, pork, pickles, slaw and lighter sauces. Dry does not mean flavourless.
  • Sparkling wine: acidity and carbonation can make rich, salty or fried toppings feel refreshing.
  • White wine: fresh styles suit chicken, fish, pork and acidic toppings; fuller Chardonnay can handle grilled texture, cheese or richer sauces.

Serving the wine

For an informal Australian barbecue or burger night, serve red wine slightly cool rather than warm, especially outdoors. Chill rosé, sparkling and white wines so their freshness is preserved.

Most casual burger pairings do not need decanting or specialist glassware. Pour the wine, take a sip with the burger and notice what happens. If the wine tastes harsh, try a softer style. If it seems flat, choose more freshness. If the sauce makes it taste bitter, look for more fruit or some sweetness.

The most useful rule is simple: match the burger’s intensity first, then adjust for sauce, toppings and heat.

Suggested FAQs

Is Shiraz the best wine with burgers?

Not universally. Shiraz can suit many beef, lamb and smoky burgers, but a lighter burger or one with chilli, pickles or delicate toppings may work better with Pinot Noir, rosé, sparkling or white wine.

Can you drink white wine with a burger?

Yes. Fresh or fuller white wines can work with chicken, fish, pork and vegetarian burgers, especially with mayonnaise, pickles, slaw, herbs or fresh sauces.

What wine goes with a burger with barbecue sauce?

Choose a fruit-forward wine with enough body for the smoky flavours. Grenache, Shiraz, sparkling rosé or a robust rosé may work. Avoid assuming that the biggest, most tannic red will be best: sweetness in the sauce can make firm tannin taste bitter.

What wine goes with a spicy burger?

Try sparkling wine, rosé, a fresh lower-alcohol red or a white wine with some sweetness. High alcohol can intensify the sensation of chilli heat.
