Conegliano Valdobbiadene is a defined wine-growing area in Veneto, north-eastern Italy. It is also the geographic setting for the protected designation Conegliano Valdobbiadene — Prosecco, an Italian DOCG and European Union Protected Designation of Origin.

For an Australian wine learner, the most important distinction is simple: Conegliano Valdobbiadene is not another name for all Prosecco. It identifies a particular hilly area and its regulated wines. A bottle labelled only “Prosecco” may come from the much wider Prosecco DOC area.

Highlights

  • It is in Veneto, in northern Italy.
  • The regulated production area covers parts of 15 municipalities, stretching between Conegliano and Valdobbiadene.
  • Its principal grape is Glera, the grape associated with Prosecco.
  • The best-known expression is Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore DOCG.
  • The designation also includes frizzante and still wines.
  • Rive and Superiore di Cartizze are more specific regulated expressions.
  • “Extra Dry” is generally sweeter than “Brut”, despite the name.
  • Hills, slopes, elevation and changing aspects help explain why wines from different sites may taste different.

Where is Conegliano Valdobbiadene?

Conegliano Valdobbiadene sits in Veneto, between the Venetian plain and the foothills of the Dolomites. The area runs broadly north-west from Conegliano towards Valdobbiadene.

The defining municipalities include Conegliano, Valdobbiadene, Vittorio Veneto, Pieve di Soligo, Farra di Soligo, Follina, Miane and Vidor, as well as San Vendemiano, Colle Umberto, Tarzo, Cison di Valmarino, San Pietro di Feletto, Refrontolo and Susegana.

These towns help you understand the landscape, but a town name and a legal wine designation are not automatically interchangeable. The protected production area is defined by regulation.

From Venice, the area is approximately 50 kilometres north-west. As a travel orientation, allow roughly 60–75 minutes by road, depending on your starting point, route and traffic. That is a useful sense of scale rather than a fixed journey time.

The existing Italy and Veneto guides provide broader geographic context between the national, regional and local scales.

What does Conegliano Valdobbiadene mean on a wine label?

The formal protected name is Conegliano Valdobbiadene — Prosecco. You may also encounter forms such as:

  • Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore DOCG
  • Conegliano — Prosecco
  • Valdobbiadene — Prosecco
  • Rive
  • Superiore di Cartizze

In an Australian bottle shop, “Prosecco” may be the most visually prominent word because it is the familiar wine name. “Conegliano Valdobbiadene” gives more specific geographic information when it is legally used on the label.

A useful rule is:

If a bottle says only “Prosecco”, do not assume it comes from Conegliano Valdobbiadene. If it names Conegliano Valdobbiadene, it points to the protected hill area around the two defining poles of Conegliano and Valdobbiadene.

The precise arrangement of words can vary between imported labels, so read the full front and back label rather than relying on one large word.

Conegliano Valdobbiadene, Prosecco and Glera: what is the difference?

These terms describe different things:

  • Conegliano Valdobbiadene is a geographic area and designation context.
  • Prosecco is the broader protected wine name and familiar style category.
  • Glera is the principal grape variety.
  • Veneto is the Italian region containing Conegliano Valdobbiadene.
  • DOCG is part of Italy’s regulated designation system.

The wider Prosecco DOC covers a substantially larger area across parts of Veneto and Friuli-Venezia Giulia. Conegliano Valdobbiadene is therefore a more specific hill-zone designation within the wider Prosecco landscape, not a synonym for every Prosecco.

Glera should not be treated as another name for Prosecco. European regulation moved away from using “Prosecco” as the grape name, helping distinguish the variety from the protected geographical name.

For a fuller explanation of the wine itself, see Prosecco. This page focuses on the place and what its name tells you.

What grapes are used?

Glera is the principal grape in Conegliano Valdobbiadene wines. It provides the foundation for the region’s familiar sparkling style, including its fruit, floral character and refreshing acidity.

Regional technical material also identifies Glera Lunga, Verdiso, Perera and Bianchetta Trevigiana among the permitted or locally relevant varieties. Exact permissions and percentages can depend on the category and current specification, so the legal production rules remain the controlling reference.

The key beginner takeaway is that the place name tells you more than the grape name alone. Glera is grown in other areas, while Conegliano Valdobbiadene describes a particular regulated origin.

What wines are made there?

The best-known wine is sparkling Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore DOCG. The designation also covers:

  • Sparkling wine, with the most visible pressure and persistent bubbles.
  • Frizzante, a semi-sparkling style with lower pressure.
  • Tranquillo, or still wine.
  • Sui lieviti, a bottle-fermented expression in which the fermentation sediment remains in the bottle.

This means the region is not legally limited to one single type of wine, even though sparkling wine dominates how most drinkers encounter it.

The principal sparkling style is associated with tank fermentation, a method that helps preserve fresh fruit and aromatic character. That does not mean every wine from the designation is made in exactly the same way. The existence of sui lieviti is a reminder that the rules allow a more unusual bottle-fermented expression as well.

This is different from saying that the region is a traditional-method sparkling region. For background, compare tank-method sparkling wine with traditional-method sparkling wine.

What do Brut, Extra Dry and Dry mean?

Conegliano Valdobbiadene sparkling wines may use regulated sweetness terms including:

  • Extra Brut
  • Brut
  • Extra Dry
  • Dry

These describe residual-sugar ranges, not simply how dry the wine feels in everyday conversation. The easily missed point is that Extra Dry is generally sweeter than Brut.

That does not mean Extra Dry will taste sweet like a dessert wine. The wine’s acidity, bubbles and fruit character affect how the sweetness is perceived. If you prefer a crisper, less-sweet style, Brut or Extra Brut may be a useful starting point; if you enjoy a softer impression, Extra Dry may suit you.

What are Rive and Superiore di Cartizze?

Rive is a more specific category associated with steep hillside sites. It identifies a single municipality or defined local area and comes with stricter production requirements, including lower permitted yields and hand harvesting. Rive wines also identify the vintage.

Rive is best understood as a more detailed geographic and production description. It is not an automatic guarantee that every Rive wine will be better than every other wine.

Superiore di Cartizze refers to a defined subzone near Valdobbiadene, associated with San Pietro di Barbozza, Santo Stefano and Saccol. It is a regulated expression within the Conegliano Valdobbiadene framework, rather than a separate subject to confuse with the wider Prosecco DOC.

“Superiore” should not be read as a universal international quality ranking. It forms part of the regulated naming system and should be considered alongside the complete designation and category.

Why are the hills important?

Conegliano Valdobbiadene is a hilly landscape rather than a single uniform plain. Vineyard elevations are broadly described as ranging from about 100 to 500 metres. Slopes face different directions, and exposure, drainage and local soils can change over relatively short distances.

Those differences may contribute to variation in ripeness, acidity, fruit character, floral notes and palate texture. A vineyard on a sunny slope may experience different conditions from one with a cooler aspect or greater shelter.

This is a useful way to think about terroir: place can influence the conditions in which grapes grow, but it does not dictate a single flavour for every bottle. A label from Conegliano Valdobbiadene tells you about regulated origin; it cannot predict every winemaking decision or tasting result.

What should you expect in the glass?

As a broad tendency, the principal sparkling wines are likely to show fresh fruit, floral aromas, lively acidity and the light, refreshing texture associated with sparkling Prosecco styles.

The hills add another layer of interest. Wines from different sites or categories may vary in concentration, acidity, fruit expression and texture. Rive and Cartizze labels offer more specific geographic information, but neither guarantees one fixed flavour profile.

Serve the wine chilled, but not so cold that its aromas disappear. A standard sparkling-wine glass is a practical choice, and the wine can be enjoyed on its own or with light foods, seafood, antipasti and many Australian summer dishes. The most useful approach is to treat these as starting points rather than strict rules.

What does DOCG mean for Australians?

DOCG is an Italian designation category. It sets rules around geographic origin, permitted grapes, production and labelling. It is useful evidence that a wine belongs to a regulated Italian designation.

It is not exactly the same as an Australian Geographical Indication, and DOCG should not be treated as a universal score or guarantee that one bottle will taste better than another. The systems protect origin and names through different legal frameworks.

For more context, see Italian DOC and DOCG and Protected wine names.

The bottom line

Conegliano Valdobbiadene is a specific hilly wine area in Veneto, not a synonym for all Prosecco. Its protected designation is built around Glera and is best known for sparkling Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore DOCG.

When you see the name on a label, think of it as a more precise place-and-designation clue. Then look at the category, sweetness term and any additional geographic wording such as Rive or Superiore di Cartizze. That is enough information to make the label feel less mysterious without expecting the name alone to tell you exactly how every bottle will taste.

Frequently asked questions

Is Conegliano Valdobbiadene the same as Prosecco?

No. Conegliano Valdobbiadene is a specific protected hill area and designation context. Prosecco is the broader wine name and production landscape. A bottle labelled only Prosecco does not necessarily come from Conegliano Valdobbiadene.

Is Conegliano Valdobbiadene a DOCG?

Yes. The protected designation Conegliano Valdobbiadene — Prosecco is recognised as an Italian DOCG and an EU Protected Designation of Origin.

What grape is used in Conegliano Valdobbiadene?

Glera is the principal grape. Some other permitted or locally relevant varieties may also be used under the production rules.

Is Extra Dry sweeter than Brut?

Generally, yes. In this terminology, Extra Dry is usually sweeter than Brut. The names do not follow ordinary conversational expectations.

What is Rive?

Rive is a more specific regulated expression associated with steep hillside sites and stricter production requirements. It identifies a more precise geographic origin, not an automatic quality ranking.

Is Cartizze a separate region from Conegliano Valdobbiadene?

Cartizze is a defined subzone and regulated expression within the Conegliano Valdobbiadene framework. It should not be confused with the wider Prosecco DOC.
