What is Aglianico?

A beginner-friendly guide to Aglianico: the southern Italian red grape, its tannin and acidity, Taurasi and Aglianico del Vulture, Australian examples and food pairing.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Aglianico is a dark-skinned red wine grape, best known from southern Italy. It commonly makes deeply coloured, full-bodied and structured wines with firm tannin, noticeable acidity and flavours that can range from dark fruit to herbs, earth and spice.

It is not one single flavour profile. Aglianico from a warm site, a cooler hillside, a young vintage or a mature bottle can taste quite different. The name tells you the grape involved—or, in some cases, points towards a regional wine identity—but it does not guarantee exactly what will be in the glass.

Highlights

  • Aglianico is a grape variety used principally for red wine.
  • It is strongly associated with Campania and Basilicata in southern Italy.
  • Its wines often have deep colour, firm tannin and lively acidity.
  • Dark fruit may sit alongside savoury notes such as herbs, earth, mushroom or olive.
  • Some examples can age well, although not every bottle is intended for long cellaring.
  • Taurasi and Aglianico del Vulture are regional wine names, not synonyms for the grape.
  • Aglianico is also grown and made in Australia, including in McLaren Vale.

What does Aglianico taste like?

Aglianico is often a serious, structured red. In a young wine, the tannins can feel firm and drying, while acidity gives the wine freshness and shape. This combination can make the wine feel concentrated rather than soft and immediately easy-drinking.

Common fruit descriptions include black cherry, red berry and other dark fruits. Depending on the wine’s origin and age, you may also find black olive, green herbs, spice, earth, mushroom or leather. With bottle age, savoury and tobacco-like notes may become more noticeable.

These are possibilities, not a checklist. Vineyard site, climate, harvest conditions, ripeness, extraction, oak and bottle age all influence the result. Aglianico is often full-bodied or medium- to full-bodied, but body and alcohol vary between wines.

Why is Aglianico so tannic and acidic?

A grape’s natural structure helps shape the wine made from it. Aglianico is associated with substantial tannin and pronounced acidity, two features that can make a wine feel firm and energetic.

Tannin is the drying sensation you may notice on your gums after a sip. Acidity creates freshness and makes your mouth water. In Aglianico, these elements can provide the framework for a dense, age-worthy wine—but they can also make a young example feel austere.

That firmness is not automatically a fault. With time, balanced tannins can soften and the wine’s fruit and savoury flavours can become more integrated. Still, “can age well” is more useful than “must be aged”: the right choice depends on the individual wine, vintage and winemaking.

Where does Aglianico come from?

Aglianico is especially associated with two parts of southern Italy:

  • Campania, including the area around Taurasi
  • Basilicata, particularly the Vulture area

These places are not interchangeable. They represent different regional settings and wine identities, even though Aglianico is central to both.

Aglianico, Taurasi and Aglianico del Vulture

This is an important label distinction:

  • Aglianico is the grape variety.
  • Taurasi is a regulated Campanian DOCG wine appellation based on Aglianico.
  • Aglianico del Vulture is a regional appellation associated with the Vulture area of Basilicata.
  • Aglianico del Vulture Superiore is a distinct recognised higher-level appellation expression.

So a bottle labelled “Taurasi” is telling you about a regional wine identity and its rules, not simply giving the grape another name. Similarly, Aglianico del Vulture does not mean every Aglianico wine; it identifies wine connected with a defined area and appellation.

Is Aglianico grown in Australia?

Yes. Aglianico is grown and made in Australia, with Wine Australia documenting Australian examples including wine from McLaren Vale.

Australian Aglianico should not be expected to copy a wine from Campania or Basilicata exactly. Local climate, vineyard site and winemaking can produce a different balance of fruit, tannin, freshness and savoury character. The Australian evidence supports local cultivation and production, but not claims that the grape is widely available nationwide or grown at a particular national scale.

For Australian drinkers, Aglianico can therefore be an interesting way to explore a southern Italian variety through a local interpretation.

What food goes with Aglianico?

Aglianico’s tannin and savoury structure make rich, protein-based foods sensible starting points. Try it with:

  • grilled or braised red meat;
  • lamb;
  • tomato-based ragù;
  • slow-cooked savoury dishes;
  • mushrooms;
  • mature hard cheeses.

The idea is balance, not a strict rule. Protein and savoury richness can make a tannic wine feel smoother, while a delicate dish may be overwhelmed by a particularly concentrated example.

Serve the wine according to its style and the producer’s guidance. A young, structured Aglianico may benefit from some air, while an older bottle may need gentler handling. If the wine feels warm, serving it slightly below typical room temperature can help it taste fresher; avoid chilling it so far that its aromas and texture disappear.

Is Aglianico a good wine for beginners?

It can be—especially if you enjoy reds with freshness and structure rather than soft, sweet-seeming fruit. If you are new to tannin, a young Aglianico may feel quite firm at first. Pairing it with food can make the texture easier to appreciate.

The most useful thing to remember is that Aglianico is a grape with a typical personality, not a rigid recipe. Look for dark fruit, acidity and tannin as broad signposts, then allow the region, vintage and winemaking to tell the rest of the story.

Frequently asked questions

How do you pronounce Aglianico?

Pronunciation can vary by language and speaker. It is not necessary to pronounce the name perfectly to order or discuss the wine; saying “ag-lee-AH-nee-co” will generally be understood.

Is Aglianico always a heavy red wine?

No. Many examples are structured and medium- to full-bodied, but body, alcohol and texture vary with climate, site, ripeness and winemaking.

Is Aglianico the same as Taurasi?

No. Aglianico is the grape. Taurasi is a regulated regional wine appellation from Campania associated with that grape.

Is Aglianico del Vulture the same as Aglianico?

No. Aglianico del Vulture is a regional appellation from Basilicata. It is associated with Aglianico but is not a synonym for every wine made from the grape.
