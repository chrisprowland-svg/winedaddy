Best temperature for storing wine

Quick answer: what temperature should wine be stored at?

For long-term storage of unopened wine, aim for approximately 10–15°C in a dark, stable environment. A consistent location around 15–20°C can also be practical for ordinary bottles, particularly when they are not intended for many years of ageing.

The most important rule is not to find one magic number. It is to avoid sustained heat, direct sunlight and repeated temperature swings. Warmer conditions generally make the chemical reactions involved in wine development happen faster. Large changes between hot days and cooler nights can also place physical stress on the bottle and closure.

A wine fridge or cellar can help when your home has no reliably cool location. But many bottles can be stored perfectly sensibly in a cool interior cupboard. You do not need specialist equipment simply because you have bought a bottle of wine.

Why stability matters

Wine continues to change in the bottle. Temperature affects the speed and character of that development. Research from the Australian Wine Research Institute found measurable differences between wine stored at approximately 15°C and wine stored at approximately 21.5°C, including greater loss of sulphur dioxide—a preservative used in wine—in the warmer samples.

That does not mean a bottle becomes undrinkable as soon as it reaches 20°C. It means that warmer storage can move wine towards maturity more quickly, potentially reducing its useful ageing life.

Repeated heating and cooling is a separate concern. Significant temperature cycling can contribute to expansion, leakage or movement of a cork closure. However, a temperature change does not automatically mean that air has entered the bottle or that the wine has been ruined. The effect depends on the wine, closure, length of exposure and the size of the temperature change.

There is no universal household rule saying that storage must vary by no more than a particular number of degrees. The practical advice is simpler: choose the steadiest suitable location you have.

Heat is the bigger household risk

The Australian Wine Research Institute identifies prolonged temperatures above approximately 25°C as a quality risk. Short exposure above 40°C can cause visual and sensory changes within days. Severe heat can strip away fresh fruit character and contribute to oxidised or cooked flavours.

That makes some familiar Australian storage locations poor choices for long-term ageing:

  • garages that heat up during summer;
  • sheds exposed to sunlight;
  • cupboards beside ovens, cooktops or hot-water systems;
  • roof-adjacent storage areas;
  • rooms or cupboards against hot, uninsulated external walls;
  • any location in direct sun.

A kitchen cupboard is not automatically unsuitable, but kitchens often experience more heat and fluctuation than other parts of a home. An internal cupboard that stays cool and dark may be a better choice than a garage, even if neither is a formal cellar.

If you are unsure, place a thermometer or simple temperature logger in the proposed storage location. Measuring the actual space is more useful than relying on the label “room temperature”.

Is a wine fridge necessary?

A purpose-built wine fridge can provide a controlled, stable environment when your home is regularly too warm or variable. It may be particularly useful for bottles intended to age for years.

It is not mandatory for ordinary bottles that will be consumed relatively soon. A cool, dark and stable cupboard may be enough. The goal is to protect wine from damaging conditions, not to turn every home into a professional cellar.

For valuable or long-lived bottles, controlled specialist storage may be more appropriate. Producer-specific advice should also take precedence over general guidance where it is available.

Can wine be stored in a normal refrigerator?

A standard household refrigerator is useful for chilling wine before serving or holding an unopened bottle temporarily. It is generally not the ideal permanent storage environment.

A domestic fridge is colder and drier than the conditions usually recommended for long-term storage, and it may not provide the space or stable cellar-like environment needed for an ageing collection. Wine education guidance also cautions that prolonged refrigerator storage may affect corks and seals, and can be particularly unhelpful for sparkling wine.

This is not a warning that a few days in the fridge will spoil a bottle. It is a distinction between short-term chilling and long-term storage. If your home is extremely hot, temporary refrigeration may be preferable to leaving wine in a hot garage or shed.

Does storage temperature differ by wine type?

Generally, no rigid storage-temperature table is needed. Still red, white, rosé, sparkling and fortified wines can usually share a cool, dark and stable storage environment.

Their differences mainly concern serving temperature. A white or sparkling wine may be served cooler than a red wine, but that does not mean it needs to be stored permanently in a household refrigerator. Similarly, fortified wine is not immune to the effects of heat simply because it contains more alcohol.

A practical storage checklist

Before putting a bottle away, ask:

  1. Is the location cool enough? Aim for around 10–15°C for long-term storage where practical.
  2. Does it stay reasonably stable? Avoid places that heat and cool sharply each day.
  3. Is it dark? Keep wine away from direct sunlight.
  4. Is it away from heat sources? Avoid ovens, appliances, heaters, garages and sheds that become hot.
  5. Will the bottle be stored for years? If so, consider a monitored, controlled environment.
  6. Are you confusing storage with serving? Store wine steadily, then bring it to a suitable drinking temperature later.

Temperature is one part of wine storage alongside light, oxygen, closure and time. Good storage cannot guarantee that every wine will improve with age, but it can reduce avoidable stress and help preserve the wine’s intended character. FAQs

Is 20°C too warm for storing wine?

Not necessarily for short-term storage, especially if the temperature is stable and the wine will be consumed relatively soon. For long-term ageing, a cooler environment around 10–15°C is preferable where practical.

Are temperature fluctuations worse than a consistently warm room?

Both sustained warmth and significant fluctuations can be harmful. Choose a location that avoids heat and remains as stable as possible. There is no evidence-based universal household fluctuation limit that applies to every wine.

Should red wine be stored warmer than white wine?

No separate permanent storage temperature is generally required. Store both in a cool, dark and stable environment. Their serving temperatures may differ.

What should I do if my home has no cellar?

Choose the coolest stable interior cupboard available, away from light and heat sources. Monitor it during hot weather. If conditions are regularly too warm or variable, controlled wine storage may be useful.

Can I store unopened wine in the kitchen fridge?

Yes, temporarily. A normal fridge is suitable for chilling or short-term holding, but it is not ideal as a permanent cellar.
