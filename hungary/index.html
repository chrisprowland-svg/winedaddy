Hungary: A Beginner’s Guide to Its Wine Regions, Grapes and Styles

Understand Hungary as a diverse Central European wine country, from Tokaj and Tokaji Aszú to Furmint, Kékfrankos, Eger, Villány, Szekszárd, Somló and the Balaton wine districts.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Hungary is a diverse Central European wine country, not a single wine style.
  • It has 22 legally recognised wine districts, grouped into broader wine areas.
  • Tokaj is Hungary’s best-known wine region, especially for Tokaji Aszú sweet wine.
  • Tokaj also produces dry wines, particularly from Furmint and Hárslevelű.
  • Kékfrankos, also called Blaufränkisch, is an important Hungarian red grape.
  • Eger, Villány, Szekszárd, Somló and the Balaton-area districts broaden the picture.

Quick answer: what should an Australian wine learner know about Hungary?

Hungary is a landlocked Central European wine country with 22 wine districts and a wide range of dry white, sweet, red, rosé and sparkling wines.

Its most famous association is Tokaj, in the north-east. Tokaj is particularly known for Tokaji Aszú, a sweet wine made using botrytised, shrivelled grapes. But Tokaj also produces dry wines, and Hungarian wine as a whole is certainly not synonymous with sweet wine.

Furmint and Hárslevelű are the key grapes to recognise in Tokaj. Elsewhere, Kékfrankos—known internationally as Blaufränkisch—and Kadarka help explain Hungary’s red-wine tradition. Juhfark is closely associated with Somló, while Eger, Villány, Szekszárd and the varied districts around Lake Balaton each add their own regional identity.

The simplest way to approach Hungarian wine is to think in layers: Hungary is the country; a wine district or protected name identifies a more specific place; a grape or traditional term helps describe what is in the bottle. Tokaj is one important part of that map, not the whole country.

Where is Hungary?

Hungary sits in Central Europe. It is bordered by Austria, Croatia, Romania, Serbia, Slovakia and Slovenia.

For an Australian learner, Budapest is the most useful reference point. Eger is approximately 1½ hours by car east or north-east of Budapest, while Tokaj is roughly 2¼ to 2¾ hours north-east by road. These are broad orientation estimates, not precise travel promises.

Hungary has no Australian-style state or territory hierarchy relevant to its wine regions. At country level, the useful structure is:

Hungary → broader wine areas → wine districts and protected regional names

Hungarian wine sources commonly describe six broader wine areas and 22 wine districts. Those broad areas are useful for learning, but they should not automatically be treated as equivalent to legally protected appellations. Individual names such as Tokaj, Eger, Villány, Szekszárd and Somló have their own protected-origin status.

A bottle may therefore show a protected regional name, a grape variety, a traditional style term, or a combination of these. A town name, wine district and protected designation are related, but they are not automatically interchangeable.

Tokaj is important—but it is not Hungary

Tokaj—approximately “TO-kay”—is a wine region in north-eastern Hungary, around the Bodrog and Tisza rivers and the southern and south-eastern edge of the Zemplén Mountains.

The name Tokaj/Tokaji is protected. “Tokaj” generally refers to the place and region, while “Tokaji” is the wine-related form commonly encountered on labels, as in Tokaji Aszú.

Tokaj’s best-known wine is Tokaji Aszú. It is made with aszú berries: grapes affected by Botrytis cinerea, often called noble rot when the conditions are favourable. The fungus can shrivel the grapes and concentrate their sugars, acids and flavours. Botrytis is not automatically beneficial; its effect depends on the conditions and how the grapes and wine are handled.

Tokaj also produces dry wines. Furmint and Hárslevelű are the two most important varieties to recognise, and both can appear in dry and sweet expressions. This is one of the most useful corrections for anyone whose idea of Tokaj begins and ends with dessert wine.

The region’s rivers, wetlands, mountains and volcanic geological material help explain its viticultural setting. They do not, however, guarantee one fixed flavour profile. Wine style also depends on grape variety, harvest conditions, winemaking and the individual site.

Hungary beyond Tokaj

Eger

Eger is another north-eastern Hungarian wine area, but it is separate from Tokaj. It is associated with both red and white wines, as well as Bikavér, traditionally translated as “bull’s blood”.

Bikavér is a blend designation rather than one universal recipe. Eger and Szekszárd each have their own regional identity and production rules, so the term needs its regional context.

Villány

Villány lies in southern Hungary near the Croatian border. It is particularly useful for understanding Hungary’s fuller red-wine tradition and the role of international red varieties alongside local grapes.

Villány is a separate protected wine name. It should not be treated as a southern extension of Tokaj or as representative of every Hungarian red wine.

Szekszárd

Szekszárd, in south-central Hungary, is an important reference point for red wines, Kékfrankos, Kadarka and Bikavér-related styles.

As with Eger, Szekszárd’s Bikavér should be understood in its own regional context. “Bikavér” does not mean that Eger and Szekszárd produce identical wines.

Somló

Somló is a small volcanic hill in western Hungary. It has a distinctive white-wine identity, with Juhfark being the variety most useful for a learner to remember.

Somló is a separate protected name. It is sometimes discussed alongside the broader Balaton area, but it is not part of Tokaj.

Balaton-area districts

“Balaton” is a broad geographical and wine-region reference around Lake Balaton, rather than one uniform appellation. It includes districts such as Badacsony, Balatonfüred-Csopak, Balatonboglár, Balaton-felvidék, Nagy-Somló and Zala.

The area is varied, and individual districts have their own protected names and wine identities. It is more helpful to think of Balaton as a group of neighbouring wine districts than as one single style.

Grapes to recognise

Furmint

Furmint is the essential Tokaj grape to know. It is used for dry wines as well as sweet Tokaji wines. Its expression can vary with site, harvest conditions and winemaking, so it is better to think of it as a versatile regional grape than as a guaranteed flavour formula.

Hárslevelű

Hárslevelű is another major Tokaj variety. It can be used alongside Furmint or bottled on its own. It is often discussed as aromatic and textured, but those are tendencies rather than rules.

Juhfark

Juhfark is a regional speciality associated particularly with Somló. It is useful as a clue that Hungarian wine extends well beyond Tokaj’s varieties.

Kékfrankos or Blaufränkisch

Kékfrankos is the Hungarian name for Blaufränkisch. Seeing either name can help an Australian drinker recognise the same grape in different national contexts.

It is one of Hungary’s important red-wine reference varieties, particularly in areas such as Eger and Szekszárd. It is not accurate to treat it as the basis of every Hungarian red wine.

Kadarka

Kadarka is a historic Hungarian red grape, especially relevant to discussions of Szekszárd and traditional red-wine styles. It is another reminder that Hungary has its own grape heritage, even though international varieties are also widely used.

International varieties

Hungarian vineyards also include varieties familiar to Australian drinkers, including Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Pinot Noir, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc. Their presence makes Hungarian wine easier to approach: you may recognise the grape, even when the regional name is unfamiliar.

What styles does Hungary produce?

Hungary’s wine styles include:

  • dry white wines;
  • sweet wines, including Tokaji Aszú;
  • red wines and blends;
  • rosé;
  • sparkling wine; and
  • late-harvest and other traditional regional styles.

The country’s protected-origin system helps connect particular names with places and production conditions. In European terminology, PDO means Protected Designation of Origin, while PGI means Protected Geographical Indication. These categories have specific legal meanings in the European Union.

An Australian GI comparison can be useful as a starting point because both systems connect geographical names with place. However, Hungarian and European PDO or PGI rules are not simply Australian GI rules under different names. Their legal structures, traditional terms and requirements differ.

How to read a Hungarian wine label

When you encounter a Hungarian wine, look for several clues rather than relying on one word.

Country: Hungary tells you the national origin, but not the specific wine area.

Protected regional name: Tokaj/Tokaji, Eger/Egri, Villány/Villányi, Szekszárd/Szekszárdi and Somló/Somlói point towards particular protected places.

Grape variety: Furmint, Hárslevelű, Juhfark, Kékfrankos or Kadarka may tell you more about the wine’s grape identity.

Traditional style term: Words such as Aszú or Bikavér carry regional and production meaning. They are not interchangeable with grape names.

Dry or sweet style: Tokaj is a particularly important example because the region produces both dry and sweet wines.

The names may look unfamiliar, but that does not mean the wine is inaccessible. Start with the place, then identify the grape or style term, and finally consider whether the wine is dry, sweet, red, white or sparkling.

FAQs

Is Hungarian wine always sweet?

No. Hungary produces dry white, red, rosé and sparkling wines as well as sweet wines. Tokaj itself produces both dry and sweet wines.

What is Tokaji Aszú?

Tokaji Aszú is a protected Tokaj sweet-wine style made using aszú berries affected by favourable botrytis, often called noble rot.

Is Tokaj the same as Hungary?

No. Tokaj is one Hungarian wine region. “Tokaji” is a wine-related form of the protected name. Hungary contains many other wine districts and styles.

What is the main Hungarian grape?

There is no single grape that explains all Hungarian wine. Furmint and Hárslevelű are central to Tokaj, while Kékfrankos/Blaufränkisch and Kadarka are important red-wine references. Other local and international varieties broaden the picture.

Are Kékfrankos and Blaufränkisch the same grape?

Yes. Kékfrankos is the Hungarian name commonly used for Blaufränkisch.

What does Bikavér mean?

Bikavér means “bull’s blood” and refers to a traditional blend designation associated particularly with Eger and Szekszárd. The regional context matters because the two areas do not produce one identical recipe.

How should an Australian approach Hungarian wine?

Do not assume that an unfamiliar place name means an unfamiliar style. Look for the region, grape, traditional term and sweetness or colour indication. Tokaj is a useful starting point, but exploring dry Furmint, Kékfrankos, Kadarka, Juhfark and wines from Eger, Villány, Szekszárd or the Balaton districts will give you a broader picture.
