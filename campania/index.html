Campania: A Beginner’s Guide to the Wines of Southern Italy

Understand Campania’s geography, native grapes, volcanic districts and Italian appellations, including Fiano di Avellino, Greco di Tufo and Taurasi.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Campania is a region of southern Italy, centred on Naples and the Tyrrhenian coast.
  • It stretches from coastal and volcanic districts into elevated inland areas including Irpinia and Sannio.
  • Its wine identity is strongly linked to native grapes, especially Fiano, Greco and Aglianico.
  • Falanghina and Piedirosso are also important Campanian varieties.
  • Campania is broader than its individual appellations, such as Fiano di Avellino, Greco di Tufo and Taurasi.
  • Volcanic geology matters in parts of the region, but not every Campanian vineyard is volcanic.
  • Italian labels often foreground an appellation name, rather than simply listing a grape and broad region.

Quick answer: what should you understand about Campania?

Campania is an official region of southern Italy, with Naples as its capital. For wine, it is best understood as a varied landscape rather than one uniform style. Coastal vineyards around Naples and the Tyrrhenian Sea sit alongside inland and elevated areas such as Irpinia and Sannio, where conditions can be cooler and more seasonally variable.

The region is particularly important for native Italian grapes. Fiano and Greco are central white-wine identities, while Aglianico is the key red-grape association. Falanghina and Piedirosso add further breadth.

The names on a Campanian wine label can appear confusing at first. Campania is the broad region and also an Italian IGP/IGT wine indication. Fiano di Avellino, Greco di Tufo and Taurasi, by contrast, are specific protected appellations within Campania. They are not interchangeable names for the whole region.

For an Australian learner, the simplest mental model is: Campania is the large geographic area; appellations identify more defined places and rules within it.

Where is Campania?

Campania is in south-western Italy, around Naples and the Gulf of Naples. It faces the Tyrrhenian Sea and includes the administrative areas of Naples, Avellino, Benevento, Caserta and Salerno.

Naples is the most useful broad orientation point. Avellino is a useful inland reference for Irpinia, while Benevento helps orient readers to Sannio. Familiar localities and landmarks include Mount Vesuvius, Campi Flegrei, Tufo and Taurasi. A town appearing in a wine name does not, by itself, define the whole Campania region or explain the full boundary of an appellation.

From Naples, Avellino is roughly a 45–60 minute drive and Benevento roughly 1 hour 15 minutes to 1 hour 30 minutes by car in ordinary conditions. Treat these as orientation only, not precise travel promises.

Campania borders Lazio and Molise to the north, and Puglia and Basilicata towards the east and south-east. Those are separate Italian regions, not parts of Campania.

A broad map of Italy can help place Campania between the Tyrrhenian coast and the inland Apennine areas. The registered visual asset VIS-024 is suitable for this orientation.

Why Campania does not have one single style

Campania’s geography changes considerably across a relatively compact part of Italy. Coastal areas have broadly Mediterranean conditions, while inland vineyards can sit at higher elevations with cooler temperatures and greater seasonal variation.

That distinction is especially useful when thinking about southern Italy. “Southern” does not automatically mean uniformly hot, low-elevation vineyards. Elevation can moderate conditions inland, helping explain why Campania is associated with both fresh white wines and more structured reds.

Volcanic landscapes are important in specific areas around Naples. Campi Flegrei and the districts around Vesuvius contain volcanic and ash-derived materials. Other Campanian vineyards have different geological settings, so it would be misleading to describe the entire region as volcanic.

Nor should volcanic soil be treated as a shortcut to a particular flavour. Wine style reflects the interaction of geology, climate, elevation, grape variety, farming and winemaking. This is a useful example of why the idea of terroir is broader than soil alone.

The grapes to know

Fiano

Fiano is one of Campania’s clearest white-grape identities. It is strongly associated with Fiano di Avellino DOCG, an appellation centred in the province of Avellino.

Campanian Fiano can be approached as a white wine with freshness, structure and potential texture, although the exact style varies with site, vintage and winemaking. The important beginner distinction is that Fiano is the grape, while Fiano di Avellino is a protected appellation linked to a defined place and production rules.

You can learn more about the grape in What Is Fiano?.

Greco

Greco is closely associated with Greco di Tufo DOCG. Here, the name combines a grape reference with a locality: Tufo is part of the appellation name, while Greco identifies the principal grape identity.

This is a good example of how Italian wine names can carry several pieces of information at once. Greco di Tufo is not simply another name for Campania, and “Greco” should not automatically be read as the same thing as every other wine using that grape name elsewhere in Italy.

Aglianico

Aglianico is Campania’s principal red-grape association. It is central to Taurasi DOCG and Aglianico del Taburno DOCG.

Aglianico-based Campanian reds can offer substantial structure, depth and ageing potential. Taurasi is a particularly useful name for Australian learners to recognise, but it remains one appellation within Campania rather than a synonym for the region.

As with any regional tendency, the bottle in front of you matters. Grape, site, vintage and winemaking choices can all change the result.

Falanghina and Piedirosso

Falanghina is an important Campanian white grape, particularly associated with Sannio and Falanghina del Sannio. Piedirosso is a meaningful local red variety, especially linked with coastal volcanic districts including Vesuvio and Campi Flegrei.

These varieties show why Campania is more than a three-grape story. Fiano, Greco and Aglianico are the most useful starting points, but Campania’s wine map includes a wider set of native grapes and appellations.

The appellations worth recognising

The Campania regional agriculture authority identifies four DOCG/DOP wine names as particularly significant:

  • Fiano di Avellino
  • Greco di Tufo
  • Taurasi
  • Aglianico del Taburno

The region also contains other DOC and IGP/IGT indications, including names associated with Irpinia, Sannio, Vesuvio, Campi Flegrei, Costa d’Amalfi, Cilento and Ischia.

The key lesson is hierarchy. Campania is the broad region. Irpinia and Sannio are useful subregional contexts. A name such as Taurasi or Greco di Tufo is a more specific appellation with its own geographic boundaries and production rules.

DOC and DOCG are Italian denomination terms. They should not be treated as direct equivalents of Australian zones, regions or subregions, or as a simple guarantee that one wine will suit every drinker. For more background, see Italian DOC and DOCG.

How to read a Campanian label in Australia

Australian drinkers often encounter a label structure such as a grape followed by a GI: for example, a variety and a recognisable Australian region. An Italian label may instead make the appellation the most prominent name.

So, when you see:

  • Campania: think broad regional or IGP/IGT context.
  • Fiano: think grape variety.
  • Fiano di Avellino: think protected appellation based around a defined area.
  • Greco di Tufo: think grape-and-place appellation.
  • Taurasi: think a specific Aglianico-based red appellation.

Availability and labelling of Campanian wines can vary in Australia, so an unfamiliar name is not a reason to assume a wine is obscure or difficult. Start by identifying whether the prominent term is a grape, a place or a classification.

The takeaway

Campania is a geographically varied region of southern Italy whose wines are built around native grapes and specific appellations. Begin with Fiano, Greco and Aglianico, then use Falanghina and Piedirosso to broaden the picture. Keep Naples, the Tyrrhenian coast, Irpinia and Sannio in mind, and remember that volcanic Campania describes particular districts, not the region as a whole.

The most useful habit is to separate the broad region from the more precise wine name on the label. Once you do that, Campania becomes much easier to understand.

Frequently asked questions

Is Campania a wine region or an Italian administrative region?

It is both. Campania is an official administrative region of southern Italy and also appears as an Italian IGP/IGT wine indication. Individual DOC and DOCG appellations sit within the broader region.

What grapes is Campania known for?

The most useful starting points are Fiano, Greco and Aglianico. Falanghina and Piedirosso are also significant Campanian varieties.

Is Taurasi the same as Campania?

No. Taurasi is a specific Campanian DOCG appellation, principally based on Aglianico. Campania is the broader region.

Are all Campanian wines made from grapes grown in volcanic soil?

No. Volcanic geology is important around Vesuvius and Campi Flegrei, but Campania also contains inland, elevated and non-volcanic vineyard areas.

Is Campania close to Naples?

Naples is Campania’s capital and the region’s most useful broad orientation point. The region extends well beyond the city into inland areas such as Irpinia and Sannio.

What should an Australian learner look for first?

Identify whether the prominent name is a grape, a place or an appellation. Fiano, Greco and Aglianico are useful grape starting points; Fiano di Avellino, Greco di Tufo and Taurasi are more specific appellation names.
