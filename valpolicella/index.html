Valpolicella: A Beginner’s Guide to the Wines of Veneto

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Valpolicella is a wine-growing area in Veneto, northern Italy, immediately north and north-west of Verona. It is also the name used for a family of related protected wine designations, from fresh, dry red wines to the rich dry wines of Amarone and the sweet wines of Recioto.

The most useful thing to remember is that Valpolicella is not one single wine style. The name on the label matters.

Highlights

  • Valpolicella is in Veneto, in northern Italy.
  • Verona is the nearest useful regional centre.
  • Corvina is the principal grape in Valpolicella’s traditional red blends.
  • Basic Valpolicella is generally fresh, dry and lighter to medium-bodied.
  • Ripasso is made by refermenting Valpolicella on the pomace left from Amarone and/or Recioto.
  • Amarone is made from dried grapes and fermented to a dry style; it is not simply a fortified wine.
  • Recioto is also made from dried grapes, but retains residual sugar and is sweet.
  • “Classico” describes a historic geographic area. It is not automatically a quality ranking.

Where is Valpolicella?

Valpolicella sits within the Italian region of Veneto and the Province of Verona. From central Verona, the southern parts of the wine area are roughly a 20–30-minute drive, depending on the route and traffic. Treat that as a travel orientation rather than a precise promise: the area extends north through several valleys, so distances vary.

The regulated area includes three commonly discussed parts:

  • Valpolicella Classica, the historic area made up of Sant’Ambrogio di Valpolicella, San Pietro in Cariano, Fumane, Marano di Valpolicella and Negrar di Valpolicella.
  • Valpantena, centred on the Valpantena valley.
  • The wider Valpolicella DOC area, which includes parts of Verona and the Illasi, Tramigna and Mezzane valleys.

Valpolicella’s landscape rises from the foothills north of Verona towards the Lessini Mountains. The western part is also influenced by nearby Lake Garda. Valley direction, altitude, soils and exposure create variation between sites, so a regional label should be read as useful context—not a guarantee that every wine will taste the same.

Valpolicella is separate from nearby wine areas such as Soave and Bardolino. They are also in Veneto, but they are different geographic designations.

What grapes are used?

Valpolicella wines are generally Corvina-led blends, rather than wines made from Corvina alone. Corvina provides the backbone of the blend, while Corvinone and Rondinella are important supporting varieties. Molinara and other permitted red varieties may also be used, depending on the designation and production rules.

Corvina is associated with the region’s familiar red-fruit character, particularly cherry-like flavours, along with freshness and structure. However, the final wine also depends on the other varieties, vineyard site, vintage and winemaking choices.

You can learn more about the grape in our guide to Corvina.

The main Valpolicella styles

Valpolicella DOC

When a bottle simply says Valpolicella DOC, it usually refers to the region’s fresher, dry red style. These wines are commonly light- to medium-bodied, lively in acidity and centred on red-fruit flavours.

They are generally made by conventional red winemaking from fresh grapes, rather than by drying all the grapes first. That distinction matters: not every Valpolicella wine is made using appassimento.

Valpolicella DOC can be a useful choice when you want a red wine with freshness rather than heaviness. It may work well with antipasti, lighter pasta dishes, poultry and other relatively delicate foods.

Valpolicella Classico

Classico identifies wine from the historic five-municipality area. It is a geographic term, not a synonym for Amarone and not an automatic guarantee of superior quality.

A Classico wine may show the character of its particular site and vintage, but the word alone does not tell you that it will be fuller, more elegant or more ageworthy than every wine from the wider area.

Valpolicella Superiore

Valpolicella Superiore is a regulated category with additional production and ageing requirements, including at least one year of ageing according to the Consorzio’s summary.

Compared with basic Valpolicella, Superiore is generally expected to feel fuller or more structured. That is a broad orientation, not a promise about every bottle. “Superiore” is a formal designation, not simply a producer’s way of saying “better”.

Valpolicella Ripasso DOC

Ripasso means “re-passed”. Valpolicella Ripasso DOC begins with Valpolicella and then undergoes a further fermentation on the pomace—the skins, seeds and other grape material left after making Amarone and/or Recioto.

This process can add body, alcohol, phenolic material and texture. Ripasso is therefore generally richer and more substantial than basic Valpolicella.

It is not Amarone, though. Ripasso uses material from Amarone and/or Recioto production; it is not simply made by drying all its own grapes and fermenting them as Amarone.

Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG

Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG is made from grapes dried before fermentation. This grape-drying process is called appassimento. As water evaporates, the remaining grape material becomes more concentrated before fermentation begins.

The resulting wine is fermented to a dry or essentially dry style and is commonly full-bodied, high in alcohol and rich in ripe or dried-fruit character. Its texture and tannin can vary with the fruit, site, drying process and maturation.

Amarone is sometimes misunderstood as a fortified wine. It is not simply fortified Valpolicella. Appassimento means drying grapes before fermentation; fortification means adding distilled alcohol. Those are different production methods. Read more about appassimento and fortified wine.

Recioto della Valpolicella DOCG

Recioto della Valpolicella DOCG is also made from dried grapes, but fermentation is stopped or managed so that residual sugar remains. The result is a sweet wine.

This gives us one of the clearest comparisons in the Valpolicella family:

  • Amarone: dried grapes, fermented to a dry style.
  • Recioto: dried grapes, with sweetness retained.

A wine can smell intensely of ripe or dried fruit and still be dry. That is why fruitiness and sweetness should not be treated as the same thing. Dry describes the amount of residual sugar; fruit-driven describes flavour and aroma.

How to read a Valpolicella label

For an Australian bottle-shop shopper, these terms provide a useful first guide:

  • Valpolicella DOC: generally the fresh, dry red category.
  • Classico: from the historic five-municipality area.
  • Superiore: a regulated category with additional requirements and ageing.
  • Ripasso DOC: Valpolicella refermented on Amarone and/or Recioto pomace.
  • Amarone DOCG: dry red wine made from dried grapes.
  • Recioto DOCG: sweet wine made from dried grapes.

The designation tells you more than the word “Valpolicella” by itself. When comparing bottles, first identify the category, then consider the producer’s description, vintage and alcohol level.

Italian terms such as DOC and DOCG are part of Italy’s system of protected wine designations. Our guide to Italian DOC and DOCG explains the terminology in more detail.

What might Valpolicella taste like?

There is no single Valpolicella flavour profile, but these broad tendencies can help:

  • Valpolicella DOC: fresh, dry, red-fruited and lighter to medium-bodied.
  • Ripasso: fuller, rounder and more textured.
  • Amarone: concentrated, dry, warming and often rich with ripe or dried-fruit flavours.
  • Recioto: sweet, concentrated and fruit-rich.

Acidity, tannin, body, alcohol and sweetness can all vary. A cooler site, a warmer vintage, grape drying, maturation and producer choices may each influence the result.

For serving, broad starting points are around 14–16°C for Valpolicella and Recioto, 15–18°C for Ripasso, and 16–20°C for Amarone. In an Australian kitchen, that usually means avoiding the temptation to serve a red wine warm. Follow the producer’s advice where available.

The takeaway

Valpolicella is best understood as both a place and a family of wines. Corvina-led blends provide the regional foundation, while production choices create very different expressions.

If you want freshness, look towards Valpolicella DOC. If you want more body, Ripasso may be the relevant label term. If you want a powerful dry wine made from dried grapes, Amarone is the category to understand. If you want sweetness, look to Recioto.

That simple distinction makes Italian labels much easier to read—and helps you choose a style that matches what you actually enjoy.

Frequently asked questions

Is Valpolicella always a light red wine?

No. Basic Valpolicella is generally fresh and lighter to medium-bodied, but Ripasso, Amarone and Recioto can be considerably richer, fuller or sweeter.

Is Amarone a fortified wine?

No. Amarone is made from grapes dried before fermentation and fermented to a dry style. Fortified wine involves adding distilled alcohol.

Is Ripasso the same as Amarone?

No. Ripasso is Valpolicella that undergoes further fermentation on Amarone and/or Recioto pomace. Amarone is made from dried grapes.

Is Recioto sweet?

Yes. Recioto retains residual sugar and is the sweet wine in this principal group of Valpolicella designations.

Are all Valpolicella wines made from dried grapes?

No. Appassimento is central to Amarone and Recioto, and the materials used for Ripasso come from those styles. Ordinary Valpolicella is generally made from fresh grapes.
