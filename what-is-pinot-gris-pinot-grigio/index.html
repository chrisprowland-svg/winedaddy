What Is Pinot Gris / Pinot Grigio?

Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio are names for the same grape. Learn why both appear on labels, how the styles can differ and what Australian drinkers should look for.

Highlights

  • Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio are two names for the same white-wine grape variety.
  • Pinot Gris is the French name; Pinot Grigio is the Italian name.
  • The grape has greyish-pink or dusky skins, but it is normally made into white wine.
  • Pinot Grigio is often associated with light, crisp and fresh wines.
  • Pinot Gris is often associated with riper, fuller and more textured wines.
  • Those style associations are tendencies, not rules. Australian producers may use either name for different styles.
  • Climate, harvest timing and winemaking choices can matter more than the name on the front label.

Quick Answer

Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio are names for the same grape variety. “Pinot Gris” is French, while “Pinot Grigio” is Italian. Both names refer to the grape’s greyish or dusky skin colour.

The variety is usually made as a dry white wine. Depending on where it is grown and how it is made, it can be light, crisp and citrusy, or fuller, riper and more textured. Common flavours include apple, pear, citrus and stone fruit, with some wines showing mineral, spice, creamy or lightly savoury notes.

The names can offer a useful clue to style. Pinot Grigio is often linked with earlier-picked, fresh and energetic wines, while Pinot Gris is often linked with riper and more substantial expressions. But this is not a universal rule. In Australia, a producer may use either name for a crisp, rich or textural wine.

If you are choosing a bottle, treat “Gris” or “Grigio” as a starting point. Then read the rest of the label or producer description for clues such as crisp, dry, fresh, textured, lees-aged, barrel-fermented or off-dry.

One Grape, Two Names

The simplest way to understand Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio is to think of them as two languages describing the same grape.

  • Pinot Gris is the French name.
  • Pinot Grigio is the Italian name.
  • Gris and grigio both mean “grey”.

The grape’s ripe skins can have a greyish-pink, dusky or coppery appearance. Despite this colour, Pinot Gris/Grigio is normally made into white wine. Grape skin colour does not automatically determine whether a finished wine is red or white; winemaking choices, especially the amount of skin contact, also matter.

Pinot Gris belongs to the Pinot family and is related to Pinot Noir. The word “Pinot” is associated with the pine-cone-like shape of Pinot-family grape bunches.

Pinot Gris = Pinot Grigio
French name | Italian name | same grape variety

Do Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio Taste Different?

They can, but not because they are different grapes.

Over time, the names have developed broad stylistic associations. A wine labelled Pinot Grigio is often expected to be:

  • light to medium-bodied;
  • fresh and crisp;
  • high in perceived acidity;
  • citrusy, mineral or green-apple driven;
  • clean and refreshing.

A wine labelled Pinot Gris is often expected to be:

  • riper and fuller;
  • broader or rounder in texture;
  • flavoured with pear, ripe apple or stone fruit;
  • silky, creamy or lightly spicy;
  • sometimes influenced by lees contact, skin contact or oak.

These are useful traditions, but they are not fixed definitions. A Pinot Gris can be bright and delicate, while a Pinot Grigio can be rich or textural. The producer, region, vintage and winemaking decisions all matter.

That is why it is better to think of Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio as a style spectrum, rather than two sealed categories.

Style spectrum:
crisp and light → rounded and textured → rich and luscious

The label name alone does not determine where a wine sits.

What Does Pinot Gris / Pinot Grigio Taste Like?

There is no single Pinot Gris or Pinot Grigio flavour profile. The wine may show:

  • lemon or other citrus;
  • green apple;
  • pear;
  • peach or other stone fruit;
  • floral notes;
  • mineral or stony impressions;
  • spice;
  • creamy or savoury texture.

Lighter examples are usually driven by freshness. They may feel lean, clean and mouth-watering, with fruit and mineral notes rather than obvious oak.

Fuller examples may feel broader and softer. Riper fruit, lees contact, skin contact or barrel fermentation can add weight and texture. Some wines may have a silky or creamy impression even when they are still dry.

Is Pinot Gris sweet?

Most table-wine examples are dry, but the wine can taste fruity or luscious. A ripe pear flavour is not the same as sweetness from residual sugar.

Residual sugar is the sugar left in a wine after fermentation. It can make a wine taste sweet, while fruit ripeness, alcohol and texture can make a dry wine seem rich or soft. If sweetness matters to you, look for terms such as dry, off-dry or sweet, and check the producer’s description where available.

Why Do Styles Vary?

The same grape can produce noticeably different wines because several decisions shape the finished style.

Climate and site

Cooler conditions can help a vineyard retain acidity and freshness. Warmer or riper conditions can contribute more body, fruit weight and richness. This is a broad tendency, not a formula: vineyard site, season and picking decisions also matter.

Harvest timing

Earlier picking generally preserves freshness and lighter fruit character. Later picking can bring more ripeness, concentration and texture. This is one reason the classic light Grigio and fuller Gris associations developed.

Skin contact

Allowing grape juice to remain in contact with the skins can influence colour, texture and phenolic feel. In Pinot Gris/Grigio, it may contribute a deeper colour or a more textured, savoury style.

Lees contact

Lees are the sediment left after fermentation, including yeast cells. Keeping wine on its lees can contribute breadth, creaminess or a more rounded texture.

Fermentation vessel and oak

Stainless steel is often used when a winemaker wants to preserve freshness and fruit character. Barrel fermentation or oak maturation may add texture, spice, toast or vanilla. Oak is optional, not a defining feature of Pinot Gris.

Style-influence flow:
climate + harvest timing + ripeness + skin contact + lees + oak → final wine style

Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio in Australia

Australian drinkers may see both names on domestic and imported labels. Pinot Grigio is common on imported Italian wines, while Australian producers may choose either Pinot Gris or Pinot Grigio for their own wines.

Australia does not have one universal “Gris style” and one universal “Grigio style”. A bottle’s name can suggest an intention, but it does not guarantee the flavour or texture inside.

Relevant Australian contexts include cool or maritime regions such as:

  • Mornington Peninsula;
  • Tasmania;
  • Adelaide Hills;
  • Yarra Valley;
  • Orange.

Wine Australia identifies Pinot Gris/Grigio as an important variety in the Mornington Peninsula, where it describes examples as medium-bodied, fruit-driven and mineral in character. These descriptions are broad regional tendencies, not rules for every bottle.

Australia also produces Pinot Gris/Grigio in larger inland regions, so the category is not limited to one type of climate or one premium style. Wine Australia’s 2024 variety snapshot recorded 70,757 tonnes crushed in 2024, while its planting figure of 3,731 hectares used 2015 data. These figures are dated and are not necessary for choosing a bottle, but they illustrate that the variety is established across Australian wine production.

How to Choose a Bottle

If you prefer a fresh, easy-drinking white, look for descriptions such as:

  • crisp;
  • fresh;
  • bright;
  • mineral;
  • citrusy;
  • light-bodied.

If you prefer more weight or texture, look for:

  • ripe pear or stone fruit;
  • rounded;
  • creamy;
  • textured;
  • lees-aged;
  • barrel-fermented;
  • skin contact.

Do not assume that “Pinot Gris” automatically means rich or that “Pinot Grigio” automatically means simple and crisp. The producer description, region, vintage and technical notes can give you a better idea of the wine’s style.

Serving and Food

Pinot Gris/Grigio is generally served chilled. Avoid serving it so cold that its aromas and texture disappear.

Crisper styles can work well with seafood, shellfish, salads and lighter dishes. Fuller examples may suit richer seafood, poultry or lightly spiced food. These are starting points rather than strict pairing rules; match the food to the wine’s body, acidity and texture.

The Bottom Line

Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio are two names for the same grape. The names come from French and Italian usage and can suggest different stylistic traditions, but neither name guarantees a particular flavour.

Expect anything from a light, crisp and citrus-driven white to a fuller wine with ripe fruit and a more rounded texture. For the most reliable guide, read beyond the grape name and look for the producer’s description of the wine’s freshness, sweetness and texture.
