Herbal aromas in wine: what they mean and how to recognise them

Learn what herbal aromas in wine mean, from mint and eucalyptus to leafy and capsicum notes, and how to tell character from excessive green aromas.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Herbal is a broad wine aroma descriptor, not one single smell.
  • It can suggest mint, eucalyptus, fennel, dill, dried herbs, grass, leafy plants or green capsicum.
  • A herbal note describes what you perceive; it does not mean herbs were added to the wine.
  • Herbal character can come from several interacting factors, including grape variety, ripeness, vineyard conditions and winemaking.
  • Herbal aromas are not automatically faults. Fresh, balanced herbal notes can be part of a wine’s character.
  • A descriptor alone does not prove a particular grape, region, climate or winemaking method.
  • The best way to learn herbal aromas is to compare wines with familiar reference smells.

Quick answer: what are herbal aromas in wine?

Herbal aromas in wine are smells, or retronasal flavour impressions, that remind you of herbs, leafy plants or related green botanical references.

You might notice fresh mint, dried herbs, fennel, dill, grass, tomato leaf, green capsicum or eucalyptus. These references overlap, but they are not identical. “Herbal” is a broad starting point; a more useful tasting note narrows it down to something specific, such as “fresh mint” or “green capsicum”.

The term describes a sensory resemblance, not the ingredients in the bottle. A wine described as herbal does not necessarily contain herbs. Nor does the descriptor, by itself, identify a grape variety, region or fault.

Herbal character can be pleasant, neutral or excessive. A fresh, integrated note may add interest and complexity. A strong, raw or dominating green character may feel unbalanced or vegetal. The difference depends on the wine’s style, intensity, structure and the taster’s perception.

What can herbal wine smell like?

It helps to group herbal aromas into families rather than memorise a long list.

Fresh herbs

These include mint, fennel, dill and fresh leafy herbs. They may smell bright, lifted or savoury. Mint can range from fresh garden mint to a cooler menthol impression.

Dried and savoury herbs

Dried-herb references can seem less bright and more savoury. A taster might be reminded of dried Mediterranean herbs or leafy seasoning. These are comparisons made by the nose, not evidence that herbs were used in winemaking.

Leafy and grassy notes

Grass, tomato leaf, asparagus and general leafy vegetation sit close to the boundary between herbal, green and vegetal. “Vegetal” usually suggests a stronger plant- or vegetable-like impression, while “herbal” can sound more neutral or positive. The terms overlap, but they are not perfect synonyms.

Capsicum and green pepper

Green capsicum or green bell pepper is a common reference for some green, herbaceous wines. It can be useful to describe the aroma more precisely than simply calling a wine “green”.

Mint and eucalyptus

Mint, menthol and eucalyptus-like aromas are especially familiar reference points for Australian wine drinkers. Wine Australia sensory work uses these as distinct descriptive attributes, and Australian research has examined compounds associated with eucalyptus and mint aromas in red wines.

That does not mean Australian wine generally tastes of eucalyptus. These are possible observations in particular wines, not a national signature or proof of a region or grape.

Aroma, flavour and taste: what are you noticing?

Aroma is the smell perceived through the nose. When you smell wine in the glass, you are assessing aroma.

As you taste, some of the wine’s volatile compounds travel from the mouth towards the nose. This is called retronasal aroma, and it contributes strongly to what we commonly call flavour.

Taste refers more narrowly to sensations such as sweetness, acidity, bitterness, saltiness and umami. So you might smell mint in a wine, then experience that mint-like impression again as part of its flavour, without the wine literally containing mint.

Why do wines have herbal aromas?

There is rarely one simple answer. Herbal character can reflect several factors working together.

Grape variety and chemistry

Different grape varieties contain different aroma compounds and precursors. Some compounds, including methoxypyrazines, can contribute green capsicum, grassy or vegetal impressions. But methoxypyrazines do not explain every herbal aroma, and no grape variety is guaranteed to smell herbal in every wine.

Ripeness

Some green capsicum and vegetal characters are associated with less-ripe grapes. As grapes ripen, certain compounds can decline. This is a useful possibility to understand, but it is not a rule: a minty, dried-herb or eucalyptus-like aroma does not automatically mean the grapes were underripe.

Climate and vineyard conditions

Temperature, sunlight, vine growth, irrigation and other vineyard conditions can influence the development or retention of some aroma compounds. Variety, season and site interact, so “cool climate equals herbal wine” is far too simple.

Winemaking and maturation

Fermentation, yeast, oxygen exposure, vessel choice and maturation can preserve, enhance, soften or mask aromas. The same grape can therefore show a different balance of fruit, herbs, spice and savoury character depending on how the wine is made.

The important beginner lesson is to separate what you smell from why it may be there. A tasting descriptor is an observation, not a complete explanation.

Is a herbal aroma a fault?

Not automatically.

A fresh mint note, savoury dried-herb character or subtle leafy quality may be an intentional or enjoyable part of a wine’s style. At another intensity, a raw, coarse or dominating green character may seem distracting or out of balance.

That judgement depends on context. Consider:

  • How strong is the aroma?
  • Is it fresh, savoury or raw?
  • Does fruit or other character balance it?
  • Does it remain pleasant as the wine opens?
  • Does it suit the style of the wine?

One descriptor cannot diagnose a fault. If you smell capsicum, grass or herbs, that alone is not enough to conclude that the wine is defective. You need to consider the whole wine, including its intensity, persistence and other aromas.

How to practise recognising herbal aromas

Try this simple exercise at home or at a cellar door.

  1. Pour a small amount of wine into a clean glass.
  2. Smell it before swirling, then swirl gently and smell again.
  3. Start broadly: does it seem green, leafy, herbal or savoury?
  4. Look for a more familiar reference: mint, dried herbs, grass, tomato leaf, green capsicum or eucalyptus.
  5. Taste the wine and notice whether the aroma returns retronasally.
  6. Describe its intensity as faint, moderate or pronounced.
  7. Add a quality word, such as fresh, leafy, savoury, raw, integrated or dominating.
  8. Compare it with another wine.

You do not need to identify the “correct” answer. Aroma perception varies between people and is affected by temperature, glass shape, room conditions, recent food and personal sensitivity. Comparing wines helps you notice differences and develop your own reference points.

A useful tasting note might be: “Moderate fresh mint and leafy aromas, with a savoury finish.” That is more informative than simply writing “herbal”.

What herbal aromas do not prove

A herbal descriptor does not prove that:

  • herbs were added to the wine;
  • the wine is faulty;
  • the grapes were underripe;
  • the wine comes from a particular Australian region;
  • the wine is made from one particular grape;
  • a single compound or winemaking technique caused the aroma.

Treat the word as an invitation to investigate what you are smelling. Start with the reference, assess its intensity and decide whether it feels fresh, integrated or excessive. That approach makes wine language more useful—and much less intimidating.
