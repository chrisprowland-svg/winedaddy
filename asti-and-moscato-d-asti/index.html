Asti and Moscato d’Asti: Two Piedmontese Wines, One Appellation Area

Asti and Moscato d’Asti are related wines from the same protected Italian designation, but they are not interchangeable.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Asti and Moscato d’Asti are separate wine categories, not two names for the same wine.
  • Both come from the Asti DOCG, a protected designation in Piedmont, north-western Italy.
  • Both are made exclusively from Moscato Bianco, also called Moscato reale in the specification.
  • Asti, also known as Asti Spumante, is fully sparkling.
  • Moscato d’Asti has a gentler sparkle, is generally lower in alcohol and is usually sweet.
  • The Asti DOCG area covers 51 municipalities across the provinces of Asti, Alessandria and Cuneo.
  • On an Australian shelf, “Moscato” is broad style or grape wording; “Moscato d’Asti DOCG” identifies a specific Italian appellation.

Quick answer: what is the difference?

Asti and Moscato d’Asti are related wines from the same protected Italian designation, but they are not interchangeable.

Both are made exclusively from Moscato Bianco, an intensely aromatic white grape. The key difference is the wine category and its level of sparkle. Asti is the fully sparkling version, with a more persistent mousse. Moscato d’Asti is lightly sparkling—often described in practical wine language as frizzante—with a softer fizz and generally lower alcohol.

Both are associated with floral and fresh-fruit aromas. Their names tell you more than the grape variety: they identify regulated categories made in a defined part of Piedmont under the Asti DOCG rules.

Where is Asti?

Asti is in Piedmont, or Piemonte, in north-western Italy. Within Italy’s geographic hierarchy, Piedmont is the wider region and the Asti DOCG is the protected wine designation. The designation area extends across parts of the provinces of Asti, Alessandria and Cuneo.

The city of Asti is a useful geographic reference, but the appellation is much larger than the municipality itself. The current official material identifies 51 municipalities in the production area.

Useful orientation points include:

  • Asti, the provincial capital and administrative reference point
  • Canelli, a wine town in the southern part of the Province of Asti
  • Santo Stefano Belbo, in the Cuneo portion of the area
  • Acqui Terme and Strevi, in the Alessandria portion
  • Nizza Monferrato, a nearby Monferrato reference point, but not another name for the whole Asti DOCG

From Turin, Asti is roughly south-east and around 45–60 minutes by car in ordinary conditions. Asti to Canelli is approximately 27 kilometres, or about 30–35 minutes by car. These are orientation estimates, not precise travel promises.

The broader Langhe-Roero and Monferrato landscape provides cultural and vineyard context, but it is not a substitute for the legal boundary of the Asti DOCG.

What does Asti DOCG mean?

Asti DOCG is an Italian protected wine designation with European Union Protected Designation of Origin, or PDO, status.

That means the name refers to a defined geographic area and regulated production categories. It does not simply mean “wine made near the town of Asti”, and DOCG should not be treated as a universal ranking that automatically makes one wine better than another.

On a bottle, the protected wording you are most likely to see is:

  • Asti
  • Asti Spumante
  • Moscato d’Asti DOCG

“Asti Spumante” uses spumante, the Italian term for sparkling wine. “Moscato” by itself is broader wording and does not automatically mean Moscato d’Asti.

Asti versus Moscato d’Asti

Feature Asti or Asti Spumante Moscato d’Asti
Appellation Asti DOCG/PDO Asti DOCG/PDO
Grape 100% Moscato Bianco 100% Moscato Bianco
Sparkle Fully sparkling Lightly sparkling or frizzante
Minimum actual alcohol in the specification 6.0% ABV Approximately 4.5–6.5% ABV
Minimum total alcoholic strength 11.5% ABV 11.0% ABV
Typical impression More persistent mousse and a fuller sparkling presentation Softer fizz, lighter body and pronounced grape-like aroma
Sweetness The specification allows a range from pas dosé to sweet Described as sweet and aromatic

The alcohol figures are regulatory parameters, not a promise that every bottle will taste or feel exactly the same. Likewise, it is too simple to say that one category is always sweeter than the other. Asti’s specification allows a broader sweetness range, while Moscato d’Asti is generally presented as sweet.

The shared grape: Moscato Bianco

Moscato Bianco is the required grape variety for both categories under the cited Asti specification. It is also referred to as Moscato reale.

The grape is known for expressive aromas. In the glass, you may find floral, fresh-fruit or grapey characters, with some wines suggesting peach, apricot or citrus-floral notes. These are common sensory expectations, not guaranteed tasting notes.

Moscato Bianco belongs to the broader Muscat family, but that does not mean every wine labelled “Muscat” or “Moscato” follows the Asti DOCG rules.

What should an Australian shopper look for?

An Australian shopper might encounter imported bottles labelled Asti, Asti Spumante or Moscato d’Asti DOCG.

The distinction is useful:

  • Moscato may be broad grape or style wording.
  • Moscato d’Asti DOCG identifies a particular Italian appellation category.
  • Asti DOCG or Asti Spumante identifies the fully sparkling Asti category.
  • Muscat is a broader grape-family term used across countries and wine styles.

Australian wine has its own geographical-indication framework. An Australian Moscato can be aromatic, sweet and lightly sparkling, but it is not automatically Asti DOCG or Moscato d’Asti. The Italian names refer to a regulated place and set of production rules.

FAQs

Is Asti the same as Moscato d’Asti?

No. They are separate categories within the Asti DOCG. Asti is fully sparkling, while Moscato d’Asti is lightly sparkling and generally lower in alcohol.

Are both wines made from Moscato?

Yes. The Asti specification identifies Moscato Bianco, also called Moscato reale, as the required grape variety for both.

Is Asti a town or a wine?

Both names exist, but in a wine context “Asti” refers to a protected designation and wine category. The Asti DOCG area covers 51 municipalities across three provinces, so it is not limited to the city of Asti.

Is Moscato d’Asti always sweeter than Asti?

Not as an absolute rule. Moscato d’Asti is described as sweet, while the Asti specification allows a range from pas dosé to sweet. Individual bottles can vary.

Does Australian Moscato count as Moscato d’Asti?

No. Australian Moscato may share broad characteristics, but Moscato d’Asti is a specific Italian appellation name with defined geographic and production requirements.
