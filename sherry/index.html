What Is Sherry? A Beginner’s Guide

Sherry is a protected-origin family of fortified wines from southern Spain. Learn how flor, oxidative ageing, fortification and solera shape styles from Fino to Pedro Ximénez, plus what Australian drinkers should know about labelling.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Sherry is a family of protected-origin fortified wines from southern Spain.
  • It is not one wine, one grape or one sweetness level.
  • Styles range from very dry and pale to rich and intensely sweet.
  • Flor is a living layer of yeast that shapes some Sherries, especially Fino and Manzanilla.
  • Other styles mature with deliberate exposure to oxygen.
  • The solera system blends wines from different ages to create continuity of style.
  • In Australia, “Sherry” is not the current generic term for locally made fortified wine.

Quick answer: what is Sherry?

Sherry is a family of fortified wines made under the protected Jerez-Xérès-Sherry designation in and around Jerez, in Andalusia, southern Spain. The related Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda designation covers Manzanilla from the Sanlúcar area.

“Fortified” means grape spirit is added to wine to increase its alcohol. But fortification is only part of the story. Sherry’s distinctive character also comes from how the wine matures: under flor, with controlled exposure to oxygen, or through a fractional blending system known as solera.

That is why Sherry can taste so different from one style to another. A Fino can be pale, dry and delicate, while an Oloroso can be darker, fuller and more intensely developed. Pedro Ximénez, often shortened to PX, is rich and intensely sweet. None of these styles represents Sherry as a whole.

Where does Sherry come from?

The protected name is formally Jerez-Xérès-Sherry. Jerez is a region and city associated with Sherry production, not a grape variety. Sherry is not a generic name for every fortified wine made in Andalusia.

Manzanilla is closely related but has its own protected designation: Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda. Keeping these names separate matters because protected wine names describe origin as well as style.

For a broader geographic introduction, see WineDaddy’s guide to Jerez. For the wider category, What Is Fortified Wine? explains how fortification fits into winemaking.

How is Sherry made?

Most dry Sherry begins with a fully fermented base wine, commonly made from Palomino grapes. Naturally sweet styles are particularly associated with Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel. These are not ingredients found in every Sherry: the grape depends on the style and production pathway.

After fermentation, the base wine is assessed and fortified. The timing and level of fortification vary according to the intended style. The wine may then mature biologically under flor, oxidatively with oxygen exposure, or through a combination of pathways.

This is a simplified overview rather than one universal recipe. Amontillado, for example, begins with biological ageing under flor before continuing oxidatively. Different styles take different routes.

You can learn more about the general process in What Is Fortification?.

What is flor ageing?

Flor is a living film of characteristic yeast that develops on the surface of wine in partly filled casks. It consumes oxygen and interacts with the wine, influencing its aroma, flavour and texture.

Flor is central to the character of Fino and Manzanilla. It helps protect the wine from direct oxygen exposure while also giving the wine its own biological-ageing character.

Flor is not mould or contamination. It is a managed part of the winemaking process. The separate guide to Flor ageing explores the idea in more detail.

What is oxidative ageing?

Oxidative ageing is deliberate maturation in contact with oxygen. In Sherry, it can contribute to deeper colour, fuller body and developed aromas.

This is different from unwanted oxidation. Accidental oxidation can make a wine seem tired or stale, whereas oxidative ageing is a controlled choice made as part of a wine’s style.

Oloroso follows an oxidative ageing pathway. Amontillado begins under flor and later continues without it, combining biological and oxidative development. You can read more about the broader concept in What Is Oxidation in Wine?.

What is the solera system?

The criaderas y solera system is a fractional blending and ageing method. A portion of mature wine is drawn from the oldest scale for bottling. It is then replenished with wine from a younger scale, which is replenished in turn.

The result is a continuing blend of wines from different ages. Solera ageing is therefore not ordinary vintage-by-vintage ageing, and the wine in a bottle should not be treated as having one simple average age.

The protected rules also recognise vintage-aged Sherries, so it is not accurate to say that Sherry can never be vintage-dated. See Solera ageing for the full explanation.

The main Sherry styles

Fino

Fino is a dry, pale style associated with biological ageing under flor. It is often delicate and fresh, with savoury, nutty or saline impressions rather than obvious sweetness.

Manzanilla

Manzanilla is a dry, flor-aged style associated with the separate Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda designation. It is generally pale, fresh and delicate.

Amontillado

Amontillado begins with biological ageing under flor and later continues oxidatively. It can combine the delicacy of flor ageing with deeper, nuttier and more developed flavours.

It is not made simply by mixing Fino and Oloroso.

Oloroso

Oloroso is matured oxidatively rather than relying on persistent flor ageing. It is generally fuller-bodied, darker and more intensely developed.

A darker Oloroso is not automatically sweet. Colour is not a reliable shortcut for sweetness.

Palo Cortado

Palo Cortado is an oxidative style that broadly combines the aromatic delicacy associated with Amontillado and the palate weight of Oloroso. Its production history is specific and should not be reduced to the idea that it is simply an accidental wine.

Pedro Ximénez

Pedro Ximénez, or PX, is a naturally sweet style principally associated with Pedro Ximénez grapes. The grapes are commonly dried in the sun to concentrate their sugars before fermentation.

PX is rich, dense and intensely sweet. It is one style of Sherry, not a synonym for every sweet Sherry.

Sweet blended styles

Some Sherries are made by blending or adjusting a dry wine to create styles such as Pale Cream, Medium and Cream. This is an important reminder that sweetness can come from the production method, not only from naturally sweet grapes.

Is Sherry sweet?

Some Sherry is sweet, but much of it is dry. “Dry” describes low sweetness or residual sugar; it does not mean flavourless, fruitless or lacking in aroma.

A pale wine is not necessarily dry, and a dark wine is not necessarily sweet. A dark, full-bodied Oloroso may be dry, while a pale style may have been sweetened.

For a useful foundation, see What Does Dry Wine Mean? and What Is Sweetness in Wine?.

Sherry and Australian fortified wine

Australian readers may encounter older Australian bottles labelled “sherry”. However, Wine Australia states that “Sherry” has been protected in Australia since 1 September 2011, following the phase-out of several European wine terms.

“Sherry” is therefore not the current generic label term for an Australian fortified wine. Qualifying Australian fortified wines may use the term Apera, subject to the relevant requirements and approved-user conditions.

An Australian fortified wine is not Sherry simply because it resembles Fino, Oloroso or PX. It should be described using the appropriate Australian terminology, such as Australian fortified wine, Apera or a relevant tawny-style term.

Labelling rules can change and may depend on the market and circumstances. This is an educational guide, not legal advice.

How should Sherry be served?

Dry, flor-aged styles are commonly served chilled or cool. Fuller dry styles and sweet styles may suit less-cold serving, depending on the wine and context.

Serve Sherry in a small wine glass if that is what you have; one special glass shape is not essential for learning about it. Once opened, keep the bottle sealed, upright and away from heat and light. Storage life varies considerably between styles, so avoid assuming that every Sherry will keep equally well after opening.

Common questions

Is Sherry a grape?

No. Sherry is a protected-origin family of fortified wines. Palomino, Pedro Ximénez and Moscatel are important grapes for different Sherry pathways.

Is all Sherry sweet?

No. Fino, Manzanilla, Amontillado and basic Oloroso are commonly associated with dry styles, while PX and some blended styles are sweet.

Is all Sherry oxidatively aged?

No. Fino and Manzanilla depend on biological ageing under flor. Oloroso follows an oxidative pathway, while Amontillado combines both.

Is Australian fortified wine the same as Sherry?

No. Sherry refers to the protected Spanish wine and is not the current generic label term for Australian fortified wine.
