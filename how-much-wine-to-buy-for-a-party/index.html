How much wine to buy for a party

A useful party-planning formula is:

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Count the guests likely to choose wine, rather than automatically counting everyone.
  • Estimate glasses according to the event’s length and format.
  • Convert glasses into millilitres, then into 750 mL bottles.
  • A 100 mL pour gives 7.5 pours per bottle; a 125 mL pour gives 6.
  • Welcome sparkling and dessert wine are easiest to calculate separately.
  • Add a modest buffer, not a large surplus.
  • Water, food, alcohol-free drinks and transport options belong in the plan too.

Quick answer

A useful party-planning formula is:

Likely wine drinkers × estimated glasses each × pour size ÷ 750 mL = bottles before buffer

For example, if eight people are likely to drink wine, each has two 125 mL glasses, and the event is a meal with other drinks available:

8 × 2 × 125 mL = 2,000 mL

That is 2.67 bottles, so round up to three. If avoiding a shortage matters more than having leftovers, four bottles gives you a modest reserve.

This is a purchasing estimate, not a recommendation for how much anyone should drink. There is no universal Australian rule for the number of bottles required at a private party. Guest preferences, event length, food, other drinks and pouring habits all change the result.

Step 1: Count likely wine drinkers

Start with the people who are likely to choose wine. Do not automatically calculate for every guest.

Your estimate may be lower when:

  • beer, spirits, cocktails or ready-to-drink beverages are also available;
  • several guests do not drink alcohol;
  • children or teenagers are attending;
  • the gathering is short or mainly focused on food;
  • guests are bringing some of their own drinks.

A mixed-drink event does not require you to replace every non-wine drink with an equivalent amount of wine. Estimate the wine separately and plan other beverages separately.

Step 2: Estimate glasses per person

The following are practical planning assumptions, not health guidance or consumption limits:

Event Planning estimate
Short gathering of 1–2 hours 1–2 glasses per likely wine drinker
Meal of 2–3 hours, with other drinks 2–3 glasses
Longer, wine-centred gathering of 3–4 hours 3–4 glasses
Welcome sparkling 1 small offered glass per person who chooses it
Dessert or fortified wine 1 small optional serve

Use the lower end for a short event, a meal with several drink options or a gathering with many non-drinkers. Use the higher end only when wine is the main alcoholic drink and will be available throughout a longer event.

A seated meal often has clearer serving moments than a cocktail-style gathering. At a standing event, self-service and generous pours can make the estimate less predictable.

Step 3: Choose a pour size

For planning, use either 100 mL or 125 mL, and state which one you have used.

  • A 750 mL bottle contains 7.5 pours of 100 mL.
  • A 750 mL bottle contains 6 pours of 125 mL.
  • A 150 mL pour gives five pours per bottle.

These are volume calculations, not promises about how many glasses a bottle will provide. Guests may pour different amounts, and a glass may be topped up before it is empty. For a detailed explanation of bottle-to-glass calculations, see How many glasses are in a bottle of wine.

Pours are not the same as standard drinks

An Australian standard drink contains 10 g of pure alcohol. The Australian Government uses a 100 mL wine serve as an example of approximately one standard drink, while a 150 mL restaurant serve may contain approximately 1.4–1.6 standard drinks depending on the wine’s strength.

A 750 mL bottle can contain different numbers of standard drinks depending on its alcohol concentration and style. Check the label for the specific bottle. Do not assume that one glass always equals one standard drink.

Worked example: a 10-person dinner

Suppose:

  • 10 guests are attending;
  • eight are likely to drink wine;
  • the meal lasts about 2.5 hours;
  • beer and alcohol-free drinks are also available;
  • you plan for two 125 mL glasses per wine drinker.

The calculation is:

8 × 2 × 125 mL = 2,000 mL

That equals 2.67 standard 750 mL bottles. Round up to three bottles. If you want a modest reserve, buy four.

The exact mix depends on the food, weather and guests. Rather than relying on a universal red-to-white ratio, make the most versatile style the largest part of the purchase and include a smaller amount of another style if useful.

Worked example: a 24-person gathering

Suppose:

  • 24 guests are attending;
  • 18 are likely to drink wine;
  • the event lasts about four hours;
  • wine is the main alcoholic drink;
  • you plan for three 125 mL glasses per wine drinker.

The calculation is:

18 × 3 × 125 mL = 6,750 mL

That equals nine 750 mL bottles. Add a modest reserve of about one bottle, giving an estimate of 10 bottles.

If beer or cocktails are equally prominent, or if many guests choose alcohol-free drinks, reduce the wine estimate rather than calculating for all 24 guests.

Calculate sparkling and dessert wine separately

Welcome sparkling is tied to a particular moment, so it is clearer to calculate it separately from dinner wine.

For one 100 mL welcome pour:

  • 6 people require 600 mL: round up to 1 bottle;
  • 12 people require 1,200 mL: round up to 2 bottles;
  • 16 people require 1,600 mL: round up to 3 bottles.

These figures apply to people who choose the welcome pour. Others may prefer water or an alcohol-free option.

Dessert or fortified wine should also be treated as a separate, optional serve rather than counted as a full dinner-wine glass. The Australian Government gives a 60 mL port serve at 17.5% alcohol as approximately 0.9 standard drinks. The amount you need depends on whether it is offered to everyone or only to guests who want it.

Add a modest buffer

After rounding up to whole bottles, add only a small reserve:

  • for a small gathering, one extra bottle may be enough;
  • for a larger gathering, around 10% above the rounded estimate is a reasonable planning convention.

This is not an official Australian standard. It is simply a transparent way to reduce the chance of running out without encouraging excessive purchasing.

If you have unopened wine left, see How long wine lasts after opening for guidance on opened bottles. Unopened bottles are generally easier to keep for another occasion, subject to the product’s storage requirements.

Make the party easier to enjoy

Put water and appealing alcohol-free drinks where guests can easily find them. Provide food, respect people who do not drink and do not pressure anyone to accept a glass.

Avoid topping up a partly finished glass: it makes it harder for someone to keep track of what they have had. Plan transport before the event, particularly for guests who may be driving. Australian alcohol laws and responsible-service requirements can vary between states and territories, so this general hosting advice is not a substitute for local legal guidance.

For practical serving logistics, including chilling wine, see Wine serving temperature explained. For broader purchasing context, see How to choose wine in a bottle shop.

Frequently asked questions

Is one bottle enough for four people?

Not necessarily. The answer depends on how many people are drinking wine, how long the event lasts, the pour size and what else is being served. Use the formula rather than a fixed bottle-to-guest ratio.

How many glasses are in a 750 mL bottle?

It depends on the pour. A 750 mL bottle provides 7.5 theoretical 100 mL pours, six 125 mL pours or five 150 mL pours. Real-world serving may vary.

Should I buy more wine for a long party?

Possibly, if wine is the main alcoholic drink and the event lasts several hours. Increase the estimated glasses per likely wine drinker, then add only a modest buffer.

Does food make drinking alcohol safe?

No. Food can be part of responsible hosting, but it does not cancel alcohol’s effects. Water, food, alcohol-free choices and transport planning should support guest choice rather than encourage anyone to drink more.
