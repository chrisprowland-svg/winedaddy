Somontano: A Beginner’s Guide to This Spanish Wine Region

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Somontano is a protected Spanish wine region in Aragón, in the province of Huesca, on the southern foothills of the Pyrenees. It is known officially as the DOP Somontano and can produce a surprisingly broad range of wines, from red, white and rosé through to sparkling, late-harvest, naturally sweet and liqueur wines.

For Australian wine learners, the simplest way to understand Somontano is this: the name identifies a regulated place, not a single grape or wine style. A Somontano label tells you where the wine comes from and that it meets the designation’s production rules. It does not, by itself, tell you whether the wine will be light or full-bodied, dry or sweet, oaked or unoaked.

Highlights

  • Somontano is a protected wine designation in north-eastern Spain.
  • It sits in Aragón’s Huesca province, between the Ebro Valley and the Pyrenean foothills.
  • Barbastro is the most useful regional centre.
  • The DOP covers 43 municipalities, with vineyards at roughly 350 to 1,000 metres above sea level.
  • Its authorised grapes include local varieties such as Moristel, Parraleta and Alcañón, as well as familiar international varieties.
  • Red, white and rosé wines are all part of the designation, alongside several specialised categories.
  • “DOP Somontano” is the precise current wording; “DO Somontano” is common shorthand and older usage.

Where is Somontano?

Somontano is in Aragón, one of Spain’s autonomous communities, in Huesca province. The vineyards occupy a transition between the Ebro Valley and the mountains, with the Pyrenees to the north.

Barbastro is the practical regional centre and the home of the designation’s Regulatory Council. Other localities used to describe the area include Alquézar, Naval, Salas Altas, Salas Bajas, Pozán de Vero, El Grado, Estadilla and Colungo. These towns help you picture the region, but no single town defines the whole DOP.

From Zaragoza, the major city most useful for orientation, Barbastro is approximately 125 kilometres away by road, generally to the north-east. A journey of around one and a half to two hours is a broad orientation only; actual travel time depends on the route and starting point.

One geographic detail matters: Somontano DOP is not exactly the same territory as the wider Somontano de Barbastro comarca. A comarca is an administrative regional area. The wine designation has its own legally defined boundary, covering 43 municipalities or municipal areas. The two territories overlap, but they should not be treated as synonyms.

What does DOP Somontano mean?

DOP means Denominación de Origen Protegida, or Protected Designation of Origin. It is the Spanish and European wording used for a protected food or wine name whose origin and production requirements are regulated.

Somontano was established as a designation in 1984. You may also see DO Somontano or Denominación de Origen Somontano on older or industry material. In this context, these are naming conventions for the same protected wine designation, not separate regions.

This is similar in purpose to the way Australian drinkers use geographical names to understand origin, but Spanish DOP rules and Australian geographical-indication rules are not identical systems. For broader terminology, see WineDaddy’s guide to Spanish DO and DOCa.

The landscape and climate

Somontano’s vineyards range from approximately 350 to 1,000 metres above sea level. That is a substantial spread, so conditions can vary between sites. The region’s setting combines an inland position, mountain influence and differences in elevation.

Official regional descriptions highlight calcareous and gypsum-bearing soils, along with notable differences between daytime and night-time temperatures in late summer. These factors help explain why regional descriptions often mention fruit, aroma and acidity.

They should not be treated as a flavour guarantee, though. A vineyard’s elevation or soil does not determine the taste of every bottle. Grape variety, harvest decisions, winemaking and maturation also matter. Two Somontano wines can share the same protected regional name while having quite different textures, aromas and levels of body.

Which grapes are used?

The DOP’s specification authorises 15 grape varieties.

Red varieties

  • Cabernet Sauvignon
  • Garnacha Tinta
  • Merlot
  • Moristel
  • Parraleta
  • Pinot Noir
  • Syrah
  • Tempranillo

White varieties

  • Alcañón
  • Chardonnay
  • Garnacha Blanca
  • Gewürztraminer
  • Macabeo
  • Riesling
  • Sauvignon Blanc

This list is one of Somontano’s useful points of difference for beginners. It combines internationally familiar varieties with grapes that give the region a more local identity.

Moristel, Parraleta and Alcañón are particularly useful names to recognise because official regional descriptions identify them as local varieties. That does not mean they are necessarily the most widely planted grapes. The evidence supports their regional significance, but not a claim that one of them dominates the vineyards.

You may also encounter Tempranillo, a variety strongly associated with Spanish red wine. Somontano is not the same region as Rioja or Ribera del Duero, however. The grape can appear in several Spanish regions, while the regional designation tells you where the grapes were grown and the wine was made under the relevant rules. You can read more about what Tempranillo is.

What styles of wine does Somontano make?

Somontano is not limited to one colour or one traditional style. The designation supports:

  • Still red wines
  • Still white wines
  • Rosé wines
  • Wines with ageing or oak-related classifications
  • Semidry, semi-sweet and sweet wines
  • Sparkling wines
  • Late-harvest wines
  • Naturally sweet wines
  • Liqueur wines

That means a Somontano label is a starting point rather than a complete tasting note. A white Somontano may be made from Chardonnay, Macabeo, Alcañón, Gewürztraminer, Riesling or Sauvignon Blanc. A red may involve Garnacha, Tempranillo, Syrah, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot or one of the local red varieties.

Regional industry descriptions often use words such as fruity, aromatic, fresh and intense. These can be useful broad tendencies, but they do not describe every wine made under the DOP. Treat them as orientation, not a promise.

How to read a Somontano label

When you see Somontano on an imported Spanish wine, separate the information into four questions:

  1. Where is it from?
    “Somontano” identifies the protected regional designation.

  2. What colour or category is it?
    Look for red, white, rosé, sparkling or sweet-wine information.

  3. Which grape or blend was used?
    The DOP name does not answer this by itself. Check whether the grape varieties are listed.

  4. Was it aged or made in a particular way?
    Look for relevant ageing or style terms, where declared.

The name Somontano does not tell you whether a wine is oaked, highly tannic, light-bodied, rich, sweet or dry. Those details come from the rest of the label and, ideally, the technical information supplied for that particular wine.

How Somontano fits into Spain

Somontano is one of Spain’s protected wine regions, but it should not be confused with the country itself, Aragón as a whole, or better-known Spanish regions such as Rioja, Ribera del Duero or Priorat. Each has its own geographic identity and regulatory context.

The most useful comparison is not “which region is better?” but “what does each regional name help me understand?” In Somontano’s case, the answer is a varied mountain-influenced setting, a broad authorised grape list and the possibility of several wine categories under one protected regional name.

Frequently asked questions

Is Somontano a grape variety?

No. Somontano is a protected wine region and designation in Aragón, Spain.

Is Somontano the same as Barbastro?

No. Barbastro is a town and the region’s practical centre. The DOP Somontano and the wider Somontano de Barbastro comarca are related but not identical territories.

Is Somontano the same as Rioja?

No. Both are Spanish wine regions, but they are separate protected designations with different geographic boundaries and production rules.

Are Somontano wines always red?

No. The designation supports red, white and rosé wines, as well as sparkling, sweet, late-harvest and liqueur-wine categories.

What should I remember when buying or tasting one?

Start with the grape, colour, sweetness and ageing information on the label. “Somontano” tells you the regulated place of origin; it does not provide a complete prediction of flavour.
