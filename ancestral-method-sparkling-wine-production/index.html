What Is Ancestral-Method Sparkling Wine Production? A Beginner’s Guide

Ancestral-method sparkling wine finishes its original fermentation in the bottle. Learn how it works, why it is linked with pét-nat, and how it differs from traditional and tank methods.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Ancestral-method sparkling wine starts fermenting before it is bottled.
  • The first fermentation continues in the sealed bottle.
  • Yeast converts remaining grape sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
  • The trapped carbon dioxide creates the wine’s sparkle.
  • The method is commonly associated with pét-nat, but the terms are not exact synonyms.
  • Cloudiness, sweetness, lees, bottle variation and disgorgement are possible outcomes—not requirements.
  • It differs from traditional-method and tank-method sparkling wine because the original fermentation finishes in the bottle.

Quick answer

Ancestral-method sparkling wine production involves bottling grape juice or partially fermented wine before its first alcoholic fermentation is complete. The wine still contains fermentable sugar and active or recoverable yeast. Once the bottle is sealed, fermentation continues inside it.

As yeast consumes the remaining sugar, it produces alcohol and carbon dioxide. Because the bottle is closed, some of that carbon dioxide dissolves into the wine, creating effervescence.

This is sometimes called méthode ancestrale or simply the ancestral method. Wines made this way are commonly associated with pét-nat, short for pétillant naturel. However, pét-nat is an informal, unregulated term in Australia, so it should not be treated as a guarantee that every bottle follows exactly the same process.

For broader sparkling-wine context, see What Is Sparkling Wine?.

How the ancestral method works

1. Fermentation begins

After grapes are harvested and pressed, yeast begins converting grape sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. The yeast may be naturally present or added by the winemaker. Using indigenous yeast is not a defining requirement of the ancestral method.

This is the same basic alcoholic fermentation explained in How Wine Is Made, but the timing of bottling changes what happens next.

2. The wine is bottled before fermentation finishes

The partially fermented wine is bottled while some fermentable sugar remains. Producers may manage the fermentation by cooling the wine or using other cellar techniques before bottling. The exact timing varies according to the desired pressure, sweetness, alcohol level and style.

The key point is not a particular temperature or sugar measurement. It is that the original fermentation has not completely finished when the wine enters the bottle.

3. Fermentation continues in the bottle

The sealed bottle traps the carbon dioxide made by the continuing fermentation. Some gas dissolves into the wine, producing bubbles.

This is why the method is sometimes described as bottle fermentation. It does not mean that a separate second fermentation has been started in a finished still wine. Instead, the initial fermentation is completed in the bottle.

What Does Yeast Do in Wine? and What Is Carbon Dioxide in Wine? explain these two ingredients in more detail.

4. The wine may be left with or separated from its sediment

Once fermentation is complete—or has reached the desired point—the bottle may contain lees. Lees are yeast deposits and other fine solids left behind after fermentation.

Depending on how the wine is handled, the lees may remain in the bottle, producing a cloudy or hazy appearance. The wine may also be clarified, filtered, riddled or disgorged. Disgorgement means removing sediment from the bottle.

There is no single finishing style that defines every ancestral-method wine. A bottle can be cloudy or clear, dry or off-dry, and more or less textural.

What does the method change in the glass?

Ancestral-method sparkling wines can show fresh fruit, lively acidity and a textured feel. If lees remain, you may notice yeast-derived, savoury or bready characters, as well as haze. Some wines have softer or less persistent bubbles than highly pressurised sparkling wines.

These are possibilities, not promises. The grape variety, ripeness, fermentation progress, lees management and finishing decisions all affect the final wine. The method does not guarantee freshness, “funkiness”, sweetness, low alcohol or a particular level of pressure.

Bottle-to-bottle variation is also possible when sediment and fermentation remain part of the finished wine. That does not automatically indicate a fault; it is a reminder that method alone does not describe every detail of production.

Is ancestral method the same as pét-nat?

Not necessarily.

Many pét-nats are made using the ancestral method, and Australian wine communication often uses the terms together. But pét-nat is a market-facing style term, not a single regulated production category in Australia. It is therefore safer to say that pét-nat is commonly made by the ancestral method than to say that every pét-nat is identical to every ancestral-method wine.

Pét-nat should not automatically be equated with:

  • natural wine;
  • organic wine;
  • low-intervention wine;
  • wild-yeast fermentation;
  • unfiltered wine; or
  • a particular sweetness level.

Those descriptions concern different aspects of wine production or presentation. A label using “pét-nat” does not, by itself, reveal every yeast, filtration, disgorgement or finishing decision.

Ancestral method versus traditional method

The simplest distinction is the fermentation sequence.

In the ancestral method, grape juice begins fermenting and that original fermentation is completed in the bottle.

In the traditional method, a finished still base wine is made first. A separate second fermentation is then started in each bottle, usually with a mixture of yeast and sugar. The bottle fermentation creates the bubbles.

Read the fuller explanation in Traditional-Method Sparkling Wine Production.

Ancestral method versus tank method

The tank method also starts with a finished base wine and uses a separate second fermentation. The difference is that the bubble-forming fermentation takes place in a pressurised tank rather than in individual bottles.

The wine is generally clarified and bottled under pressure after that tank fermentation. As with the traditional method, the method describes production steps rather than guaranteeing a particular quality or flavour profile.

See Tank-Method Sparkling Wine Production for more detail.

What might you see on an Australian label?

In Australia, “sparkling wine” has a defined meaning under the Food Standards Code relating to carbon dioxide produced through complete or partial fermentation of contained sugars. Wine Australia also explains that pét-nat is not a regulated term and must not be used in a misleading way.

Labels may use terms such as pét-nat, pétillant naturel or méthode ancestrale, but those terms may not disclose every production detail. “Méthode champenoise” is a protected term and should not be used casually for Australian wine.

If you want to understand a bottle more confidently, treat the method term as a useful clue rather than a complete technical specification. The wine’s clarity, sweetness, pressure and sediment still depend on how it was finished.

The takeaway

Ancestral-method sparkling wine is defined by where and when the original fermentation finishes: it begins in juice or partially fermented wine and is completed in the bottle. The yeast’s remaining work creates carbon dioxide, and the sealed bottle retains that gas as sparkle.

Pét-nat is often made this way, but the terms are not universally identical. Cloudiness, sweetness, lees and minimal handling may be common associations, yet none is an automatic requirement. Understanding that distinction makes it much easier to read the label—and to approach the wine in the glass with sensible expectations.

Suggested FAQs

Is ancestral-method sparkling wine always cloudy?

No. It may remain cloudy if lees are left in the bottle, but filtration, clarification or disgorgement can produce a clearer wine.

Is ancestral-method sparkling wine always sweet?

No. Final sweetness depends on how much sugar remains and whether fermentation finishes. The method does not determine a single sweetness level.

Is pét-nat always made by the ancestral method?

No. The terms commonly overlap, but pét-nat is an informal, unregulated term in Australia and should not be treated as a precise guarantee of one production process.

Does ancestral method mean natural wine?

No. Ancestral method describes fermentation timing and location. Natural wine is a broader and less consistently defined production or philosophical term.
