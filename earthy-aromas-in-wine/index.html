Earthy aromas in wine

Learn what earthy aromas in wine can smell like, why they occur, how to distinguish complexity from possible faults and how Australian wine drinkers can describe them with confidence.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • “Earthy” is a broad tasting descriptor, not one specific aroma.
  • It can suggest forest floor, dried leaves, mushroom, truffle, beetroot, damp earth, undergrowth, dust or savoury character.
  • Earthy does not prove that a wine tastes of soil, comes from a particular place or is faulty.
  • Earthiness can come from the interaction of grape variety, fermentation, stems, maturation, bottle age and microbial chemistry.
  • A little earthy character can add complexity; strong mouldy, medicinal, barnyard or musty aromas may be undesirable.
  • “Earthy” becomes more useful when you add a specific description, such as “dried leaf and mushroom”.

Quick answer: what does earthy mean in wine?

Earthy aromas in wine are sensory impressions that remind a taster of natural, fungal, vegetal or dusty environments. You might think of forest floor, damp earth, dried leaves, mushroom, truffle, beetroot, undergrowth or dusty savouriness.

The important word is remind. “Earthy” describes your perception of the wine; it does not demonstrate that literal soil flavour has transferred into it. Nor does the word prove a wine’s terroir, age, grape variety, production method or quality.

It is also not one precise aroma. Australian sensory research has recorded related descriptors including earthy, forest floor, dirt, dust, fungal, mossy, muddy and mushroom. Different tasters may use the same word for slightly different impressions, so it helps to be more specific when you can.

What can earthy wine smell like?

Use these descriptions as reference points rather than a checklist:

  • Forest floor: fallen leaves, moss, damp undergrowth and soil.
  • Dried leaves: a drier, more leafy or autumnal character.
  • Mushroom: fresh or dried mushroom and a savoury fungal note.
  • Truffle: earthy, fungal and savoury.
  • Beetroot: a distinctive root-vegetable character.
  • Damp earth: moist, soil-like and sometimes muddy.
  • Undergrowth or humus: a broad natural-environment impression involving leaves, moss and vegetation.
  • Dusty: dry, powdery or dusty rather than damp.
  • Savoury: less fruit-driven and more reminiscent of herbs, stock, olives or other savoury foods.

These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Savoury is broader than earthy. Dusty is not automatically musty, and earthy is not the same as herbal, mineral or floral. Comparing the descriptor with wine aroma, floral aromas, herbal aromas and spice aromas can help sharpen your vocabulary.

Why might a wine seem earthy?

There is rarely one simple explanation. Wine aroma is shaped by the interaction of grape compounds, fermentation, maturation, the wine’s structure and the taster’s perception.

Grape and wine composition

Grape variety and fruit composition can influence the aromas and texture through which earthiness is perceived. But no variety guarantees an earthy result. The same variety may produce fruit-driven, floral, herbal, spicy, savoury or earthy wines depending on growing conditions, ripeness, fermentation and maturation.

Australian learners may encounter earthy language in tasting notes for both red and white wines. Australian Cabernet Sauvignon sensory research includes terms such as forest floor, dirt, dust, fungal, mossy, muddy and mushroom. This documents a useful local vocabulary; it does not mean Australian wine has one national earthy style.

Fermentation and stems

Fermentation changes the wine’s aroma profile. Stem inclusion or whole-bunch fermentation can also alter structure and create leafy, stemmy or savoury impressions. In some wines, a taster may interpret those impressions as earthy.

That outcome is not automatic. It depends on the fruit, the proportion of stems, fermentation conditions and the balance of the finished wine. Whole-bunch fermentation is therefore one possible contributor, not a guaranteed cause.

The broader distinction between grape-derived, fermentation-related and developed aromas is explained in primary aromas, secondary aromas and tertiary aromas.

Maturation and bottle age

As some wines mature, fresh fruit can become less prominent while aromas such as dried leaves, mushroom and forest floor become more noticeable. These are often called tertiary or developed aromas.

That does not mean every earthy wine is old, or that bottle age always creates earthiness. A young wine can seem earthy, and an older wine may remain primarily fruity or savoury. Age is one possible influence, not a conclusion you can draw from the descriptor alone.

Microbial chemistry

Microbial activity can produce aromas that overlap with earthiness. Brettanomyces, for example, may be associated with medicinal, smoky, spicy, savoury, sweaty, cheesy, barnyard or animal-like characters, sometimes alongside earthy impressions.

Geosmin and related compounds are associated with earthy, muddy or musty odours. But geosmin is not the default explanation for every wine described as earthy. These examples show why aroma descriptors and chemical diagnoses should be kept separate.

Is earthy a good or bad smell in wine?

It depends on intensity, balance and context.

Earthiness can be attractive when it is moderate and integrated with fruit, freshness, savoury detail or spice. A wine may seem layered rather than simply fruity, with the earthy character adding interest without dominating.

It may be less appealing when it becomes strongly mouldy, damp-cellar-like, medicinal, barnyard, sweaty, cheesy, muddy or persistently musty. A dominant character that overwhelms fruit and freshness may indicate a fault or imbalance.

However, “earthy” alone cannot diagnose a problem:

  • Brettanomyces can overlap with earthy aromas, but earthy does not mean Brett.
  • Reduction requires consideration of sulphide-related aromas and how the wine behaves with air.
  • Oxidation is not the same as earthiness.
  • A mouldy or damp-cardboard impression may raise the question of cork taint, but one earthy note cannot confirm it.
  • Volatile acidity is better considered alongside vinegar-like sharpness or solvent-like character, not earthy aroma alone.

The practical question is not simply “Is this earthy?” It is “What kind of earthy, how strong is it, and does it fit with the rest of the wine?”

How to taste for earthy aromas

Try this simple method with any wine:

  1. Smell before swirling. Notice the first impression. Is it mainly fruity, floral, herbal, spicy, savoury or earthy?
  2. Swirl and smell again. Swirling releases more aroma. Look for a more specific association such as dried leaf, mushroom, beetroot or damp earth.
  3. Compare the character. Is the earthiness fresh and leafy, dry and dusty, fungal, muddy or medicinal?
  4. Taste the wine. Consider its fruit, acidity, tannin, texture and finish. Does the earthy note sit comfortably within the wine?
  5. Revisit the glass. Smell it again after a short period. Some aromas become clearer as the wine opens.
  6. Write a fuller note. Instead of “earthy”, try “red fruit with dried leaf and mushroom” or “savoury, dusty and slightly undergrowth-like”.

If you call a wine earthy, you are not claiming to have identified its origin. You are describing what it reminds you of.

Earthy aromas, terroir and soil

A wine’s place can influence growing and production conditions, but an earthy tasting note is not proof that literal soil aroma has entered the wine. It is also not a synonym for terroir or minerality.

This distinction matters because tasting language is often metaphorical. “Forest floor” does not mean the wine contains forest floor. It is a shared comparison that helps communicate an impression.

For an Australian wine learner, the most useful approach is to stay curious and specific. Ask whether the wine smells of dried leaf, mushroom, damp earth, beetroot, dust or savoury undergrowth—and then consider how that character interacts with the wine’s fruit, freshness and structure.

Frequently asked questions

Does earthy wine mean it has gone bad?

No. Earthy can describe an attractive, neutral or undesirable character. Strong mouldy, medicinal, musty or barnyard aromas may suggest a fault, but the word “earthy” alone is not enough to diagnose one.

Does earthy wine mean the wine is old?

Not necessarily. Bottle development can reveal mushroom, dried-leaf or forest-floor notes, but young wines can also seem earthy and older wines may not.

Does earthy mean Brettanomyces?

No. Brettanomyces can create aromas that overlap with earthy descriptions, but it may also produce medicinal, smoky, spicy, sweaty, cheesy or barnyard characters. Technical confirmation cannot be replaced by one tasting word.

How should I describe an earthy wine?

Add the closest specific association and describe its intensity. For example: “dried leaf and mushroom”, “dusty earth with red fruit” or “savoury undergrowth”. Then say whether it is fresh and integrated or dominant and musty.
