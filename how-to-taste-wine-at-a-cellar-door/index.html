Wine Fundamentals

How to taste wine at a cellar door

A beginner-friendly guide to tasting wine at an Australian cellar door, including what to expect, how to compare wines, useful questions, etiquette, spitting and safe-transport considerations.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • A cellar-door tasting is a guided chance to notice and compare wine—not a test you can pass or fail.
  • You only need a simple sequence: look, smell, taste, notice, compare and reflect.
  • You can use everyday words such as fresh, dry, soft, light, full, smooth or warming.
  • Spitting is normal during a tasting, especially when several wines are being poured.
  • Ask the host questions. They expect visitors to be curious.
  • You do not have to like every wine, buy anything or pretend to understand something you do not.
  • Check the venue’s arrangements before you visit, and organise safe transport if you plan to drink.

Quick answer: what do you do at a cellar-door tasting?

A cellar door is a winery’s visitor-facing tasting and sales venue. In Australia, the experience might be walk-in or booked, standing or seated, hosted or partly self-directed, and free or fee-based. Check the venue’s current information before travelling so you know what to expect.

Once you are there, take your time. Look at the wine, smell it before and after a gentle swirl, then take a small sip. Notice its flavour and physical sensations: does it seem crisp, soft, dry, sweet, light, full, smooth, grippy or warming? Think about what remains after you swallow or spit, then compare it with the previous wine.

That is enough. You do not need to identify every aroma or use professional tasting language. The useful question is not “What is the correct answer?” but “What do I notice, and do I enjoy it?”

Before you arrive

Check whether you need to book, whether there is a tasting fee, how long the experience takes and whether there are group-size or age requirements. Some venues offer a structured hosted tasting; others may have a more casual format. Policies can change, particularly during busy periods.

Regional cellar doors may involve driving between venues, warm weather and a full day of tasting. Take water with you where appropriate and decide how everyone will travel home before the first pour. If you drink, do not drive. Consider a non-drinking driver, taxi, rideshare, public transport or nearby accommodation, depending on what is available in the area.

Australian Government guidance defines a standard drink as 10 grams of pure alcohol. A tasting glass, a restaurant glass and a bottle are not automatically one standard drink: serving size and wine strength vary. If you are keeping track, use the standard-drink information provided on packaged wine and ask staff if you are unsure. Follow the venue’s responsible-service instructions and the laws applying where you are visiting.

A simple tasting sequence

1. Look

Hold the glass by the stem or base if that feels comfortable, rather than warming the bowl with your hand. Notice the colour and intensity. Is the wine pale or deep? Still or sparkling? Clear or slightly cloudy?

These are observations, not quality scores. A darker wine is not automatically better, and appearance cannot tell you everything about what the wine will taste like.

2. Smell

Bring the glass towards your nose and take a gentle sniff before swirling. Then swirl carefully and smell again. If you are worried about spilling, keep the glass on the table while you swirl it.

The movement helps release aromas into the space above the wine. You might notice fruit, flowers, herbs, spices, earth or something you cannot name. “It smells fresh” or “I notice something herbal” is a perfectly useful tasting note.

Many flavours seem to come from smell as wine moves through your mouth and nose. The tongue detects basic tastes, while aroma contributes much of the detail. That is why a blocked nose can make food and wine seem less expressive.

3. Taste

Take a small sip and let it move across your mouth. Consider:

  • Sweetness: Does the wine seem dry or sweet? Fruity does not necessarily mean sweet.
  • Acidity: Does it feel fresh, crisp or mouth-watering?
  • Tannin: Does it leave a drying or grippy sensation, particularly towards the gums?
  • Alcohol: Does it feel warming? This is an impression, not a precise measurement.
  • Body: Does the wine feel light or full, rather like the difference between skim milk and cream?
  • Texture: Is it smooth, silky, sharp, creamy, rough or mouth-coating?

You do not need to assess every item every time. Choose one or two sensations that stand out.

4. Notice the finish

The finish is what remains after the sip has been swallowed or spat. Does the flavour disappear quickly, or does it linger? Does the wine leave freshness, warmth, grip or another sensation?

A long finish is not automatically better. It is simply a way of describing persistence.

5. Compare

Cellar-door tastings are useful because wines arrive in a sequence. Ask yourself what changed from one sample to the next:

  • Is this one fresher or softer?
  • Does it feel lighter or fuller?
  • Is the texture smoother or more grippy?
  • Are the aromas fruitier, more floral, herbal or savoury?
  • Which one would you rather drink with food?

Comparison is often easier than judging a wine in isolation. You are building a personal sense of what different wines feel like—not sitting an examination.

Questions to ask the host

The person leading the tasting can add context that the glass cannot provide. Try questions such as:

  • What grape variety or varieties are used?
  • Where is the wine from?
  • What should I notice in this wine?
  • How is it different from the previous sample?
  • How is it best served?
  • What food might suit it?
  • Is this style intended to be enjoyed young, or can it develop with time?

You can also say, “I’m new to tasting—could you explain that term?” A good question does not need to sound sophisticated. Curiosity is more useful than trying to impress the host.

Spitting, water and pacing

Spitting is normal at a wine tasting. If a spittoon is provided, use it discreetly after tasting the wine. Spitting can help you assess more samples without swallowing each one, but it does not make a tasting alcohol-free or guarantee that you will not feel alcohol’s effects.

Take pauses, drink water and pay attention to how you feel. Do not assume that water reverses intoxication or that a small sample has a fixed alcohol content. Wine strength and pour sizes vary.

If you feel affected, stop tasting and follow the venue’s advice. Do not drive after drinking, regardless of whether you swallowed every sample. Arrange transport that does not require you to drive.

Cellar-door etiquette

A few simple habits make the visit more enjoyable for everyone:

  • Arrive on time for a booking.
  • Follow the venue’s instructions about where to stand or sit.
  • Handle glasses carefully and avoid reaching across other visitors.
  • Listen while the host is explaining a wine.
  • Ask questions, but leave room for others to participate.
  • Keep your group’s conversation at a level that does not overwhelm the tasting.
  • It is fine to decline a sample politely.
  • It is fine not to finish a wine.
  • You do not have to buy a bottle.

If you dislike a wine, that does not necessarily mean it is faulty. It may simply not suit your taste. You can say, “This style isn’t for me,” or ask whether the next wine is different. If something seems genuinely unusual and you are concerned, describe what you are noticing and ask the host politely.

Tasting for observation versus drinking for enjoyment

At a cellar door, you may taste wines in a more concentrated way than you would at home. You are looking, smelling, comparing and thinking about structure. At dinner, you might simply enjoy a glass with food and conversation.

Both approaches are valid. Tasting is not about finding an objectively correct favourite. It is about becoming more aware of your own preferences. Write down a few words if that helps—perhaps “fresh, light, lemony” or “full, warming, drying”—but do not turn the visit into paperwork.

Before leaving, ask yourself: “Would I enjoy drinking this?” That answer belongs to you.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need wine-tasting experience?

No. A simple look–smell–taste–notice sequence is enough. Tasting skill develops through attention and practice, not through having a specially trained palate.

Should I swallow every wine?

No. Spitting is normal during a tasting, particularly when several wines are involved. You can also decline a sample or leave some in the glass.

What if I cannot smell the aromas the host mentions?

That is normal. People notice different things, and your perception can be affected by food, perfume, fatigue, illness or the environment. Say what you notice—or say that you are not picking it up.

Is “dry” the same as drying?

No. “Dry” generally describes a lack of noticeable sweetness. A drying sensation is more often associated with tannin. A wine can be dry without making your mouth feel dry.

Do I have to buy wine?

No. A tasting is an opportunity to learn and decide what suits you. Thank the host, be honest about your preferences and follow the venue’s arrangements.

Can I drive after a tasting if I only had small samples?

Do not rely on sample size or a personal estimate. If you drink, arrange transport that does not involve driving, and follow the rules applying to your licence and jurisdiction.
