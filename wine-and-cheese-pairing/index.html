Wine and Cheese Pairing: How to Choose a Wine for Any Cheese Board

Learn how to pair wine and cheese by matching salt, creaminess, texture, intensity and maturity with wine acidity, sweetness, tannin and body. Includes practical pairings for fresh, soft, washed-rind, hard, aged and blue cheeses.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Pair wine and cheese by comparing their strongest features, not by following rigid rules.
  • Salt, creaminess, texture, rind, age and flavour intensity all affect the match.
  • Fresh cheeses usually suit fresh, lighter wines; creamy cheeses often enjoy acidity or bubbles.
  • Sweet or fortified wines are excellent starting points for salty, intense blue cheese.
  • Tannic reds can be powerful with delicate or salty cheeses, so choose them carefully.
  • A dry wine can still taste fruity. Dryness describes sweetness, not the absence of fruit flavour.
  • There is no single perfect wine for every cheese. The best pairing is the one that tastes balanced and enjoyable to you.

Quick answer: how do you pair wine and cheese?

Start by identifying the cheese’s main character. Is it fresh and tangy, creamy, pungent, firm, salty or intensely savoury? Then choose a wine that either matches that intensity or provides a useful contrast.

Fresh, mild cheeses generally work with crisp whites such as Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc or Pinot Gris, as well as fresh sparkling wine. Creamy cheeses often benefit from acidity and bubbles, which create freshness between bites. Firm or aged cheeses can support wines with more flavour, structure or development, including Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon or fortified wine.

Blue cheese is a classic opportunity to try a sweet wine. Sweet Riesling, a late-harvest style or fortified wine can balance the cheese’s salt and intensity. That does not make sweet wine the only correct choice: a fresh sparkling wine or dry Riesling may suit a milder blue cheese or a particular cheese-board accompaniment.

The important point is that grape variety alone does not decide the pairing. A fresh Chardonnay and a rich, oaky Chardonnay can behave very differently. The same is true of Pinot Noir, Shiraz and Riesling. Look for the wine’s acidity, sweetness, tannin, body, alcohol and flavour intensity.

Match the wine to the cheese style

Fresh cheese

Fresh cheeses are usually soft, moist and mild, with a lactic or tangy character. Think of ricotta, cream cheese, fromage blanc or fresh chèvre.

Try these with:

  • Riesling
  • Sauvignon Blanc
  • Pinot Gris
  • Fresh sparkling wine
  • A fresh, lightly chilled Chardonnay

The aim is freshness rather than power. A heavily tannic red, high-alcohol wine or strongly oaked wine can overwhelm a delicate cheese. Herbs, citrus, olive oil or honey can change the result, so taste the cheese with its accompaniments before making a final decision.

Fresh goat cheese is often a useful match for crisp white wine, but “goat cheese” is not a complete flavour description. Age, texture and added ingredients still matter.

Soft-ripened or bloomy-rind cheese

Brie- and camembert-style cheeses have a soft interior and a surface rind. They can be creamy, buttery and mild, although a mature example may be much more aromatic.

Try:

  • Australian sparkling wine
  • Fresh Chardonnay
  • Light Pinot Noir
  • Riesling

Sparkling wine provides a lively contrast to creaminess. Acidity in a still wine can play a similar role by keeping the combination from feeling heavy. Pinot Noir can work when it is relatively light and fresh, but a very tannic or alcoholic red may dominate the cheese.

Washed-rind cheese

Washed-rind cheeses are matured with the rind washed in brine, wine, beer, brandy or another liquid. Their aroma can be much more pungent than their actual flavour.

Because these cheeses can be savoury and aromatic, they need a wine with enough flavour presence. Try:

  • Medium-bodied Chardonnay
  • Pinot Noir
  • Sparkling wine
  • Medium-bodied Shiraz
  • An aromatic white wine

Do not assume that a pungent rind requires a heavy red. The cheese may be soft, salty and delicate on the palate, making a high-tannin or high-alcohol wine feel harsh. Freshness and proportion matter more than simply choosing the biggest wine available.

Semi-hard and hard cheese

Firm cheeses have more chew and can range from mild to strongly savoury. Examples include cheddar- and gouda-style cheeses, along with parmesan-style cheeses.

Try:

  • Chardonnay
  • Pinot Noir
  • Shiraz
  • Cabernet Sauvignon
  • Sparkling wine

The cheese’s age and saltiness are important. A young, mild cheese may suit a fresher wine, while a mature, savoury cheese can stand up to more structured Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon. Sparkling wine offers a different route, combining freshness with enough texture for a salty, firm cheese.

Hard cheese does not automatically require red wine. A fresh white, sparkling wine or fortified wine may be more enjoyable depending on the cheese and the rest of the board.

Aged cheese

As cheese matures, it often becomes firmer, more concentrated and more savoury. That can make it a better partner for a wine with greater flavour intensity or structure.

Try:

  • Structured Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon
  • Developed Chardonnay
  • Pinot Noir
  • Fortified wine
  • Sparkling wine for contrast

Tannin needs to be proportionate. A rich, firm, savoury cheese may make a structured red feel more integrated, but cheese does not simply “cancel” tannin. If the wine leaves your mouth feeling dry and bitter, choose a softer red or a fresher wine.

Blue cheese

Blue cheeses can be salty, creamy, crumbly, pungent and intense. This is where sweetness often becomes especially useful.

Try:

  • Sweet Riesling
  • Late-harvest wine
  • Fortified wine
  • Sparkling wine
  • Dry Riesling with a milder blue

Sweetness can balance the salt and intensity of blue cheese, particularly when the wine also has enough acidity to stay lively. A dry wine can work too, especially with a milder blue, but it may taste harder beside a very salty cheese or sweet accompaniment.

Dry does not mean flavourless

A dry wine can smell and taste strongly of fruit. “Dry” refers mainly to perceived sweetness; it does not mean that the wine lacks apple, citrus, stone-fruit or berry character.

This distinction matters on a cheese board. A fruity dry Riesling may provide flavour without adding sweetness, while a sweet Riesling adds both fruit character and noticeable sweetness. The latter may be a better choice for salty blue cheese or a board with intense savoury flavours.

How to build a mixed cheese board

  1. Choose three or four contrasting cheeses.
    For example, combine a fresh or soft cheese with a semi-hard cheese, an aromatic washed-rind cheese and a blue or aged cheese.

  2. Decide whether you want one wine or a progression.
    One fresh, moderate wine is simplest. Two or three wines allow you to move from delicate flavours to stronger ones.

  3. Taste from mild to intense.
    Start with fresh and soft cheeses, then move towards washed-rind, aged and blue cheeses.

  4. Taste without strong accompaniments first.
    Honey, fruit paste, chutney, pickles, nuts, crackers and cured meats can change the balance. Establish how the wine and cheese work together before adding them.

  5. Reserve sweetness for the strongest flavours if needed.
    A sweet or fortified wine can be saved for blue cheese or a particularly salty, intense cheese.

For one versatile wine, fresh sparkling wine, Riesling or moderate Chardonnay is often a sensible starting point for a board built around mild to medium cheeses. If the board centres on aged or blue cheese, add a second, more structured or sweeter wine rather than forcing one bottle to do everything.

What to do when the pairing tastes wrong

It tastes harsh or drying

The wine may have too much tannin, alcohol or acidity for the cheese. Try a softer red, a fresher white or sparkling wine. You can also retaste with a richer, firmer cheese and remove sharp pickles or vinegar-heavy accompaniments.

It tastes flat

The cheese or accompaniment may be more intense than the wine. Try a wine with more acidity or flavour, or pair sparkling wine with a rich creamy cheese. Removing chutney, honey or fruit paste may also restore balance.

It tastes bitter or metallic

Strong tannin, alcohol, oak, salt or savoury accompaniments may be contributing. Try a lower-tannin Pinot Noir, fresh Chardonnay or sparkling wine, or retaste with a less intensely savoury cheese.

It tastes too sweet

The cheese may be too mild for the wine, or sweet accompaniments may be amplifying the sweetness. Try a drier, higher-acid wine, remove the sweet condiments or choose a saltier, more intense cheese.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best wine for a cheese board?

There is no single best wine. Fresh sparkling wine, Riesling or moderate Chardonnay can be versatile starting points, while sweet or fortified wine is useful for salty blue or aged cheese.

Is red or white wine better with cheese?

Neither is always better. White and sparkling wines are often useful with fresh or creamy cheese, while softer or structured reds may suit firmer, aged cheeses. The wine’s style matters more than its colour.

Does cheese always go with red wine?

No. Many cheeses work beautifully with white, sparkling, sweet or fortified wines. Red wine can taste drying or dominant with fresh, salty or delicate cheeses.

Why does sweet wine work with blue cheese?

Sweetness can balance the salt and intensity of blue cheese. Acidity helps keep the wine fresh rather than heavy.

Can I pair one wine with several cheeses?

Yes. Choose a wine with freshness, moderate body and enough flavour to remain present without overwhelming the mildest cheese. A small progression of wines gives you more flexibility.
