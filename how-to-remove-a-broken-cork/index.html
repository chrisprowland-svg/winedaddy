How to remove a broken cork

Quick answer

These instructions are for an ordinary still-wine bottle, such as one commonly opened at home in Australia. If the bottle is sparkling or otherwise pressurised, stop: pressure can eject the cork. Use separate guidance for opening sparkling wine safely.

For a still-wine bottle, keep it upright and stable. If part of the cork remains in the neck, centre a corkscrew in the remaining cork and pull slowly. If the cork starts crumbling, tilting or sinking, stop forcing it. A fragile cork may need a two-pronged opener or professional help.

If the cork falls into the bottle, leave it there. Pour the wine carefully through a clean coffee filter or fine-mesh strainer into a clean jug, decanter or other suitable container. This removes visible cork pieces, but it does not guarantee that the wine’s flavour is unchanged.

Highlights

  • Use this method only for still wine, not sparkling or pressurised bottles.
  • Keep the bottle upright on a stable surface.
  • Centre the corkscrew and withdraw slowly.
  • Stop if the cork is crumbling, badly tilted or sinking.
  • Do not use a knife, heat, impact or improvised sharp tool.
  • Visible cork fragments are not the same as cork taint.
  • Stop if the bottle, neck or glass appears damaged.

If part of the cork remains

  1. Place the bottle upright on a steady surface and hold it securely.
  2. Inspect the cork. If enough remains and it is reasonably firm, position the corkscrew in the centre of the cork.
  3. Twist the corkscrew in carefully, keeping it as vertical as possible.
  4. Pull upwards slowly and with controlled pressure.

If the cork begins to crumble or lean sharply, do not keep forcing it. A two-pronged opener, sometimes called an Ah-So, can be more suitable for a weak or fragile cork because it is designed to work alongside the cork rather than simply driving a spiral through it. If you do not have an appropriate tool, stopping is better than improvising.

If the cork is pushed into the bottle

Do not try to fish it out with a knife, fork or another household implement. You may push the cork further in or damage the bottle.

Instead, leave the cork in the bottle and pour the wine slowly through a clean coffee filter or fine-mesh strainer into another clean container. Pouring carefully helps you remove visible pieces before serving. A filter is not a guarantee that the wine is unaffected, and it does not correct other wine faults.

A clean filter matters: the aim is to remove cork pieces without introducing new flavours or debris. If you use a kitchen strainer, remember that an unspecified strainer may not catch every small particle.

Broken cork versus cork taint

A broken cork does not automatically mean the wine is spoiled or “corked”. Cork pieces and cork dust are physical fragments. Cork taint is a sensory wine fault, commonly associated with musty, mouldy or damp-cardboard aromas and flavours.

After removing visible pieces, smell and taste a small amount of the wine. If it seems sound, the presence of cork fragments alone does not establish cork taint. For more detail, see what cork taint is.

When to stop

Do not continue opening or serving the wine if:

  • the bottle neck is cracked or the glass appears chipped or damaged;
  • you suspect the fragments may be glass rather than cork;
  • the closure or bottle is unstable;
  • the wine bottle is sparkling or otherwise pressurised; or
  • you are unsure whether the bottle can be handled safely.

For a still bottle with a broken but manageable cork, calm, controlled handling is usually preferable to force. The goal is not to rescue the cork perfectly—it is to keep the bottle intact and the wine free of visible fragments.
