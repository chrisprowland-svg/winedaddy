What is Fiano?

Fiano is a white wine grape that can make fresh, citrus-led or fuller, textural wines. Learn what Fiano tastes like, where it is grown in Australia and how to read it on a label.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Fiano is a white wine-grape variety, not a wine region or brand.
  • It is associated with southern Italy, particularly Campania.
  • Fiano wine can be light and fresh or fuller-bodied and textural.
  • Common descriptors include lemon, grapefruit, peach, apricot and hazelnut.
  • Australian Fiano is grown across several regions, rather than belonging exclusively to one.
  • Climate, site, ripeness and winemaking can all change how Fiano tastes.
  • It is a useful partner for seafood and cream- or tomato-based pasta dishes.

Quick answer: what is Fiano?

Fiano is a white grape variety used to make wine. More precisely, it is a Vitis vinifera wine grape associated historically with southern Italy, especially Campania.

In the glass, Fiano can sit somewhere between bright and refreshing and broad and textural. It may show citrus characters such as lemon or grapefruit, stone-fruit notes including peach and apricot, and sometimes a hazelnut-like character. Acidity gives the wine freshness, while texture can give it more weight across the palate.

That description is a starting point, not a guarantee. Fiano does not have one fixed taste. A lighter, fresher Fiano will feel different from a fuller, more textural example, and climate, ripeness, site and winemaking can all influence the result.

For an Australian wine drinker, the key label lesson is simple: “Fiano” names the grape; a regional name tells you where the grapes or wine come from. Fiano is grown in several Australian areas, so no single region defines the Australian style.

What does Fiano wine taste like?

Fiano’s flavour profile is best understood as a range rather than a checklist.

At the fresher end, it may have lemon and grapefruit characters, with acidity that makes the wine feel lively and refreshing. At the fuller end, peach and apricot can give it a rounder fruit profile, while a more noticeable texture makes the wine feel broader and more substantial.

Hazelnut is another descriptor associated with Fiano. Not every bottle will taste nutty, though. Wine descriptions are comparisons that help us recognise aromas and flavours; they are not ingredients added to the wine.

It can help to think about three parts of the experience:

  • Aroma and flavour: citrus, stone fruit and sometimes hazelnut.
  • Acidity: the fresh, mouth-watering quality that gives the wine energy.
  • Body and texture: how light or substantial the wine feels, and how broadly it sits across your palate.

Is Fiano dry or sweet?

Fiano is commonly encountered as a dry white wine, but the grape itself does not automatically tell you how sweet a wine will be. Fruit aromas such as peach or apricot can make a wine seem fruitier without making it sweet.

The final style depends on factors including ripeness and winemaking. A wine can be dry while still having generous fruit flavour and a rounded texture. See what sweetness means in wine for the difference between fruitiness and sweetness.

For a first look at a bottle, the label, producer’s description and the wine’s overall style are more useful than assuming every Fiano will taste the same.

Where is Fiano grown in Australia?

Fiano is an established but small Australian grape variety. Wine Australia’s latest available planting dataset records 111 hectares of Fiano, while its 2024 data record 2,625 tonnes crushed. These figures come from different datasets, so they should not be read as measurements from the same year.

The 2024 crush data show Fiano coming from several Australian areas. The largest reported shares were:

  • Murray Darling–Swan Hill in New South Wales: 43%;
  • Murray Darling–Swan Hill in Victoria: 11%;
  • McLaren Vale: 10%;
  • Clare Valley: 7%; and
  • Riverland: 7%.

The remaining 21% came from other areas in the dataset.

These figures describe where Fiano was recorded in the 2024 crush data, not a ranking of quality. They also do not mean that Fiano belongs exclusively to any of these regions. Australian wines can show variation from one site and growing climate to another. Climate and wine and terroir provide useful background.

How should you read Fiano on a wine label?

When you see Fiano on an Australian wine label, it is ordinarily a reference to the grape variety. The name is separate from Australia’s protected geographical indications, or GIs.

A GI is a geographic name used to communicate origin. A label might therefore show Fiano alongside an Australian regional name. The regional name does not turn Fiano into a regional grape, guarantee a particular flavour profile or promise a specific winemaking method.

The practical takeaway is that the grape name gives you a broad expectation, while the region, vintage and winemaking choices add further context. For more guidance, read how to read a wine label.

What food goes with Fiano?

Fiano’s combination of acidity and texture makes seafood a natural starting point. Wine Australia also identifies cream-based and tomato-based pasta as useful pairing directions.

Rather than treating pairing as a strict rule, match the wine’s style with the dish. A lighter, fresher Fiano may suit delicate seafood, while a fuller, more textural example can work with richer sauces. The preparation and intensity of the food matter as much as the main ingredient.

The takeaway

Fiano is a white grape variety with southern Italian associations and a growing Australian presence. Expect possibility rather than a single formula: citrus and stone fruit, freshness from acidity, and texture ranging from light to broad. If you see Fiano on a bottle, you are looking at the grape name—not a region—and the best clue to the final style will come from the wine’s regional and producer information.
