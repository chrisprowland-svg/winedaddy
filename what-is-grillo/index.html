What is Grillo?

Grillo is an Italian white wine grape strongly associated with Sicily. Learn what Grillo tastes like, how its style varies and how to read the label.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Grillo is an Italian white wine grape, strongly associated with Sicily.
  • It is a grape variety, not a general category of Italian white wine.
  • Dry Grillo often tastes fresh, fruity and medium-bodied, but the style can vary.
  • You may find citrus, orchard-fruit, floral, herbal or mineral notes.
  • Grillo can also be used for late-harvest, passito and sparkling wines.
  • It has a historical connection with Marsala, but Grillo and Marsala are not the same thing.
  • In Australia, the name may appear on an imported bottle, restaurant list or locally made wine.

Quick answer

Grillo is a white wine-grape variety from Italy, best known for the fresh, fruity and medium-bodied wines associated with Sicily.

A bottle labelled Grillo is usually named for the grape used to make the wine. That does not mean every Grillo tastes the same. Harvest timing, ripeness, climate, sweetness and winemaking can all change the finished wine.

A typical dry Grillo may feel lively and refreshing, with citrus, orchard-fruit or floral flavours. Some examples are rounder or more textured, while others are lighter and fresher. Grillo is also used in sweeter late-harvest and passito wines, sparkling expressions and the historical production of Marsala.

What does Grillo taste like?

There is no single Grillo flavour profile, but dry examples are commonly described as:

  • fresh and balanced;
  • fruity, sometimes with citrus or orchard-fruit notes;
  • lightly floral in some examples;
  • medium-bodied or medium-structured;
  • occasionally herbal, mineral or gently savoury.

The word dry refers mainly to sweetness, not to a lack of flavour. A dry Grillo can still taste richly of fruit or flowers.

Acidity may give the wine a lively, food-friendly quality, although Grillo is not automatically crisp or high-acid. A warmer growing site, riper grapes or particular winemaking choices may produce a fuller, rounder wine. Earlier harvesting or a cooler site may preserve more freshness.

Where does Grillo come from?

Grillo is most strongly associated with Sicily, the large Italian island in the Mediterranean. Many bottles encountered internationally are Sicilian, including wines made under the Sicilia DOC designation.

These are two different kinds of information on a label:

  • Grillo identifies the grape variety or a wine made from it.
  • Sicilia DOC identifies a protected geographical designation with its own production rules.

The two names are related, but they are not interchangeable. Grillo is a grape; Sicilia DOC is a regional wine designation.

Grillo also has a historical connection with Marsala, Sicily’s fortified wine. That does not make Grillo another name for Marsala. A dry, still Grillo and a fortified Marsala are different wine contexts.

Why can Grillo wines taste so different?

The grape name is only the starting point. Several decisions shape the wine in your glass.

Climate and site influence how quickly grapes ripen and how much freshness they retain. Harvest timing matters too: earlier-picked grapes generally preserve more acidity, while later-picked grapes can produce riper, fuller or sweeter expressions.

Winemaking can then change the wine’s texture and aroma. Fermentation and maturation choices may make a Grillo feel leaner and more refreshing, or broader and more rounded.

That is why “Grillo” should be treated as a useful clue rather than a guarantee. The label may tell you the grape, but the bottle’s style details tell you more about what to expect.

How should you choose a Grillo in Australia?

An Australian drinker might see Grillo on an imported Italian bottle, a restaurant list or a locally produced wine. The variety is recognised in Australian wine-industry terminology, but availability and style can differ between producers and markets.

When choosing a bottle, look for clues such as:

  • dry, if you want a savoury, fresh white rather than a sweet wine;
  • late harvest or passito, if you expect a sweeter, richer style;
  • sparkling, if you want bubbles;
  • fortified, if the wine is made in a very different style from ordinary still Grillo;
  • the alcohol level and producer description, which may suggest whether the wine is lighter or fuller.

If you enjoy fresh Australian whites but want something a little less familiar, a dry Grillo may be a useful style to explore. It is better to compare the wine’s freshness, body and texture than to assume that every bottle will resemble the same grape.

What food goes with Grillo?

Dry, fresh Grillo may suit seafood, salads, vegetables and lighter pasta dishes. Fuller or more textured examples can work with grilled vegetables, richer seafood or food with a little more savoury weight.

These are starting points, not rules. Match the food to the wine’s style: fresh wines generally suit lighter dishes, while rounder wines can handle more richness. Sweeter, sparkling and fortified Grillo expressions need different pairing approaches.

For serving, chill a dry Grillo so it is refreshing, but avoid serving it so cold that its aroma disappears. Letting it warm slightly in the glass can make the fruit and texture easier to notice.

Is Grillo the same as Grillo B.?

The Italian National Register records the variety in the technical form Grillo B. In everyday wine language, the bottle and wine are generally referred to simply as Grillo.

The variety also has confirmed synonyms including Ariddu and Riddu. A similar-looking name on a source or label should not automatically be treated as the same grape without checking the context.

Frequently asked questions

Is Grillo a white wine?

Grillo is more precisely a white wine grape. Wines made from it are commonly white, but the grape name describes the variety, not one mandatory sweetness, texture or production method.

Is Grillo always dry?

No. Dry versions are common, but Grillo can also be used for medium-dry, late-harvest and passito expressions, as well as sparkling styles.

Is Grillo sweet?

Usually, when you see an everyday still Grillo, you may expect a dry or close-to-dry wine. Check the label for terms such as late harvest or passito if you want to understand whether it is likely to be sweeter.

Is Grillo the same as Marsala?

No. Grillo is a grape variety. Marsala is a separate Sicilian fortified-wine context in which Grillo has historically been used.

Should I try Grillo if I like fresh white wine?

It may suit you, particularly if you enjoy fresh, fruity and medium-bodied whites. Look for a dry example, then use the description and alcohol level to decide whether you prefer a lighter or fuller style.
