Riverina Wine Region: A Beginner’s Guide

Understand the Riverina wine region in New South Wales, its Big Rivers GI hierarchy, inland climate, irrigation, key grapes and distinctive sweet Semillon association.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Riverina is a registered Australian wine geographical indication (GI) in New South Wales.
  • It sits within the broader Big Rivers zone.
  • The wine region is centred on Griffith, but the legal GI extends across a much larger area of inland NSW.
  • Riverina has a warm, sunny and dry growing environment, with low natural rainfall and high evaporation.
  • Irrigation is fundamental to commercial grape growing in the region.
  • Chardonnay, Shiraz, Pinot Gris/Grigio, Semillon and Sauvignon Blanc are important current varieties.
  • Riverina is particularly associated with sweet Semillon made in suitable years when noble rot develops.
  • “Riverina” on a label identifies geographical origin; it does not guarantee a grape variety, sweetness or flavour profile.

Quick answer: what is Riverina known for?

Riverina is a large inland wine region in south-western New South Wales, within the Big Rivers zone. Its warm, dry conditions and low natural rainfall make irrigation central to commercial viticulture.

The region is best known for a broad range of warm-climate wines, including Chardonnay, Shiraz, Pinot Gris/Grigio, Semillon and Sauvignon Blanc. Its most distinctive style association is sweet Semillon made from grapes affected by Botrytis cinerea, often called noble rot. In suitable late-season conditions, this fungus can reduce water in ripe grapes and concentrate their sugars and acids.

That association is useful, but it is not a rule. Not every Riverina Semillon is sweet, and the Riverina GI does not require any particular grape or wine style. Vineyard site, variety, irrigation, vintage and winemaking all influence the bottle.

Where is the Riverina wine region?

The formal hierarchy is:

Australia → New South Wales → Big Rivers zone → Riverina region

Riverina was entered in Australia’s Register of Protected Names on 12 November 1998. The official GI name is Riverina; “Riverina wine region” is a helpful reader-facing description rather than a separate official alias.

Wine Australia describes the wine region as a broad, mostly flat area on the south-western plains of NSW, centred on Griffith. Other familiar towns and localities associated with the wider geography include Leeton, Narrandera, Wagga Wagga, Hay, Deniliquin, Finley, Berrigan, Junee and Temora.

These places help you picture the region, but they should not be treated as a complete list of towns inside the legal GI. Nor is Griffith the boundary of Riverina. The formal boundary is defined by Wine Australia’s written GI description; maps are useful translations of that legal description.

For a broad Australian orientation, Riverina lies west to south-west of Sydney. Griffith is approximately 570–600 kilometres from Sydney by road, or around seven hours of continuous driving in some route estimates. That is only an orientation guide: the actual time depends on your starting point, route, traffic, road conditions and stops.

Griffith is the most useful centre for understanding Riverina wine. Wagga Wagga is an important regional city in the broader administrative Riverina, but it should not be used as a synonym for the Riverina wine GI.

What does Riverina on a wine label mean?

When “Riverina” appears as a geographical indication on an Australian wine label, it identifies the wine’s geographic origin within the protected GI system.

It does not, by itself, tell you that the wine is:

  • made from Semillon;
  • sweet or affected by noble rot;
  • irrigated in a particular way;
  • from Griffith or another specific town;
  • made from one grape variety;
  • oaked or unoaked;
  • high or low in alcohol;
  • suitable for ageing;
  • a particular quality level.

Read the regional name alongside the variety, blend, vintage and winemaking information. A GI is a place signal, not a promise that every bottle from that place will taste alike. Our guide to how to read a wine label explains how those details work together.

Riverina’s climate and viticulture

Riverina has a warm inland growing environment with abundant sunshine, high evaporation, low relative humidity and limited natural rainfall. These conditions can help grapes ripen, but they also make water management important.

Wine Australia states that grapes can only be grown economically in Riverina with irrigation because natural rainfall is low. Irrigation is therefore a fundamental part of commercial viticulture in the region. That does not mean every vineyard uses the same equipment or receives the same amount of water. It also does not mean irrigation guarantees a particular yield, quality or flavour.

Heat is another important part of the region’s viticultural story. Some ripening seasons include temperatures of 40°C or more, creating pressure for growers to monitor vine stress and manage water carefully. This helps explain why variety choice, vineyard decisions and irrigation practices matter so much in an inland region.

Late in the growing season, humidity can sometimes increase. When the timing and conditions are suitable, this may allow Botrytis cinerea to develop on ripe grapes. That occasional combination of dry warmth and favourable late-season humidity is central to Riverina’s sweet Semillon association.

It is safer to say that Riverina can provide conditions for noble rot than to suggest that Botrytis occurs every year or in every vineyard.

Soils and the influence of place

Riverina’s vineyards occupy varied inland alluvial landscapes formed from ancient stream deposits. Wine Australia describes red-brown earths, clay, sand, gravel and limestone-related material across the region.

These differences can affect drainage, water-holding capacity, vine vigour and the conditions around a vine’s roots. They are part of Riverina’s sense of place, but soil should not be treated as a simple flavour recipe. Limestone in a vineyard does not automatically make every wine taste “mineral”, and no single soil type describes the entire GI.

This is one reason regional wine descriptions should be treated as tendencies rather than guarantees. Climate, soil, irrigation, variety, vintage and winemaking interact. You can explore those relationships further in our guides to terroir, how climate affects wine and how soil affects wine.

Which grapes are important in Riverina?

Wine Australia’s 2024–25 regional snapshot identifies Chardonnay, Shiraz, Pinot Gris/Grigio, Semillon and Sauvignon Blanc as the leading varieties by the 2025 regional crush share. These figures describe a particular period, not permanent rankings or legal requirements.

Cabernet Sauvignon is also an important regional reference. Other varieties are grown as well, but a short list is more useful for beginners than an exhaustive inventory.

Chardonnay

Chardonnay is one of Riverina’s major varieties. Regional examples can range from fresh, varietal wines to fuller styles in which oak and maturation add weight and texture.

That does not mean every Riverina Chardonnay is oaked, full-bodied or designed for long ageing. The grape, vineyard and winemaking choices remain important. Our guide to Chardonnay explains the variety in more detail.

Shiraz

Riverina Shiraz can cover a broad range, from accessible, fruit-driven wines to more structured or refined examples. Warm conditions can support ripe fruit, but the final style also depends on harvest decisions, site and winemaking.

There is no single Riverina Shiraz profile. It is more useful to expect variation than to assume that every bottle will be powerful, sweet-fruited, high in alcohol or heavily oaked. Learn more about Shiraz.

Semillon and sweet wine

Semillon is the variety most strongly connected with Riverina’s distinctive sweet-wine story.

In suitable years, some Semillon grapes remain on the vine beyond normal maturity. If late-season conditions allow noble rot to develop, the grapes can lose water while retaining and concentrating sugars and acids. The result may be a rich, sweet dessert style.

The important word is suitable. Weather, fruit selection and winemaking all matter. Not every Riverina Semillon is sweet, and the GI does not guarantee Botrytis. Dry Semillon is also possible.

For a general explanation of sweetness, see what is sweet wine?.

Pinot Gris/Grigio and Sauvignon Blanc

Pinot Gris/Grigio and Sauvignon Blanc are also important current Riverina varieties. They add white-wine diversity beyond Chardonnay and Semillon.

The available regional evidence does not justify assigning one universal Riverina flavour profile to either grape. Look to the individual label and wine description for clues about freshness, texture, sweetness and oak.

Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon is among the other significant varieties identified in Riverina’s regional profile. As with Shiraz, its style can vary according to site, vintage and winemaking. The regional name provides useful geographical context, but not a complete tasting note. See Cabernet Sauvignon for a broader guide.

What should you expect from a Riverina wine?

A Riverina wine may be a useful choice when you want to explore warm-climate Australian styles, but the label alone cannot tell you exactly what is in the bottle.

Broad tendencies include:

  • ripe fruit expression in some warm-climate wines;
  • a range of fresh and fuller Chardonnay styles;
  • varied Shiraz styles;
  • important plantings of Pinot Gris/Grigio and Sauvignon Blanc;
  • the possibility of sweet, noble-rot Semillon in suitable conditions.

These are regional associations, not guarantees. A Riverina wine may be dry or sweet, light or full-bodied, unoaked or oaked, fresh or richly textured. Variety and winemaking information will usually tell you more about the expected style than the GI name alone.

Riverina and nearby wine regions

Riverina is sometimes confused with other inland Australian wine regions, but these are separate geographical indications.

  • Murray Darling is a separate GI spanning parts of western New South Wales and north-western Victoria.
  • Perricoota is a separate NSW GI in the broader inland and Big Rivers conversation.
  • Swan Hill is a separate GI associated with inland Victoria.
  • Cowra is a separate NSW GI in the Central Ranges context.
  • Hilltops and Gundagai are also separate NSW wine GIs.

These regions may share some broad inland conditions or viticultural challenges. That does not make them Riverina subregions or synonyms. The broader administrative Riverina is also not identical to the legally defined Riverina wine GI.

Frequently asked questions

Is Riverina a wine region or a grape variety?

Riverina is a wine geographical indication, or GI. It is a protected place name, not a grape variety.

Is Riverina wine always sweet?

No. Riverina is particularly associated with sweet Semillon made in suitable noble-rot years, but the region produces dry wines and many grape varieties.

What is Riverina’s most famous wine style?

The strongest evidence-backed style association is sweet Semillon made when late-season conditions allow Botrytis, or noble rot, to develop. That is a tendency, not a guarantee.

Is Griffith the Riverina wine region?

Griffith is the principal wine-region centre and a useful way to orient yourself, but Riverina is a much larger legally defined GI.

Does Riverina wine come from Wagga Wagga?

Wagga Wagga is an important city in the broader administrative Riverina. It should not automatically be treated as the centre or synonym of the Riverina wine GI.

Which grapes are grown in Riverina?

Current important varieties include Chardonnay, Shiraz, Pinot Gris/Grigio, Semillon and Sauvignon Blanc. Cabernet Sauvignon is also a significant regional reference.

What does Riverina mean on a wine bottle?

It indicates geographic origin within the protected Riverina GI. It does not guarantee a specific grape, sweetness, flavour, quality level or winemaking method.
