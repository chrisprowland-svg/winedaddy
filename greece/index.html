Greece: A Beginner’s Guide to Greek Wine

Understand Greek wine through its geography, protected place names, key grapes and styles, with practical guidance for Australian wine learners.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Greece is a diverse wine country, not a single wine style.
  • Mountains, elevation, islands, coastline and inland conditions all help create regional differences.
  • Assyrtiko, Xinomavro and Agiorgitiko are three useful starting points.
  • Greek labels may lead with a grape, a place, or both.
  • PDO and PGI are protected European geographical categories.
  • A town or island name is not automatically the same as a legally defined wine region.
  • Greek wine can include crisp whites, structured reds, sweet wines, fortified styles and Retsina.

Quick answer: what should an Australian wine learner understand about Greece?

Greece is a geographically varied wine country in south-eastern Europe. Its wines reflect the interaction of native grape varieties, mountain and island landscapes, elevation, maritime exposure, local climate and winemaking choices.

For an Australian wine learner, the easiest way in is to remember three grapes and three place associations:

  • Assyrtiko, especially associated with Santorini, for dry, high-acid white wines.
  • Xinomavro, associated with Naoussa and Amyndeon, for structured, savoury reds.
  • Agiorgitiko, associated with Nemea, for a range of red wine styles, from approachable to more structured.

Other useful names include Moschofilero from Mantinia, Muscat from Samos, and varieties such as Malagousia, Vidiano, Robola and Limnio.

Greek wine is not defined by one climate or flavour. The mainland, Peloponnese, Aegean islands, Crete, Ionian Islands and northern regions all bring different combinations of altitude, exposure, rainfall, warmth and maritime influence. Even within one protected region, vineyards at different elevations or orientations can produce different wines.

When reading a Greek label, look for the combination of grape + place + style. That approach is often more useful than trying to memorise every Greek wine name.

Where is Greece’s wine made?

Greece occupies the southern part of the Balkan Peninsula and combines mountainous mainland areas, coastal zones, peninsulas and numerous islands. Much of the country is mountainous, while its extensive coastline creates many places where the sea can influence growing conditions.

The main geographic contrasts are useful to remember:

  • Northern Greece includes inland and elevated areas with stronger continental influences than many islands.
  • The Peloponnese contains mountain ranges, inland plateaux and maritime-influenced areas.
  • The Aegean islands include exposed, dry sites where wind and limited water can be important vineyard influences.
  • Crete is a large southern island with several wine areas and a range of native varieties.
  • The Ionian Islands and Epirus provide western examples where maritime conditions and elevation are important.

These are broad patterns, not guarantees. Elevation, aspect, soils, vine age, yields, harvest timing and winemaking can all change the final wine. A wine from an island is not automatically light, salty or fresh, just as a wine from a mountain area is not automatically high in acidity.

Greek wine areas worth knowing

You do not need to learn every Greek PDO and PGI to begin understanding the country. A few representative areas provide a useful mental map.

Santorini and the Cyclades

Santorini is one of the best-known reference points for Assyrtiko and for island viticulture. The dry, exposed environment and strong winds are important parts of its vineyard story. Wines labelled with the Santorini PDO or the broader Cyclades PGI connect the bottle to a defined geographic framework.

Assyrtiko-based wines are often dry and high in acidity, with citrus or stone-fruit character. Some also have a firm, mineral or saline-associated impression. These descriptors are tasting observations, not simple measurements of seawater or soil.

Naoussa and Amyndeon

Naoussa and Amyndeon, in northern Greece, are important places to understand Xinomavro. PDO Naoussa is strongly associated with the variety, while Amyndeon offers another significant northern reference.

Xinomavro can make red wines with pronounced acidity and tannin, red-fruit character and savoury, herbal or earthy notes. The style can vary considerably with altitude, site, ripeness, extraction, oak and ageing. It is better to understand Xinomavro on its own terms than to call it “the Greek version” of another grape.

Nemea

Nemea, in the Peloponnese, is a principal reference point for Agiorgitiko. The PDO area includes villages with differences in elevation, soils and orientation, so wines from the broader area need not taste identical.

Agiorgitiko can produce several styles, from fresh and approachable reds to fuller or more structured wines. The grape is not inherently soft, sweet-fruited or full-bodied; those are possible outcomes influenced by site and winemaking.

Mantinia

Mantinia is especially useful for understanding Moschofilero, an aromatic, high-acid variety. It provides a contrast with the more familiar Australian habit of choosing white wine primarily by varieties such as Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc or Riesling.

Crete

Crete is a large island wine area with several protected designations and a range of local grapes, including Vidiano and Liatiko. Its wines are a reminder that “island wine” is not a single category. A large island can contain substantial variation in elevation, aspect and local climate.

Samos and the eastern Aegean

Samos is strongly associated with Muscat-based sweet wines. The important lesson is to distinguish that association from the idea that every wine from Samos, or every Greek island wine, is sweet.

Attica, the Ionian Islands and Epirus

Attica is useful context for Savatiano and Retsina. The Ionian Islands and Epirus introduce varieties and places such as Robola of Kefalonia and Zitsa. Together, these areas broaden the picture beyond the three best-known grape associations.

Three Greek grapes to start with

Assyrtiko

Assyrtiko is a major Greek white grape and a practical starting point for drinkers who enjoy dry, high-acid whites.

It is closely associated with Santorini but is also grown elsewhere. Depending on origin and winemaking, Assyrtiko may show citrus, stone fruit, firm acidity and a mineral or saline-associated character. Oak, lees contact, ripeness and blending can all alter the style.

A useful bottle-shop question is: “Is this a crisp, high-acid style, or a broader and more textured interpretation?”

Xinomavro

Xinomavro is a principal northern Greek red grape, particularly associated with Naoussa and Amyndeon.

It can offer substantial acidity and tannin, along with red fruit, savoury, herbal or earthy notes. Some examples can develop complexity with age, but not every Xinomavro will have the same level of structure or ageing potential.

If you enjoy structured reds, Xinomavro may be an interesting variety to explore. It does not need to resemble Australian Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon to be understandable.

Agiorgitiko

Agiorgitiko is especially associated with Nemea and can produce a broad range of red styles.

Depending on site, yields, ripeness and winemaking, it may be fresh and approachable or deeper and more structured. This flexibility makes it a useful reminder that a grape name alone does not tell you everything about a wine’s body, tannin or flavour.

Other Greek varieties worth recognising

Greek wine includes many native varieties, alongside international grapes such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and Merlot.

The most useful secondary names include:

  • Moschofilero: aromatic and high-acid, especially relevant to Mantinia.
  • Malagousia: an aromatic white variety used in expressive dry wines.
  • Savatiano: important in Attica and associated with both dry white wine and Retsina.
  • Roditis: a widely used pink-skinned variety.
  • Vidiano: a useful Cretan white variety.
  • Robola: associated with Kefalonia.
  • Limnio: an important northern Greek red variety.
  • Mavrodaphne: linked with sweet and fortified traditions, while also appearing in dry interpretations.
  • Muscat or Moschato: central to sweet-wine traditions including Samos.

You do not need to memorise this list. Recognising a few names makes an unfamiliar label feel more approachable.

What do PDO and PGI mean?

Greek wines may use the European Union categories PDO and PGI.

  • PDO, or Protected Designation of Origin, identifies wines from a defined place made under specified conditions.
  • PGI, or Protected Geographical Indication, is a broader protected geographic category with defined geographic and production requirements.

On Greek labels, you may also see the Greek abbreviations ΠΟΠ for PDO and ΠΓΕ for PGI.

These categories are about place and production rules. They are not a simple quality ranking in which PDO automatically means “better” than PGI.

A familiar place name is not necessarily the same as a legally defined wine designation. A town, island or broader geographic area may be used in different ways, so it is worth checking the full label rather than assuming that every place name has the same legal status.

What styles of Greek wine might you encounter?

Greek wine is more varied than the country’s most famous white and red examples suggest. You may encounter:

  • dry, high-acid whites made from Assyrtiko, Moschofilero, Malagousia, Roditis, Vidiano or Robola;
  • structured or savoury reds made from Xinomavro;
  • lighter, fresher or fuller red styles made from Agiorgitiko and other varieties;
  • rosé wines;
  • sweet Muscat wines, especially associated with Samos;
  • sweet or fortified wines involving Mavrodaphne;
  • Retsina, a traditional resin-influenced wine category;
  • sparkling wines, where the method and designation should be checked on the individual bottle.

Retsina deserves particular care. It is not a generic word for Greek white wine. It describes a traditional category associated with resin-related production and protected geographical usage. If you have never tried it, approach it as a distinct style rather than assuming it will taste like a standard dry white.

How to approach Greek wine in Australia

Greek wine available in Australia is a selective sample of the country’s production. Importers may use English explanations, transliterations or additional back-label information, and the label presentation may differ from what you would see in Greece.

When choosing a bottle at an Australian bottle shop, start with three questions:

  1. What is the grape? Is it Assyrtiko, Xinomavro, Agiorgitiko, Muscat or another variety?
  2. What is the place? Does the label mention Santorini, Naoussa, Nemea, Mantinia, Samos or another protected area?
  3. What style should I expect? Is it dry, sweet, sparkling, fortified, resin-influenced, light-bodied or structured?

This method is more useful than looking for a direct Australian equivalent. A Greek wine may remind you of a style you already enjoy, but unfamiliar varieties should not be reduced to “Greek Shiraz” or “Greek Riesling”.

Greek geographical names should also be used accurately in Australia. Protected geographical indications are not generic names for any wine with a similar style.

Frequently asked questions

Is Greek wine always high in acidity?

No. Some Greek wines, including many Assyrtiko and Moschofilero examples, can be notably high in acidity, while other wines are broader, softer or more full-bodied. Grape, place, ripeness and winemaking all matter.

Is Assyrtiko only grown on Santorini?

No. Santorini is its most famous reference point, but Assyrtiko is also grown elsewhere. Origin and winemaking can produce different styles.

Is Xinomavro the same as Nebbiolo?

No. They can share some broad impressions, such as acidity, tannin and savoury complexity, but Xinomavro is its own Greek variety.

Is Retsina simply Greek white wine?

No. Retsina is a distinct traditional category with resin-related production and protected geographical usage.

What is the best Greek wine?

There is no useful universal answer. A better starting point is to match the wine’s grape, place and style with what you already enjoy.
