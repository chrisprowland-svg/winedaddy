What is noble rot?

Noble rot is a beneficial form of *Botrytis cinerea* infection that concentrates ripe grapes. Learn how it differs from harmful rot, late harvest and Icewine.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Noble rot is a beneficial infection of ripe wine grapes by the fungus Botrytis cinerea.
  • It needs the right balance of moisture and drying. Too much persistent wetness can lead to destructive rot instead.
  • The fungus causes grapes to lose water and shrivel, concentrating their juice.
  • Noble rot can help produce distinctive sweet wines, but it does not determine one universal style or sweetness level.
  • It is often uneven, so suitable grapes may need to be picked selectively.
  • Noble rot is not the same as late-harvest wine, Icewine or wine made from raisined grapes.

Quick answer

Noble rot is a desirable, carefully managed form of infection by Botrytis cinerea on ripe wine grapes. Under suitable conditions, moisture or humidity allows the fungus to infect the berries; a following period of drying helps the berries lose water and shrivel rather than remain wet and decay.

As water leaves the grapes, their juice becomes more concentrated. This can increase the concentration of sugar and intensify some flavours, helping winemakers produce rich, distinctive sweet wines. The exact result depends on the grape variety, weather, degree of infection, harvest decisions and winemaking choices.

The important catch is that Botrytis cinerea is not always beneficial. The same fungus can cause grey mould or Botrytis bunch rot, a destructive disease that damages grapes and may contribute mouldy or faulty wine. Noble rot is therefore not simply “any grapes with mould”; it is the favourable outcome of a complicated vineyard process.

How does noble rot work?

Noble rot begins with Botrytis cinerea, a fungus found in vineyard environments. Moisture, high humidity and mild or cool conditions can encourage infection, particularly when berries have small openings or wounds.

The infection may then develop in a useful direction if the grapes dry sufficiently. The fungus damages the berry in a way that allows water to escape. The shrivelled grape contains less water, so its juice becomes more concentrated.

That concentration can affect several parts of the grape and the resulting wine:

  • sugar and other dissolved grape components become more concentrated;
  • the texture of the juice may become richer;
  • flavours and aromas may become more intense or complex;
  • the balance between sweetness, acidity and alcohol depends on the fruit and the winemaking.

It is better to think of noble rot as concentrating and transforming the grape than as simply “making the grape sweeter”. Not every berry develops in the same way, and noble rot does not guarantee one particular level of sweetness or acidity.

Why is noble rot difficult to produce?

Noble rot depends on a narrow set of conditions. The vineyard needs enough moisture for infection, followed by enough drying to prevent the fungus from turning into destructive wet rot. Weather, airflow, temperature, bunch compactness, grape variety, ripeness and berry condition all matter.

The timing is difficult to predict. A vineyard may have some berries with desirable noble rot, some that remain sound and others affected by damaging grey mould. Infection can also vary between bunches or across different parts of the vineyard.

For that reason, suitable fruit may be harvested selectively. Pickers can separate appropriately affected grapes from sound fruit or from grapes that have deteriorated too far. In some situations, harvesting may happen in more than one pass as different berries reach the desired condition.

This uncertainty makes noble-rot wines risky to produce. Unhelpful weather can reduce the crop, allow disease to spread or create fruit that is unsuitable for quality wine.

Noble rot versus harmful rot

The word “noble” describes the desirable winemaking result, not a different fungus.

When conditions remain wet, Botrytis cinerea can develop into grey mould or Botrytis bunch rot. The grapes may become soft and covered with grey growth, and the disease can spread through a bunch. It may reduce yields and contribute unwanted mould-related characters to wine.

Noble rot is therefore conditional. A small amount of Botrytis infection is not automatically beneficial, and all Botrytis-affected fruit is not suitable for making noble-rot wine. Vineyard decisions and careful sorting are part of the difference between a concentrated, useful crop and a damaged one.

What does noble-rot wine taste like?

There is no single noble-rot flavour. Depending on the grape, climate, harvest condition and winemaking, descriptions may include apricot, citrus, marmalade or honey, along with a richer or more concentrated texture.

These are possibilities, not guarantees. A wine made from botrytised grapes can vary in sweetness, acidity, aroma and body. Fermentation choices also matter: noble rot influences the grapes, but it does not dictate the finished wine on its own.

Noble rot in Australia

Australian wine-industry education identifies the Riverina in New South Wales and the Hunter Valley as places where suitable humid conditions can support Botrytis styles. Australian examples include Botrytis Riesling and Botrytis Semillon.

That does not mean every sweet wine from either area is botrytised. The relevant conditions vary from season to season, and producers may use “botrytised”, “noble rot” or a style description in different ways. A label should be treated as a useful clue, not as a complete explanation of how the grapes were grown or the wine was made.

Australia also has a recognised history of Botrytis Semillon, including the Noble One style. This is an example of Australian botrytised dessert wine rather than a claim that one producer or region represents the entire category.

How is noble rot different from other sweet-wine methods?

Noble rot is one way to concentrate grapes. It should not be confused with these related terms:

Botrytised wine

A botrytised wine is made, at least in part, from grapes affected by Botrytis cinerea. The term describes the grape condition, not one identical recipe or sweetness level used everywhere.

Late-harvest wine

“Late harvest” is a broad description of grapes picked later than usual. Those grapes may be very ripe, dehydrated, affected by noble rot or concentrated in another way. Late harvest and noble rot are not synonyms.

Icewine

Icewine is made from grapes that freeze naturally on the vine and are pressed while frozen under the relevant production method. Its concentration comes from freezing and removing water as ice, not from fungal infection.

Raisined or dried-grape wine

These wines use grapes concentrated through drying or raisining. The grapes lose water without requiring Botrytis cinerea. The biological process is therefore different from noble rot.

What should you remember?

Noble rot is best understood as a fortunate but carefully managed vineyard outcome. The fungus Botrytis cinerea can help ripe grapes lose water and concentrate their juice when moisture is followed by suitable drying. The same fungus can also destroy grapes when conditions remain too wet.

So when you see “noble rot” or “botrytised” in a wine description, think of concentrated grapes and a distinctive production method—but not a guaranteed sweetness, flavour or quality. The final wine still depends on the vineyard, the harvest and the winemaking.

Frequently asked questions

Is noble rot actually mould?

Yes. Noble rot is caused by the fungus Botrytis cinerea. It is considered “noble” only when the infection develops under suitable conditions and contributes to a desirable wine outcome. The same fungus can cause destructive grey mould.

Does noble rot always make a sweet wine?

No. Noble rot commonly supports sweet-wine styles because it concentrates grape juice, but the finished sweetness also depends on fermentation and other winemaking decisions.

Is noble rot the same as botrytised wine?

They are closely related terms. Noble rot describes the beneficial grape condition, while botrytised wine broadly describes wine made from grapes affected by Botrytis cinerea. Usage can vary, so neither term should be assumed to describe one identical style everywhere.

Is noble rot the same as Icewine?

No. Noble rot involves fungal infection and dehydration. Icewine involves grapes freezing naturally on the vine, with water removed as ice during pressing.

Why are noble-rot grapes harvested selectively?

Noble rot is often uneven. Some grapes may be suitably affected, while others are still sound or have developed destructive rot. Selective harvesting helps separate fruit with different conditions.
