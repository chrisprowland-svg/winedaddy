What Is Acidity in Wine?

Learn what acidity in wine tastes like, how it differs from pH, sweetness and tannin, and how variety, climate and winemaking shape freshness and balance.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Acidity gives wine freshness, liveliness and a mouth-watering sensation.
  • You may notice it as tartness, similar to the effect of citrus or green apple.
  • Acidity is not the same as sweetness, tannin, sourness or pH.
  • A high-acid wine is not automatically better, and a low-acid wine is not automatically faulty.
  • Grape variety, ripeness, climate, harvest timing, winemaking and ageing can all influence acidity.
  • You can recognise acidity by noticing how much your mouth waters and how fresh the wine feels.

Quick Answer

Acidity in wine is the presence and effect of naturally occurring acids that make wine feel fresh, lively and mouth-watering. It is one of wine’s main structural components, working alongside sweetness, alcohol, tannin, fruit flavour and body.

When acidity is noticeable, a wine may taste crisp, bright or energetic. It can create a tart sensation, rather like biting into a green apple or tasting citrus. A wine with less noticeable acidity may feel softer, rounder or broader.

Neither style is automatically better. Acidity needs to make sense in the wine as a whole. It can keep a sweet wine from seeming heavy, give a rich white wine freshness, or help a red wine feel energetic rather than broad. But too much, too little or poorly integrated acidity can make a wine seem sharp, flat or unbalanced.

How Does Acidity Feel?

Acidity is easiest to recognise on the palate rather than by looking at the wine. Take a sip and notice whether your mouth begins to water. Does the wine feel refreshing and lively, or soft and broad? Does that freshness continue into the finish?

Wine writers may describe acidity as:

  • High-acid: acidity is clearly prominent.
  • Low-acid: acidity is less noticeable.
  • Crisp: fresh and lively, often with noticeable acidity.
  • Soft: rounder or less obviously acidic.
  • Flabby: an informal term for wine that seems to lack freshness or structural energy.

These are descriptions, not quality scores. A crisp Riesling and a soft, full-bodied red can both be enjoyable when their acidity suits the style.

Acidity can also feel like tension or energy. It gives the wine shape and movement rather than allowing it to seem static. However, the sensation is affected by temperature, sweetness, alcohol, tannin, fruit flavour and the person tasting it. Mouth-watering is a useful clue, not a laboratory test.

Acidity Is Not the Same as Sourness or Sharpness

Acidity can contribute to sourness or tartness, but the words do not mean exactly the same thing.

  • Acidity is the chemical property and the related sensory quality.
  • Sourness is a taste perception that may be caused by acids.
  • Tartness is a common way of describing the fresh, sour-edged sensation acidity can create.
  • Sharpness is an informal description. It may mean that the acidity feels especially prominent or aggressive, but it is not a precise technical term.

A wine can have noticeable acidity without tasting unpleasantly sour. The final impression depends on how much acidity is present, the types of acids involved and how the acidity interacts with the wine’s other components.

Acidity, pH and Titratable Acidity

Wine professionals use measurements to describe acidity, but those measurements are not identical to what you experience while drinking.

pH is a chemical measurement related to the strength or activity of acidity in a solution. A lower pH indicates a more acidic chemical environment, but it does not perfectly predict how sour or refreshing a wine will taste.

Titratable acidity, often shortened to TA, estimates the amount of acid that can react during a laboratory titration. It is commonly expressed as grams per litre of tartaric-acid equivalent.

Perceived acidity is the experience of tasting the wine: how fresh, tart, lively or mouth-watering it feels.

These measurements are related, but they are not interchangeable. Sweetness, alcohol, tannin, flavour concentration and texture can all change how acidity is perceived. This is why a number on a laboratory report cannot tell you exactly how a wine will feel in your mouth.

How Acidity Interacts with Other Parts of Wine

Acidity does not work alone.

Sweetness

Sweetness can make acidity seem less prominent, while acidity can stop a sweet wine from feeling heavy or cloying. Sweetness does not remove acidity; it changes how the two are experienced together.

A dry wine with noticeable acidity may seem particularly crisp. A sweet wine with both richness and refreshing acidity can feel balanced rather than syrupy.

Alcohol and Body

Alcohol and body can make a wine feel broader, warmer or rounder, which may make its acidity seem less obvious. A lighter-bodied wine with similar acidity may feel brisker.

This is not a fixed rule. The overall impression depends on the wine’s fruit, texture and structure.

Tannin

Tannin is a separate sensation. It can feel drying, grippy or slightly rough, especially in red wine. Acidity feels more tart, fresh and mouth-watering.

The two can appear together. A red wine with firm acidity and tannin may feel structured and energetic, while a wine with noticeable tannin but less acidity may feel drying without feeling particularly fresh.

Fruit Flavour

Fruit character changes how acidity is interpreted. Citrus, green apple and some red-fruit flavours may reinforce a fresh impression. Riper tropical or dark-fruit flavours can make a wine seem fuller, even when it still has substantial acidity.

Ripe fruit does not automatically mean low acidity.

What Influences Acidity?

Several factors shape the acidity of a finished wine.

Grape variety and ripeness

Different grape varieties have different natural acid profiles and differ in how well they retain acidity as they ripen. As grapes ripen, sugar generally increases while some acids decline, although the pattern varies with variety and growing conditions.

Harvest timing is therefore a decision about the relationship between sugar, acidity, flavour and texture. An early harvest is not automatically better, and a late harvest is not automatically low-acid.

Climate and vineyard conditions

Cooler conditions can help grapes retain acidity, while warmer conditions may accelerate ripening and acid loss. But climate does not determine a wine’s acidity by itself. Variety, site, vintage, water availability, vineyard management, harvest decisions and winemaking also matter.

This is one reason Australian wine cannot be described as having one acidity profile. The country’s regions, elevations, varieties and climates produce a wide range of styles.

Fermentation and malolactic fermentation

Some wines undergo malolactic fermentation, in which sharper-tasting malic acid is converted into softer-tasting lactic acid. This can make a wine feel rounder, but it does not simply remove all acidity.

The choice to use or avoid this process is one of many winemaking decisions that can affect texture and the way acidity is perceived. For more on the wider winemaking process, see How wine is made.

Ageing

Acidity can help a wine retain freshness as it develops, but acidity alone does not guarantee that a wine will improve or last for a long time. Ageing also depends on the wine’s other components, its style, storage and bottle condition.

How Acidity Appears in Different Wines

Acidity can be present in white, red, rosé and sparkling wine.

  • White wine: acidity may be especially easy to notice because white wine usually has little tannin and is often served chilled. It can make the wine feel crisp or citrusy.
  • Red wine: acidity helps shape the relationship between fruit, alcohol and tannin. It may be less obvious in a full-bodied or strongly tannic wine.
  • Rosé: acidity can support a fresh, dry style and help fruit flavours feel light rather than heavy.
  • Sparkling wine: acidity works with carbon dioxide and sweetness to create freshness and lift.

These are broad observations, not rules for every bottle.

Australian Examples

Australian Riesling is often a clear example of acidity supporting vibrant fruit and freshness. Adelaide Hills wines are also often described in terms such as lively and crisp. Southern Highlands Sauvignon Blanc can show good acidity.

These descriptions are tendencies, not guarantees. Australian wine includes everything from bright, high-acid styles to softer, fuller wines with less prominent acidity. Warm-region wines can also retain balancing acidity, depending on variety, site, vintage, harvest timing and winemaking.

The important lesson is not to judge acidity from a region name alone. Even two wines made from the same variety in the same region can feel different.

A Simple Acidity Tasting Exercise

Try this the next time you open a bottle:

  1. Take a small sip.
  2. Let the wine move naturally across your palate.
  3. Notice whether your mouth waters.
  4. Ask whether the wine feels fresh and lively or soft and broad.
  5. Notice whether the sensation continues into the finish.
  6. If possible, compare it with another wine.
  7. Describe the acidity separately from whether you like the wine.

You do not need to identify a number. “Fresh and mouth-watering” is already a useful observation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is high acidity good in wine?

Not automatically. High acidity can make a wine refreshing and energetic, but balance depends on how it works with sweetness, alcohol, tannin, fruit and body.

Is acidity the same as sweetness?

No. Acidity creates freshness and tartness, while sweetness creates a sense of sugar or richness. They can balance each other but are separate sensations.

Is acidity the same as tannin?

No. Acidity is usually felt as freshness, tartness and mouth-watering. Tannin is more often felt as dryness, grip or astringency.

Are Australian wines high in acidity?

There is no single Australian acidity style. Different varieties, regions, climates, vintages and winemaking choices produce different profiles.

Does acidity mean a wine will age well?

Acidity can help a wine retain freshness during ageing, but it does not guarantee longevity or improvement. Other components and storage conditions matter too.

How can I tell if a wine has acidity?

Take a sip and notice whether it makes your mouth water and feels fresh or lively. Compare it with a softer wine if you can.
