What Is Zweigelt? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what Zweigelt is, where it comes from, how it tastes, where it is grown—including Australia—and what food to serve with it.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Zweigelt is an Austrian red-wine grape variety, not a wine region or a generic red-wine style.
  • It was bred in Austria in 1922 from St Laurent and Blaufränkisch.
  • It is often associated with cherry and berry fruit, spice, noticeable acidity and relatively restrained tannin.
  • Its wines can range from fresh, lighter reds to fuller, darker and oak-matured styles.
  • Austria is Zweigelt’s principal wine context, particularly Burgenland and eastern Lower Austria.
  • It is also grown commercially in Australia, including documented plantings in the Adelaide Hills.
  • Zweigelt may be made as a red, rosé, sweet wine or blend, so bottles can vary considerably.

The quick answer: what is Zweigelt?

Zweigelt is a red wine-grape variety from Austria. It was bred in 1922 by crossing St Laurent with Blaufränkisch, under the direction of Friedrich Zweigelt.

In the glass, Zweigelt is commonly associated with dark colour, cherry and berry flavours, spicy notes, refreshing acidity and moderate or relatively restrained tannin. That combination can produce a red wine that feels fruity and supple rather than heavily structured. However, Zweigelt is versatile: some examples are light and fresh for early drinking, while others are fuller, more concentrated and matured in oak.

The variety is most strongly associated with Austria, especially Burgenland and eastern Lower Austria. Australian-grown Zweigelt also exists, including documented commercial plantings in the Adelaide Hills, although the available evidence does not establish a significant national planting area or production volume.

How do you pronounce Zweigelt?

An approximate English pronunciation is “TSVY-gelt”. Pronunciation can vary between German and English speakers, so this is a useful guide rather than a strict rule.

You may also encounter the names Blauer Zweigelt, Rotburger or Zweigeltrebe. These names refer to the same grape variety. St Laurent and Blaufränkisch, by contrast, are its parent varieties—not alternative names for Zweigelt.

Where is Zweigelt grown?

Austria is Zweigelt’s defining home and principal commercial context. It is especially important in Burgenland, including areas associated with Neusiedlersee, Carnuntum and Rosalia. It is also grown in eastern Lower Austria and across other Austrian wine-growing areas.

Austrian examples may be labelled by region, producer and variety, so the grape name will not appear on every bottle. Understanding the variety can therefore help when reading an Austrian wine list or label.

Zweigelt is also grown outside Austria. For Australian wine learners, the most useful point is that it is not only an imported curiosity: commercial cultivation is documented in the Adelaide Hills. That is evidence of an Australian-grown context, but it should not be taken to mean that the region is a major national centre for Zweigelt.

What does Zweigelt taste like?

A typical starting point is cherry-led red fruit with a spicy edge. Depending on the wine, you may notice:

  • red or dark cherry;
  • berry fruit;
  • subtle herbal notes;
  • peppery or other spice-like characters;
  • refreshing acidity; and
  • a soft, supple or moderately tannic texture.

The colour is often medium-deep to deep ruby, red-purple or blue-red. Body can range from light-medium to full. Tannin—the drying, grippy sensation also found in black tea—may feel fairly restrained in a fresh, approachable wine but more noticeable in a concentrated or oak-matured example.

Fruitiness does not necessarily mean sweetness. A dry Zweigelt can smell or taste strongly of cherries and berries while containing little residual sugar. The wine’s sweetness, body, acidity and fruit character are separate parts of its overall impression.

Why can Zweigelt wines taste so different?

The grape does not determine the finished wine on its own. Climate, vineyard site, vintage, crop level and winemaking all matter.

Cooler conditions and a lighter winemaking approach may emphasise freshness, red-cherry character and a lighter body. Warmer conditions, lower yields and riper fruit can contribute to deeper colour, darker fruit and greater concentration. Oak maturation may add texture and seasoning, while stainless-steel or large-vessel ageing can preserve a more direct fruit profile.

Zweigelt can be made in several forms:

  • fresh, young red wine;
  • fuller red wine designed to develop with age;
  • rosé;
  • sweet wine; and
  • a single-variety wine or blend.

So a bottle described simply as Zweigelt may be very different from another bottle carrying the same grape name. Treat the variety as a useful expectation, not a guarantee that every wine will taste identical.

How might an Australian drinker encounter Zweigelt?

There are two main routes: Australian-grown wine and imported Austrian wine.

Australian plantings are documented in the Adelaide Hills, a cool-climate Australian wine region. Imported examples are most likely to connect you with Zweigelt’s Austrian identity, particularly wines from Burgenland and eastern Lower Austria.

It is worth keeping the two contexts separate. Australian and Austrian Zweigelt may share cherry fruit and refreshing acidity, but their expression can differ because of climate, site, vintage, yields and winemaking choices.

What food goes with Zweigelt?

For a fresh or medium-bodied red Zweigelt, try dishes with moderate richness and savoury flavours:

  • roast or grilled poultry;
  • sausages and pork;
  • mushroom or lentil dishes;
  • tomato-based pasta;
  • mild-to-medium cheeses; and
  • lighter lamb or game dishes when the wine has enough body.

Serve a lighter example slightly cool—around 13–16°C is a useful starting range—rather than at a warm room temperature. Fuller, oak-matured wines may be more comfortable towards the warmer end of that range. These are starting points, not rules: the wine’s body, alcohol and oak influence the best serving temperature.

Frequently asked questions

Is Zweigelt a red or white grape?

Zweigelt is principally a red wine grape. It is commonly made into red wine, but rosé and sweet styles are also documented.

Is Zweigelt the same as Blaufränkisch?

No. Blaufränkisch is one of Zweigelt’s parents. St Laurent is the other. Zweigelt is a separate, named variety.

Is Zweigelt always light-bodied?

No. Fresh, early-drinking examples can be relatively light and supple, while lower-yielding, riper or oak-matured wines can be fuller and more concentrated.

Is Zweigelt sweet?

Not necessarily. Many Zweigelt wines are dry. Cherry and berry aromas describe fruit character, not automatically sugar or sweetness.

Is Zweigelt grown in Australia?

Yes. Commercial cultivation is documented in Australia, including the Adelaide Hills. Public evidence supplied for this article does not establish a reliable national planting or production figure.

Is Zweigelt suitable for ageing?

Some fuller, well-made examples may develop with age, but Zweigelt is not universally an ageing wine. Fresh examples are often intended to be enjoyed earlier.
