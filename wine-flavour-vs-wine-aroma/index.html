Wine flavour vs wine aroma: what’s the difference?

Wine aroma is smell, while flavour combines aroma, taste and mouthfeel. Learn about orthonasal and retronasal aroma, fruitiness versus sweetness and tasting-note language.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Aroma is the smell-related part of wine perception.
  • Taste is the narrower set of sensations detected by taste receptors, including sweetness, sourness and bitterness.
  • Flavour is broader: it combines aroma, taste and sensations such as texture, tannin and alcohol warmth.
  • Smelling wine through the glass uses one route; smelling it from the back of the mouth while drinking uses another.
  • A fruity wine is not necessarily sweet.
  • Wine tasting notes use words such as peach, lemon, spice and flowers as perceived comparisons—not claims that those ingredients were added.
  • If you cannot smell the wine properly, its flavour may seem less detailed, but taste and mouthfeel remain.

Quick answer: is flavour the same as aroma?

No. Aroma is smell; flavour is the wider experience of drinking the wine.

When you smell wine in a glass, you are noticing its orthonasal aroma—smell entering through your nostrils. When you sip, volatile compounds travel from the mouth towards the smell-sensing area through the back of the throat. This is called retronasal aroma, or smell perceived during tasting.

Your brain combines those smells with taste sensations and mouthfeel. The result is what you experience as flavour.

A simple way to remember it is:

Aroma = smell-related perception
Taste = gustatory sensations
Flavour = aroma + taste + mouth sensations

That is why “flavour” is not simply another word for what happens on the tongue.

Aroma, taste, flavour and mouthfeel

Term What it means A wine example
Aroma Smell-related perception, before or during drinking A smell that reminds you of citrus or herbs
Taste Sensations detected by taste receptors Sweetness, sourness or bitterness
Flavour The combined experience of aroma, taste and mouth sensations A wine that seems citrusy, fresh and textured
Mouthfeel Physical, tactile and warming sensations Tannin-related dryness, body or alcohol warmth

Acidity can create a fresh, sour or mouth-watering impression. Sweetness is a taste sensation. Tannin can create grip or dryness. Alcohol may contribute warmth, while body and texture affect how substantial the wine feels. These sensations contribute to the overall flavour experience, but they are not aromas.

Orthonasal and retronasal aroma

The technical terms sound complicated, but the difference is practical.

Orthonasal aroma is what you smell through your nostrils when you bring a glass to your nose. Swirling the wine can help release volatile compounds, making its smell easier to notice.

Retronasal aroma is smell reaching the olfactory system from the mouth, through the back of the throat, while you taste. You do not need to sniff the glass for this to happen. Gently breathing out through your nose while wine is in your mouth can make this part of the experience more noticeable.

The two routes are related, but they are not identical experiences. The delivery route and tasting context can change what you notice. Both contribute to flavour.

This is also why a wine may seem to have more character after you sip it than it did when you first smelled it.

Why aroma affects flavour

During tasting, the brain receives information from several systems at once:

  • smell from the mouth;
  • taste sensations;
  • texture and movement in the mouth;
  • temperature;
  • sensations such as tannin, warmth or irritation.

It combines these signals into a single experience. If aroma perception is reduced, the wine may seem less recognisable or less detailed, even though its acidity, sweetness, bitterness, tannin and texture are still present.

This is the practical reason aroma affects perceived flavour. It does not mean that aroma is the whole of flavour, or that holding your nose removes every sensation.

Fruitiness is not sweetness

A common beginner confusion is treating “fruity” as another word for “sweet”.

Fruitiness is an aroma or flavour impression that reminds you of fruit. Sweetness is a taste sensation. They can occur together, but they are not the same thing.

A dry wine can smell of peach, pear, raspberry or citrus. That does not mean it contains those fruits or must contain noticeable sugar. Likewise, a sweet wine is not automatically strongly fruity.

Australian tasting notes commonly use fruit, herb, spice and floral descriptors in this comparative way. If a note mentions lemon, apple or flowers, it is usually communicating what the wine reminds the taster of—not suggesting that the ingredient was added.

A simple tasting exercise

Try this with a small sample of wine:

  1. Smell the wine normally and note one or two impressions.
  2. Take a small sip.
  3. Let it move gently around your mouth.
  4. Breathe out softly through your nose while tasting.
  5. Notice whether the impression changes from the smell of the glass.
  6. If comfortable, compare this with a sip while gently holding your nose closed, then release your nose and breathe normally.

The nose-holding comparison may make the wine seem less aromatic or less detailed. It will not remove sweetness, acidity, tannin, texture or alcohol sensations.

This is an informal illustration, not a laboratory test. Individual results vary with nasal congestion, temperature, glassware, expectation and personal sensitivity. Do not forcefully inhale or hold your breath.

Why tasting notes sometimes blur the terms

In careful sensory language, aroma, taste, flavour and mouthfeel are distinct. In everyday wine writing, however, a note may say a wine has “flavours of peach and citrus”. Those impressions are largely aroma-related, especially when perceived retronasally.

This is common shorthand rather than necessarily a contradiction. Wine professionals do not always use the terms in exactly the same way. The useful lesson is to understand the distinction while recognising how wine language works in practice.

When writing your own notes, you can be more precise:

  • “I smell citrus” describes orthonasal aroma.
  • “The wine tastes sour and fresh” describes taste and acidity-related sensation.
  • “It has citrus-like flavour” describes the combined impression in the mouth.
  • “It feels light-bodied and drying” describes mouthfeel.

The goal is not to find the one perfect word. It is to separate what you smell, what you taste and how the wine feels—then describe the whole experience.

The takeaway

Wine aroma is smell-related perception. Taste covers sensations such as sweetness, sourness and bitterness. Flavour is the broader experience created when aroma, taste and mouth sensations come together.

So when someone says a wine has “flavours of berries”, they are usually describing a berry-like sensory impression—not claiming the wine is sweet or contains berries.
