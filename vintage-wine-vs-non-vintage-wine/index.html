Vintage wine vs non-vintage wine: what’s the difference?

Understand what vintage and non-vintage mean, how harvest years differ from bottling dates, and how to read Australian and sparkling-wine labels.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Vintage usually identifies the year the grapes were harvested.
  • Non-vintage generally means the wine combines wines from more than one harvest year.
  • A vintage year is not the bottling date, release date or a guarantee that the wine is ready to drink.
  • Non-vintage wine is not automatically cheaper, younger or lower quality.
  • Vintage wines may show more year-to-year variation, but vintage alone cannot predict quality.
  • Australian wine labels and overseas labels may follow different rules and conventions.
  • In sparkling wine, non-vintage blending is commonly used to maintain a recognisable house style.

Quick answer

The difference between vintage and non-vintage wine is mainly about harvest-year information.

A vintage wine carries a year that identifies the harvest year of the grapes used to make it. A non-vintage wine generally combines wines from two or more harvest years and does not display one harvest year as its vintage.

Neither is automatically better. Vintage tells you something about the wine’s harvest year; non-vintage often tells you that the winemaker has blended wines to create a consistent style across different years. Grape variety, region, winemaking, storage and the wine’s style still matter.

This distinction is especially useful with sparkling wine, where non-vintage blending is a long-established way to maintain a recognisable house style.

What does “vintage” mean in wine?

In plain English, a wine’s vintage is the year in which its grapes were harvested.

For Australian wine, the legal definition relates to the year the grapes were harvested—not the year the wine was bottled or released. Australian rules also include a specific treatment for grapes harvested between 1 September and 31 December: for the statutory vintage definition, they are treated as belonging to the following calendar year.

A vintage year therefore gives you a clue about the growing season represented in the bottle. It does not tell you:

  • when the wine was bottled;
  • when it was released for sale;
  • how long it has been stored;
  • whether it is ready to drink; or
  • whether it will improve with age.

A four-digit year can sometimes appear elsewhere on a label for another reason, such as a winery’s establishment or a vineyard’s planting date. Look at the wording and position of the date rather than assuming every number is a vintage.

What does “non-vintage” mean?

Non-vintage, often shortened to NV, generally means a wine made by blending wines from more than one harvest year.

The purpose is often consistency. By combining wines from different years, a winemaker can aim for a familiar house style even when growing conditions change from one harvest to the next.

That does not mean the wine is made from poor fruit, is mass-produced, is artificially made or is necessarily young. A non-vintage wine can contain older reserve wine and may spend significant time maturing before release.

There is one important label-reading caution: a wine without a visible year is not automatically confirmed to be a non-vintage blend. It may simply be a wine for which no vintage claim has been made, or it may follow a category-specific labelling convention.

Vintage year, bottling date, release date and age

These terms are easy to mix up:

  • Vintage year: the year the grapes were harvested.
  • Bottling date: when the wine was put into its final bottle.
  • Release date: when the wine was made available for sale.
  • Wine age: the time that has passed since a particular reference point, which should be made clear in context.

A wine may be harvested in one year, matured for some time, bottled later and released later again. The vintage year does not reveal the full timeline.

It also does not answer whether the wine improves with age. That is a separate question involving the wine’s structure, style, storage and condition. For that broader topic, see Does Wine Improve With Age?.

Why can vintages taste different?

Harvest conditions vary. Temperature, rainfall, frost, disease pressure, harvest timing and grape ripeness can all influence the grapes that reach the winery. Vineyard site, variety, picking decisions, winemaking and maturation also shape the finished wine.

That is why wines from different vintages can differ in flavour, texture, freshness or structure.

But vintage variation is not a simple quality scoreboard. A warm year is not automatically better, and a cool year is not automatically worse. A vintage year is useful context, not a complete prediction of what you will enjoy.

Non-vintage blending can soften the effect of one unusual harvest, but it does not guarantee that the wine will taste exactly the same every year.

How Australian wine labels work

For Australian wine, vintage claims are regulated and must be supported by auditable records under the Label Integrity Program. Australian rules allow a wine to be described as one vintage when at least 850 millilitres per litre—85%—comes from that vintage under the applicable rule.

That means Australian wine is not generally required to contain 100% grapes from the stated year for a vintage claim to be made.

When reading a bottle-shop label or restaurant list, ask:

  1. Is the year presented as the wine’s vintage?
  2. Could another date refer to the winery, vineyard, bottling or lot?
  3. Is the wine labelled “NV” or “non-vintage”?
  4. Does the category or country of origin have its own conventions?

Australian sparkling producers may use vintage and non-vintage conventions, but there is no reason to assume every producer uses an identical system.

Imported wines sold in Australia may also reflect the rules and traditions of their country, region or appellation of origin. Australian labelling rules should not be transferred automatically to every imported wine.

For a broader guide to label information, see How to Read a Wine Label.

Vintage and non-vintage sparkling wine

Sparkling wine makes the difference particularly clear.

Non-vintage sparkling wine commonly blends wines from different harvest years. This helps the producer aim for a recognisable style across releases.

Champagne is a useful example, but its rules are specific to Champagne. Comité Champagne explains that vintage Champagne is made exclusively from the stated harvest year, while non-vintage Champagne blends wines from different years.

That does not mean all Australian sparkling wine follows Champagne’s rules. It is better to treat Champagne as a clear example of one appellation’s system, not as a universal definition.

A further point: maturation is separate from vintage status. A non-vintage sparkling wine can mature before release, while a vintage sparkling wine is not defined simply by being old.

You can learn more about the category in What Is Sparkling Wine? and What Is Traditional-Method Sparkling Wine?.

Which should you choose?

Choose based on what you want from the wine, not on the assumption that vintage is superior.

  • Choose a vintage wine if you are curious about a particular harvest year or want to compare how different years affect a style.
  • Choose a non-vintage wine if you value a familiar, consistent style or want the category’s usual blend-based character.
  • For sparkling wine, consider whether you prefer the consistency of a non-vintage blend or the more harvest-specific identity of a declared vintage style.
  • If you are deciding whether a bottle is ready to drink, do not rely on the vintage year alone.

The most useful question is not “Which is better?” but “What does this label tell me, and does that suit what I want to drink?”

Frequently asked questions

Is vintage wine always better than non-vintage?

No. Vintage and non-vintage describe harvest-year composition and labelling. They are not universal quality rankings.

Does non-vintage mean the wine is young?

No. Non-vintage wine may include older wines and may mature before release.

Does the vintage year show when the wine was bottled?

No. It generally identifies the grape-harvest year. Bottling and release dates are separate pieces of information.

What does NV mean?

NV commonly means non-vintage, particularly in sparkling-wine contexts. Its exact use should be understood in the context of the wine category and jurisdiction.

Is a wine with no year automatically non-vintage?

No. The absence of a visible year does not by itself prove the exact composition or legal status of the wine.

Is vintage wine the same as vintage fortified wine?

Not necessarily. “Vintage fortified wine” is a distinct category with its own context. See What Is Vintage Fortified Wine?.

Does vintage tell me whether a wine will age well?

No. Age-worthiness depends on more than the harvest year. See Does Wine Improve With Age?.
