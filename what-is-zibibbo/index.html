What is Zibibbo?

Zibibbo is an aromatic white wine grape associated with Muscat of Alexandria and Pantelleria. Learn how its wines can range from dry to sweet, sparkling and fortified.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Zibibbo is a white wine grape.
  • In Italian wine usage, it is commonly associated with Muscat of Alexandria.
  • It is strongly linked with Sicily, especially the island of Pantelleria.
  • Zibibbo can make dry, sweet, sparkling and fortified wines.
  • The name does not tell you whether a wine is sweet, fizzy, strong or from Pantelleria.
  • Australian drinkers may encounter Zibibbo as a grape name on a bottle, wine list or retailer page.

Quick answer: what is Zibibbo?

Zibibbo is a white wine-grape variety, commonly known in Italian contexts as the grape internationally called Muscat of Alexandria. It is particularly associated with Sicily and Pantelleria, a volcanic island off Sicily’s coast.

Zibibbo is an aromatic grape, so wines made from it may show floral, fresh-grape, citrus or ripe-fruit notes. But the finished wine can vary considerably. Depending on where and how it is made, Zibibbo can be dry and still, sweet and rich, sparkling, lightly sparkling or fortified.

That means “Zibibbo” tells you chiefly about the grape—not necessarily the wine’s sweetness, alcohol level, texture, bubbles, production method or place of origin.

For an Australian drinker, the best approach is to read the grape name alongside the region, alcohol statement, producer description and any words such as secco (dry), passito (made from dried grapes), spumante (sparkling) or liquoroso (fortified).

Is Zibibbo the same as Muscat of Alexandria?

Zibibbo is commonly used in Italy, particularly Sicily, for the grape known internationally as Muscat of Alexandria. This is why you may see the names discussed together.

The relationship is not quite as simple as treating every bottle labelled Muscat, Moscato or Zibibbo as the same wine. Grape names can change with language, region and wine regulations, while winemaking choices can produce very different results from the same variety.

Formal European material for the Pantelleria designation records the grape as “Zibibbo B – Moscatellone”. Other Italian regional sources describe it as Moscato di Alessandria, with Zibibbo as a local name. For a beginner, the useful conclusion is that these names are closely connected in Italian wine—but the label’s full wording still matters.

What does Zibibbo wine taste like?

Zibibbo belongs to the aromatic Muscat family. Its wines may have a noticeably fragrant character, with descriptions such as:

  • flowers;
  • fresh grapes;
  • citrus;
  • ripe orchard or tropical fruit; and
  • spice in some examples.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. A dry Zibibbo made from earlier-picked grapes may feel very different from a sweet wine made from grapes that have been dried. Fermentation, skin contact, ageing, climate and the producer’s choices also influence the final wine.

It is therefore risky to assign every Zibibbo wine the same body, acidity or alcohol level. The variety can produce lighter, fresher wines as well as richer, sweeter or more concentrated expressions.

Is Zibibbo sweet?

Not necessarily. Zibibbo can be made into dry wine as well as sweet wine.

Pantelleria is especially known for styles made from grapes that are dried on the vine or after picking. This process, used for passito wines, concentrates the grapes and can produce a rich, sweet wine. The island is also associated with dry white wines, sparkling expressions and fortified wines.

Look for clues beyond the grape name:

  • Secco means dry.
  • Passito indicates a dried-grape style.
  • Spumante means sparkling.
  • Frizzante generally indicates lightly sparkling wine.
  • Liquoroso is used for a fortified style in the relevant regional context.

If the label simply says “Zibibbo”, do not assume that the wine is sweet.

Why is Zibibbo associated with Pantelleria?

Pantelleria is the most recognisable place associated with Zibibbo. The island’s wine identity is closely tied to the grape and to traditional bush-trained vines, known locally as alberello.

Pantelleria is a place and a formal wine-designation context; Zibibbo is the grape. They are related, but they are not interchangeable. A wine made from Zibibbo does not have to come from Pantelleria, and a bottle labelled Zibibbo elsewhere should not automatically be treated as Pantelleria wine.

On a qualifying Pantelleria label, “Zibibbo” may appear as part of a traditional or formal designation. In that setting, a word such as secco can provide the sweetness information that the grape name does not.

How might Australians encounter Zibibbo?

Australian drinkers may see Zibibbo used as a grape reference on domestic wine labels, wine lists, retailer pages or trade material. Australian wine communications have included Zibibbo from regions such as the Riverland and McLaren Vale, including dry, sparkling, blended and alternative-method examples.

That does not mean there is one standard Australian Zibibbo style. Australian producers can use the grape in different ways, just as producers elsewhere can.

Wine Australia explains that variety names on Australian labels are subject to recognition and substantiation requirements when a variety claim is made. Variety claims themselves are optional. In practical terms, a Zibibbo label can be useful information, but it is only one part of the story.

Check:

  1. the stated region;
  2. the alcohol level;
  3. whether the wine is still or sparkling;
  4. any sweetness or production wording; and
  5. the producer’s description of the wine.

The word “Zibibbo” alone does not establish residual sugar, strength, bubbles, skin contact, fortification or origin.

What food suits Zibibbo?

The food match depends on the style in the glass.

A dry, aromatic Zibibbo may work well as a refreshing partner for lighter dishes, while a sweeter or passito version can suit fruit-based desserts or salty foods where sweetness provides contrast. Sparkling versions can bring freshness and lift, while richer or fortified examples are better approached as after-dinner wines or alongside intense flavours.

There is no single pairing rule for Zibibbo because there is no single Zibibbo style. Match the food to the wine’s actual sweetness, weight and texture—not just the grape name.

Frequently asked questions

Is Zibibbo a grape or a wine?

It is a grape variety. The name can also appear in names for wines made from that grape, particularly in Italian and Pantelleria contexts.

Is Zibibbo the same as Moscato?

The names are related in some Italian contexts, and Zibibbo is commonly associated with Muscat of Alexandria. But “Moscato” can be used in different regional and stylistic contexts, so the full label still needs to be read.

Is Zibibbo always from Italy?

No. It is strongly associated with Sicily and Pantelleria, but Zibibbo is also grown and discussed in Australian wine contexts.

Is Zibibbo always sweet?

No. It can be dry, sweet, sparkling or fortified. The grape name alone does not determine sweetness.

What should I expect from a Zibibbo bottle?

Expect an aromatic grape, but not one fixed wine style. Check the label and description for sweetness, bubbles, alcohol, origin and production method.
