How to start a wine collection

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

A wine collection does not need to fill a cellar, contain rare bottles or cost a fortune. It can be a small, organised group of wines kept for future drinking, learning, entertaining or special occasions.

The best way to start is to decide what you want the collection to do, buy mostly wines you enjoy, keep any ageing plans realistic, and store the bottles in suitable conditions.

Highlights

  • Start with your purpose, available space and drinking habits—not a fixed number of bottles.
  • Buy wines you genuinely expect to drink, rather than collecting for appearances.
  • Not every wine improves with age, and no single factor guarantees that a wine will develop well.
  • Stable, cool, dark and low-vibration storage matters more than having an impressive cellar.
  • A few bottles of the same wine can make ageing easier to understand.
  • Keep a simple record so bottles do not disappear at the back of a cupboard.
  • “Drink now”, “hold” and “check again” are useful estimates, not promises.
  • Collecting for enjoyment is different from investing.

Quick answer: how do you start?

Begin by choosing a purpose. You might want wines for weeknight drinking, future dinners, birthdays, entertaining, learning or a mixture of these. Your purpose will determine what you buy and how long you need to keep it.

Next, set a manageable size and spending limit. There is no universal correct number of bottles or Australian dollar amount. A collection that fits your storage space and drinking habits is more useful than one that becomes expensive or difficult to manage.

Buy mostly wines you already like, then add a smaller number of bottles that may develop with age. Before keeping a wine for years, look for credible drinking-window guidance and consider the wine’s style, vintage, closure, storage history and structure.

Finally, record what you own and store it somewhere stable, cool, dark and protected from movement. Review the bottles regularly and adjust your plans as you learn more about them.

Decide what your collection is for

A collection is simply an organised supply of bottles retained for later use. It might be as small as a few bottles in a cupboard or wine fridge.

Purpose is more helpful than prestige. A collection for entertaining may need a range of styles and bottles suitable for drinking soon. A learning collection might include two or three bottles of the same wine or style, allowing you to compare them at different points. A collection for celebrations may focus on bottles you want to open for particular occasions.

You do not need to choose only one purpose. The useful question is: What do I want these bottles to do for me?

Set a realistic size and budget

Start with the space you actually have, not the cellar you imagine having one day. Count how many bottles can fit without stacking them where they will be forgotten, overheated or difficult to retrieve.

Your expected drinking rate matters too. If you usually open a bottle every fortnight, buying faster than you drink can quickly create a storage problem. A smaller collection that turns over regularly is often easier to enjoy and learn from.

Set a limit for each buying occasion or month if that helps. Leave room for everyday bottles as well as wines you intend to keep. Avoid treating price as a shortcut to quality or ageability: a more expensive bottle is not automatically more suitable for long-term cellaring.

Choose wines for drinking first

Your collection should reflect your preferences. If you enjoy fresh whites, lighter reds, sparkling wine or fortified wine, those preferences are a sensible starting point. You do not need to collect a style simply because other people consider it important.

It can be useful to create three broad groups:

  • Drink soon: bottles bought for ordinary meals, casual occasions or immediate curiosity.
  • Hold: wines with credible guidance suggesting they may benefit from more time.
  • Explore: bottles chosen to teach you something new about a grape, style, region or vintage.

This approach creates variety without turning the collection into a checklist. It also prevents every bottle being treated as a long-term project.

Understand which wines can age

Not every wine improves with age. Wine Education and Spirit Trust guidance describes ageworthy wines as a relatively small proportion of all wines. Wines that age well often have a combination of flavour intensity and preserving features such as acidity, tannin or sweetness.

These factors are clues, not a formula. Some white wines can age without tannin, while some red wines with relatively modest tannin can also develop well. Alcohol, concentration and balance may matter too, but none guarantees a particular result.

Style and intended drinking period are important. A wine designed to be fresh and youthful may not be made for extended cellaring. Producer guidance and credible drinking-window information are usually more useful than assuming that a grape variety, region, price or score tells you everything.

Vintage can affect a wine’s development, but it does not create a universal drinking date. Storage history and provenance—the wine’s history of storage and ownership—also matter. For a broader explanation, read does wine improve with age? and what makes wine ageworthy.

Consider the closure without making it a ranking

Closure is worth recording, but cork and screwcap are not a simple “good versus bad” choice.

Wine Australia’s ageing guidance recommends storing cork-closed bottles on their side and says screwcap bottles can stand upright. That does not mean screwcaps are only for young wines, or that every cork-closed bottle will age successfully. Treat the closure as one part of the storage and ageability picture.

When buying a bottle to keep, look for the producer’s information, the vintage where present, the wine’s style and any stated drinking guidance. Record the exact label details rather than relying on memory. How to read a wine label can help with this.

Store the bottles somewhere stable

For long-term cellaring, Wine Australia’s educator guide gives approximately 13–16°C, about 70% relative humidity, darkness and minimal movement as useful conditions. These figures are long-term cellaring guidance, not a requirement to measure every household cupboard or to treat every short-term bottle as a technical project.

The practical principle is consistency. Wine should be protected from heat, light, vibration and large temperature changes. A controlled wine fridge can help when your home does not provide a suitable environment, but a wine fridge is not automatically necessary for every small collection.

Australian homes experience very different climates. A garage, shed, kitchen or roof space may be unsuitable if it becomes hot, bright or highly variable. Do not assume that an enclosed space is a cellar simply because it is indoors.

Keep a simple inventory

A notebook, spreadsheet or phone app is enough. Record:

  • producer and wine name;
  • grape variety or style;
  • vintage, if shown;
  • quantity;
  • purchase date;
  • price paid;
  • storage location; and
  • intended drinking period.

You can also add tasting notes, but do not let record-keeping become a barrier to opening a bottle. The point is to make the collection visible and useful.

Buying two or three bottles of one wine can be an enjoyable learning experiment. You might open one now, keep another for later and compare the differences. This is optional, not a test of serious collecting.

Review the collection and its drinking windows

A drinking window is an estimate of when a wine may show well. It is not an expiry date or a guarantee of improvement.

Use simple labels such as “drink now”, “hold” and “check again”. If a wine has been stored somewhere warm or inconsistent, be more cautious about relying on a general drinking-window note. Review the collection periodically, especially before buying more bottles.

When you open a bottle marked “check again”, taste it with curiosity rather than expecting a particular result. Wine can develop in ways you enjoy—or in ways that show it should have been opened earlier. When to drink a wine and how to tell if wine is ready to drink cover those decisions in more detail.

Collect for enjoyment, not speculation

Wine Australia identifies enjoyment, experimentation and life events as reasons people cellar wine, while treating investment as a separate motive. Keeping wine can be rewarding, but this guide does not treat a collection as a financial investment.

Do not assume that rarity, high price or a strong score guarantees future value or better ageing. A bottle that you enjoy opening is already doing the job your collection was created to do.

One final Australian practical note: a bottle’s label states its number of standard drinks, and one Australian standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol. A bottle or glass is not a fixed measure of standard drinks because alcohol strength and serving size vary. Use the label when planning how quickly to work through a collection.

Frequently asked questions

How many bottles should a beginner collect?

There is no universal target. Start with the number that fits your available storage and normal drinking habits. You can expand when the collection remains easy to find, record and enjoy.

Does expensive wine age better?

Not automatically. Ageability depends on the wine, its structure, style, vintage, closure and storage, among other factors. Price alone is not a reliable drinking-window guide.

Do all wines improve with age?

No. Some wines are intended to be enjoyed young, while others may develop positively under suitable conditions. Even ageworthy wine does not have a guaranteed peak date.

Is a wine fridge necessary?

Not for every collection. It can provide more controlled conditions, but the important question is whether your existing storage is stable, dark, cool enough for its purpose and protected from heat and movement.

Should every bottle be stored on its side?

No. Wine Australia’s guidance distinguishes between closures: cork-closed bottles are recommended on their side, while screwcap bottles can stand upright.

Is a wine collection an investment?

It can be collected for many reasons, including drinking and learning. This guide does not provide investment or financial-return advice. For most beginners, enjoyment is the clearest place to start.
