How to transport wine safely

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Stop bottles moving before you start driving.
  • Separate glass bottles with dividers, sleeves or purpose-made wine packaging.
  • Keep wine out of prolonged heat, freezing conditions and direct sunlight.
  • Never leave loose bottles where they could injure someone during sudden braking.
  • Sparkling wine needs secure cushioning, but it does not have one universal transport orientation.
  • Airline, postal and courier rules vary, so check them before you travel or ship.
  • Inspect every bottle when it arrives.

Quick answer

The safest way to transport wine is to pack each sealed bottle so it cannot knock against another bottle or move freely inside a box. Use a rigid wine carton with dividers, individual bottle sleeves or a purpose-made wine shipper. Fill empty spaces with cushioning, then close and secure the outer packaging.

For a car trip, keep the packed bottles stable and low where practical. Prevent the carton from sliding, and do not leave loose glass on a seat, in a footwell or anywhere it could become a hazard during sudden braking.

Temperature matters too. Avoid prolonged exposure to heat, freezing conditions and direct sunlight. A hot parked car is not a wine-storage solution, particularly during an Australian summer or on a long road journey. Heat damage can be difficult to detect and cannot be reliably reversed.

For flights, Australia Post or a courier, check the current rules for that specific airline, carrier, route and destination. Personal transport and commercial shipment are not governed by exactly the same conditions.

How to pack wine bottles

The main packing goal is simple: prevent movement and absorb impacts.

For one or two bottles, place them in a rigid container rather than relying on a soft shopping bag. Use a bottle sleeve, the original cardboard divider or other protective material that keeps the bottles apart. Make sure closures and capsules are not pressing against hard objects.

For several bottles, use a wine carton with internal dividers or a purpose-made wine shipper. Each bottle should have its own space. Fill gaps so the bottles cannot rattle or shift, but do not pack so tightly that the glass is under unnecessary pressure.

Give the box a gentle test before leaving. If you can hear or feel bottles moving, add more cushioning. Seal the outer carton securely with durable tape.

These principles also apply to commercial delivery. Australia Post recommends secure packaging, filled empty spaces and dedicated wine packaging for applicable wine services. Its detailed wine-shipping requirements should not be treated as a universal rule for every person carrying a bottle in a car.

Transporting wine in a car

Put the packed carton in a stable cargo area, such as the boot, where practical. Arrange surrounding luggage so it cannot crush the bottles or allow the carton to slide. Use existing cargo restraints if they are suitable.

Do not carry individual bottles loose:

  • on a seat;
  • in a door pocket;
  • in a footwell;
  • beside the driver’s pedals; or
  • anywhere they could strike an occupant during sudden braking.

The concern is not just broken wine. Loose glass can become a serious passenger hazard. General Australian vehicle-loading guidance emphasises restraining loads and preventing loose objects from moving.

Avoid stacking heavy luggage on top of wine. Drive smoothly where practical, especially if the carton contains older wine or bottles with sediment. Unnecessary jolting increases the chance of breakage, leakage or disturbed sediment.

Protect wine from heat and cold

Wine should be transported away from temperature extremes whenever possible. The Australian Wine Research Institute identifies temperature variation, heat exposure, super-chilling and freezing as conditions that can affect bottled wine.

There is no universal “safe” temperature or maximum time that applies to every bottle, vehicle and journey. Do not assume that a bottle is safe in a parked car for a particular number of hours.

On a hot day, plan to take wine home promptly after visiting a bottle shop, cellar door or event. Keep bottles shaded during the journey and avoid leaving them in a parked car. An insulated bag may slow temperature change, but it is not unlimited temperature control.

Severe cold deserves attention as well. Do not deliberately freeze wine as a transport or chilling strategy. If a bottle appears frozen, is leaking or has been pushed out at the closure, handle it carefully and let it thaw gradually in a safe place. Thawing is a handling precaution, not a way to restore wine quality.

For long Australian road trips, think about the route, weather, stops and where the vehicle will be parked. Transport is temporary movement; it is not the same as safe long-term storage. For that separate question, see How to Cellar Wine and Best temperature for storing wine.

Sparkling wine and sedimented bottles

Pack sparkling wine particularly securely. Its pressurised glass bottle format makes separation, cushioning and careful handling especially sensible. Keep bottles from knocking together and protect them from extreme heat.

There is no supported universal rule that sparkling wine must travel upright or that ordinary transport makes it explode. Choose the position that keeps the bottle most stable in its packaging.

For sedimented wine, stable packing is useful because movement can disturb the sediment. After a long or rough journey, allowing the bottle to settle before opening is a sensible precaution. There is no universal resting time, however, and settling does not repair heat damage or oxidation.

For safe opening, see How to Open Sparkling Wine Safely.

Flying with wine

Under current Australian Civil Aviation Safety Authority guidance, alcoholic drinks at or below 24% alcohol by volume—including wine and champagne—are permitted in carry-on and checked baggage categories. That does not mean every airline, route or bag will have identical conditions.

Before flying, check:

  • the airline’s current baggage size and weight limits;
  • airport security requirements;
  • whether the bottle can be carried in your particular baggage;
  • international customs and import rules; and
  • any restrictions at your destination.

Use a rigid bottle protector inside checked luggage. Clothing can provide extra padding, but it should not be the only protection. Keep bottles apart and away from the suitcase shell, and do not rely on a fragile sticker to guarantee special handling.

Sending wine by post or courier

Use a service that expressly accepts alcohol and follow its current packaging, declaration, age-verification and destination requirements. Do not assume that ordinary parcel rules apply to wine.

Australia Post’s wine and alcohol services include conditions relating to eligible customers, service types and destinations. Some circumstances involving delivery to Victoria include declaration and identification requirements. These are service-specific conditions, not a single rule for every Australian sender or courier.

Before sending wine, confirm:

  • whether the carrier accepts wine from an individual sender;
  • what packaging is required;
  • whether the destination has additional restrictions;
  • what damage or loss cover actually includes; and
  • whether international customs forms or import permissions are needed.

Carrier policies can change. Check directly before shipping rather than relying on an old label, website page or previous experience.

What to check when the wine arrives

Open the outer package carefully. Look for:

  • broken glass;
  • wet or stained packaging;
  • leakage;
  • a damaged capsule or cork;
  • a closure that appears raised or displaced; and
  • signs of unusual heat or cold exposure.

Do not taste wine from a cracked, leaking or contaminated bottle. If glass is broken, keep people and pets away and avoid handling shards with bare hands.

If a delivery appears damaged, photograph the bottle, packaging and shipping label before discarding anything. The carrier may require this information for a claim, although compensation is not guaranteed and may depend on the service and packaging used.

A bottle that looks normal may still have experienced quality loss. Heat-related damage can be difficult to diagnose from appearance alone. If a long or rough journey has disturbed sediment, let the bottle settle before opening. If you want to serve it at the right temperature afterwards, see Wine Serving Temperature Explained.

Frequently asked questions

Should wine travel upright or on its side?

Neither position is universally correct for transport. Choose the orientation that keeps the bottle most secure. Upright packing can be practical for limiting leakage concerns and keeping sediment settled, while horizontal packing can work well in a suitable divided carton.

Can I leave wine in a hot car?

Avoid it, especially for a prolonged period. A parked car can expose wine to significant heat, and there is no universal safe time that applies to every situation.

Does wine need to rest after a car trip?

Not every bottle needs a fixed resting period. Resting can be sensible after a long or rough journey, particularly for wine with sediment, but it does not reverse heat damage or oxidation.

Can I send wine through any courier?

No. Check that the specific service accepts alcohol and follow its current packaging, declaration, destination and liability rules.
