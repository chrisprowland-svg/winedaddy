How alcohol is removed from wine

Learn how alcohol is removed from fermented wine using vacuum, distillation, spinning-cone and membrane methods, and why aroma, flavour and body can change.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Alcohol is usually removed from wine after fermentation, not before it.
  • The main approaches use vacuum and distillation, spinning-cone systems, membranes such as reverse osmosis, or a combination.
  • These processes can affect more than alcohol: aroma, flavour, body, acidity and balance may change too.
  • Producers may collect and return some aroma compounds, then adjust or blend the wine.
  • Reverse osmosis is not an alcohol-only filter. Water and other small compounds may also move through the membrane.
  • In Australia, the product’s stated alcohol content and label wording are the practical guide. Terms such as “alcohol-free”, “no-alcohol” and “low-alcohol” are not automatically interchangeable.

Quick answer: how does alcohol come out of wine?

Dealcoholisation generally starts with an already fermented wine. The producer separates ethanol from that wine using a process such as vacuum distillation, a spinning-cone system, reverse osmosis or another membrane method.

The challenge is that ethanol is not the only component affected. Some aroma compounds and water may also be removed or redistributed. Depending on the equipment and the target alcohol level, producers may collect aroma fractions, manage separate liquid streams, blend the wine or make other adjustments before bottling.

So the process is not simply “filtering alcohol out”. It is closer to taking a finished wine apart in controlled stages, then putting some of it back together in a different balance.

Why remove alcohol from wine?

A producer may want to make a wine with less alcohol while retaining as much of the original wine’s character as possible. The reduction might be partial, or it might be substantial.

The starting wine matters. A wine with plenty of aroma, flavour concentration and structure may respond differently from a delicate wine when alcohol is reduced. The equipment, processing conditions and desired final alcohol level matter too.

This is why there is no single dealcoholisation recipe that produces the same result every time.

The broad production sequence

A simplified process may look like this:

  1. Start with fermented wine. In most dealcoholisation processes, the wine has already undergone alcoholic fermentation.
  2. Choose the target alcohol level. The aim may be to reduce alcohol rather than remove nearly all of it.
  3. Separate alcohol and other affected components. The method may use reduced pressure, heat-assisted separation, a membrane or a combination.
  4. Manage aroma compounds. Some systems can collect volatile aroma fractions for possible reintroduction.
  5. Recombine or adjust the wine. Separated streams may be blended or adjusted to improve balance.
  6. Analyse and label the finished product. The final measured alcohol content is important for the product label.

This is a general framework, not a universal manufacturing sequence. Producers may repeat stages or use only some of them.

The main ways alcohol is removed

Vacuum and distillation-based systems

Vacuum distillation lowers the pressure around the wine. Under reduced pressure, volatile components can be separated at lower temperatures than they would require under ordinary atmospheric boiling.

This can help remove ethanol, but other volatile compounds may travel with it. Those compounds can contribute to fruity, floral and varietal aromas. Aroma collection and later reintroduction may therefore be part of the process.

Vacuum treatment can be used for partial alcohol reduction or for a much larger reduction. The result depends on factors such as pressure, temperature, processing time and the wine itself.

Spinning-cone systems

A spinning-cone column is a staged separation system. Thin films of liquid move across spinning cones while a gas stream helps strip volatile components.

In wine production, the system can be arranged to separate aroma compounds and ethanol in stages. Some aroma fractions may be collected and returned later.

That does not mean the finished wine will taste exactly like the original. The equipment can help manage volatile compounds, but the final result still depends on the wine and the process settings.

Membrane methods, including reverse osmosis

Reverse osmosis uses pressure to move some components through a semi-permeable membrane. The material that passes through is called the permeate; the material retained by the membrane is the retentate.

Depending on the membrane and the process, the permeate may contain ethanol, water and some other small compounds. The retained wine fraction is then managed separately, and the streams may be treated and recombined.

This makes reverse osmosis different from an imaginary alcohol-only sieve. It can provide a relatively low-temperature way to adjust alcohol, but it can also affect water balance, aroma and other components.

Other membrane or hybrid approaches include nanofiltration, pervaporation and osmotic distillation. They belong to the same broad family of separation technologies but should not be assumed to work identically.

What happens to aroma and flavour?

Alcohol removal can change a wine’s volatile profile. Fruity and floral aroma compounds may be lost, redistributed or reduced along with ethanol. The extent varies with the wine, the equipment and the target alcohol level.

Producers may collect aroma fractions before or during separation and add some back later. Blending, water management, acid adjustment or other permitted rebalancing steps may also be used, depending on the product and process.

Alcohol itself contributes to the perception of warmth, weight and fullness. Reducing it can make a wine seem lighter or less rounded. Changes in concentration and the handling of other components may also affect perceived sweetness, acidity, texture and balance.

None of these outcomes is guaranteed. Alcohol removal does not always make a wine thin, sweet or acidic. It means the wine’s original balance may need to be managed again.

The finished product may therefore taste different from the starting wine, even when aroma recovery and blending are used. That is a consequence of changing the wine’s composition, not evidence that one method is automatically better than another.

How should Australian drinkers read the label?

For Australian products, check the specific label rather than relying on a front-of-pack phrase alone.

Under the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code, beverages containing at least 0.5% alcohol by volume (ABV) require alcohol-content information. Products at or below 1.15% ABV use different alcohol wording from products above that level. The Code also contains rules about standard-drink statements and representations such as “low alcohol”.

Terms including “alcohol-free”, “no-alcohol”, “de-alcoholised” and “low-alcohol” should not be treated as interchangeable descriptions of one universal alcohol level. Their use can depend on the jurisdiction, the product and the applicable rules.

The practical habit is simple: look for the stated ABV and the relevant label wording. Do not assume that a phrase such as “zero” means the product contains absolutely no alcohol unless the applicable information confirms it.

Common questions

Is alcohol removed before or after fermentation?

Generally, dealcoholisation starts with fermented wine. The wine is made first, then alcohol is reduced through separation. This is different from making a lower-alcohol wine by changing grape ripeness, fermentation conditions or other winemaking choices.

Is dealcoholised wine just filtered?

No. Ordinary clarification or filtration removes suspended particles; it is not the same as separating ethanol. Dealcoholisation uses processes designed to separate volatile or membrane-permeable components from wine.

Does reverse osmosis remove only alcohol?

No. Depending on the equipment and conditions, water, ethanol and other small compounds may move through the membrane. The separate streams may then be managed and recombined.

Does removing alcohol remove all aroma?

It can affect aroma, because some volatile compounds may be removed alongside ethanol. Aroma recovery can help, but it is not a guarantee that the final wine will match the original.

Does “alcohol-free” always mean zero alcohol?

Not necessarily. Consumer terms are not a universal substitute for checking the product’s stated ABV and applicable label rules. Read the specific label.

What is the simplest way to understand the process?

Think of dealcoholisation as controlled separation followed by rebalancing. The producer removes ethanol from an already made wine, manages other affected components, and may return selected aroma or wine fractions before bottling.
