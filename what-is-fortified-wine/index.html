Wine fundamentals

What is fortified wine?

Fortified wine is wine with distilled spirit added. Learn why some styles are sweet, others dry and what Australian fortified terms mean.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Fortified wine is wine with distilled spirit added to increase its alcohol level.
  • The spirit may be added before fermentation finishes or after it has finished.
  • Early fortification usually leaves more natural grape sugar, creating a sweeter wine.
  • Later fortification can produce a dry fortified wine.
  • Fortified wine is not the same thing as sweet wine, dessert wine or a spirit.
  • Fortified wines can be shaped by wood, oxygen, flor yeast and bottle maturation.
  • Australia has a long fortified-wine history, particularly with styles from Rutherglen.
  • Apera and Topaque are the current Australian terms associated with wines once commonly called Australian Sherry and Tokay.

Quick answer

Fortified wine is wine that has had distilled spirit added to it, increasing its alcoholic strength. The base wine is made through fermentation, like other wine, but the added spirit changes its alcohol level, texture, sweetness and potential for ageing.

The timing of that addition matters. If spirit is added while fermentation is still under way, it can stop or inhibit the yeast. Some natural grape sugar remains, so the finished wine is often sweet. If the wine is allowed to ferment until it is dry and is fortified afterwards, the result can be a dry fortified wine.

That is why fortified wine is a production category rather than a single flavour profile. It can be rich and sweet, light and dry, nutty and oxidative, or fresh and aromatic.

Fortified wine is also not a spirit. A spirit is made primarily through distillation. Fortified wine begins as fermented wine and remains wine-based, even though distilled spirit has been added.

How is fortified wine made?

Fortified wine begins with a base wine: wine made from fermented grape juice. During fermentation, yeast converts grape sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide.

At a chosen point, the winemaker adds a distilled spirit, commonly grape spirit in classic fortified-wine production. This raises the wine’s alcoholic strength. The exact process varies between styles and producers.

Early fortification: usually sweeter

When spirit is added before fermentation has finished, the increased alcohol can stop or inhibit the yeast. The wine retains more residual sugar—sugar left in the finished wine—and is generally sweeter and fuller-bodied.

Late fortification: potentially dry

When fermentation is allowed to finish first, most fermentable sugar may be converted into alcohol. The wine can then be fortified after it has become dry.

This produces a dry fortified style. Many Sherry styles, and Australian fortified wines made in a Sherry-like tradition, show that fortified wine does not have to be sweet.

Fortified wine versus table wine, sweet wine and spirits

  • Fortified wine describes a production method: distilled spirit has been added.
  • Sweet or dry describes the level of perceptible sweetness.
  • Dessert wine is a broad serving or style description, not a specific production method.
  • Spirit describes a distilled alcoholic drink, rather than wine with spirit added.
  • Table wine is a useful everyday term for ordinary still wine that has not been fortified, although its legal meaning can vary.

A fortified wine may be sweet or dry. A sweet wine may be unfortified. And a spirit may be used to fortify wine without turning the finished drink into a spirit.

The main fortified-wine families

Port

Port is a fortified wine from Portugal’s Douro region. It is commonly associated with sweet, fruit-driven wines, often red, although white and rosé examples also exist.

Because Port is a protected geographical name, it should not be treated as a casual generic name for every sweet fortified wine made in Australia.

Sherry and Apera

Sherry is associated with the Jerez region of Spain and includes both dry and sweet styles. Some styles mature under a layer of flor, a film-forming yeast, while others develop through controlled exposure to oxygen.

In Australia, Apera is the current protected term for qualifying Australian fortified wines made in a Sherry-like style.

Madeira

Madeira is known for distinctive maturation traditions involving heat and oxygen. These processes can create concentrated, nutty, dried-fruit and caramel-like flavours.

Australian Muscat and Topaque

Australia is especially well known for sweet fortified wines made from Muscat, including the historic styles associated with Rutherglen in Victoria.

Topaque is the current Australian term for qualifying fortified wines formerly associated with the Australian use of “Tokay”.

Vermouth

Vermouth is an aromatised wine: it is generally fortified and flavoured with botanicals. Its use in cocktails and botanical flavouring make it a distinct subject of its own.

How ageing changes fortified wine

Fortification is only part of the story. Maturation can change a fortified wine just as dramatically.

Some wines age in wood. Others mature with controlled oxygen exposure, contributing nutty, dried-fruit, caramel-like or savoury characters. Some styles develop under flor yeast. Others continue to develop in bottle.

  • Spirit addition changes strength and can affect sweetness.
  • Wood ageing changes texture and flavour.
  • Oxygen exposure can create oxidative character.
  • Flor ageing gives some wines a distinctive biological ageing profile.
  • Bottle ageing may allow further development after bottling.

Why fortified wine matters in Australia

Fortified wine was central to Australian wine production and drinking culture through much of the twentieth century. Wine Australia describes fortified wines as the focus of the Australian industry until the 1970s, when preferences shifted towards other wine styles.

That history is still visible in regions such as Rutherglen, which remains strongly associated with fortified wines including Muscat.

Australian terminology has changed as geographical names and traditional terms have received greater protection:

  • Apera replaced “Sherry” for qualifying Australian fortified wines.
  • Topaque replaced “Tokay” for qualifying Australian fortified wines.
  • Port is a protected geographical name associated with Portugal.

How should fortified wine be served?

Dry and lighter fortified wines are commonly served chilled or cool and can work as aperitifs or alongside savoury foods. Richer, sweeter styles are often served cool rather than very cold and can suit desserts, blue cheese, nuts or dried fruit.

These are useful starting points, not rules. A modest pour in a small wine or fortified-wine glass is practical because fortified wines are usually served in smaller quantities than table wine.

How should an opened bottle be stored?

Reseal an opened bottle promptly and store it upright in a cool, dark place. Refrigeration is a sensible default because keeping the bottle cool slows chemical changes.

There is no single guaranteed shelf life for every fortified wine. A style that has already spent a long time ageing oxidatively may remain enjoyable longer than a delicate, fresh or fruit-driven wine.

The easiest way to remember fortified wine

  1. Was spirit added? If yes, the wine is fortified.
  2. How much sugar remains? That helps determine whether it tastes dry or sweet.
  3. How was it matured? Wood, oxygen, flor and bottle ageing can all shape its character.

This prevents a common misunderstanding: “fortified” does not mean “sweet”. It tells you how the wine was made.
