How to choose wine at a restaurant

Before looking closely at the list, answer five questions:

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Learning path

Choosing and buying

Choose bottles for real situations, assess value and navigate shops, lists, scores and marketing.

Explore this learning path →

Choosing wine at a restaurant does not require memorising grape varieties or knowing the “right” answer. Start with the food, the people drinking, your preferences and your budget. Then use the wine list—and the restaurant’s staff—to narrow the choice.

Highlights

  • Start with the whole dish, including its sauce, seasoning and richness.
  • Think about what the group actually enjoys, not what sounds prestigious.
  • Describe the style you want in ordinary language: “crisp and dry”, “lighter red” or “not too sweet”.
  • It is reasonable to tell staff your budget and ask for two or three options.
  • Choose by the glass if people want different wines or a bottle may not be finished.
  • Check the bottle details before it is opened.
  • The first taste is mainly a check for faults, not a test of whether the wine is your new favourite.
  • BYO, corkage, pour sizes and service practices vary, so confirm the restaurant’s current terms.

Quick answer: the easiest way to choose

Before looking closely at the list, answer five questions:

  1. What are we eating? Consider the complete dish, including cooking method, sauce, spice and richness.
  2. Who is drinking? A shared bottle works best when several people want something similar.
  3. What do we enjoy? Think about colour, body, sweetness and freshness rather than feeling obliged to name a grape.
  4. What is the budget? Set a maximum in Australian dollars before choosing.
  5. Will we order glasses or a bottle? Glasses offer flexibility; a bottle can suit a group with shared preferences.

Then ask a server or sommelier for help. For example:

“We’re having grilled fish and pasta, and we’d like something dry, fresh and not too heavy. We’d like to stay under $80 for a bottle. Could you suggest two or three options?”

That gives the staff useful information without requiring technical wine knowledge.

Read the list for clues, not guarantees

A restaurant wine list might identify a wine by its producer, grape variety, region, geographical indication, vintage, style, format and price. It may also group wines by colour, country, region, grape or house-created categories.

Not every list includes all these details. Some descriptions may be brief or informal, so treat them as clues rather than a complete technical profile.

A grape variety is the type of grape used, such as Chardonnay or Shiraz. A region or geographical indication tells you where the wine comes from. A vintage is the year the grapes were harvested. These details help identify the wine, but none guarantees that it will suit your taste.

If a list includes unfamiliar terms, you can ask what they mean or describe the kind of experience you want. For more on producer, grape, region and vintage information, see How to Read a Wine Label.

Use simple preference language

You do not need to ask for a particular grape. Try describing the wine’s likely shape and feel:

  • “I’d like a crisp, dry white.”
  • “Something fresh and aromatic, but not sweet.”
  • “Could we have a lighter-bodied red?”
  • “I’m after a fuller-bodied white for a rich sauce.”
  • “Something smooth rather than strongly tannic.”
  • “Do you have a local Australian option within this budget?”

Body means how full or weighty a wine feels in your mouth. Acidity contributes freshness and a mouth-watering sensation. Sweetness is the perception of sugar; a wine can smell or taste fruity without being sweet.

You can explore these ideas in What Is Wine Body?, What Is Acidity in Wine? and What Is Sweetness in Wine?.

Let the food guide you—flexibly

Pairing is a useful starting point, not a set of compulsory rules. A dish’s preparation can matter as much as its main ingredient. A creamy sauce, chilli heat, salty seasoning or rich cooking method may point you towards a different style than the ingredient alone would suggest.

Personal preference matters too. If you dislike very dry wines, strong tannins or highly aromatic styles, that information is more useful than a rigid rule about what “must” go with a dish.

For a broader explanation, read How Wine Pairing Works.

Bottle or glass?

Choose wine by the glass when:

  • people in the group want different styles;
  • only one or two people want wine;
  • you are unsure whether a bottle will be finished;
  • you would rather keep the choice flexible.

A bottle may make sense when several diners want the same wine and are happy to share it. Do not assume every venue offers the same bottle sizes, carafes or glass pours.

Compare like with like: compare a bottle price with other bottle prices, and a glass price with other glass prices. If the decision depends on value, ask how large the pour is and whether the listed price is per glass or per bottle.

A restaurant pour is not necessarily one Australian standard drink. The Australian Government defines a standard drink as 10 grams of pure alcohol, and the number of standard drinks depends on both the pour size and the wine’s alcohol strength.

What to do when the bottle arrives

In a formal bottle service, the bottle is presented before it is opened. Check that the producer, wine name, vintage where relevant and format match what you ordered.

After opening, the person who ordered the wine may be offered a taste. This is mainly a check that the wine appears sound—not an examination of its prestige, quality score or personal popularity.

If it smells musty, stale, sharply vinegary or otherwise clearly wrong, say:

“I’m not sure this wine is sound. Could you please check it?”

A musty smell can be a sign of cork taint, a wine fault associated with TCA. However, unfamiliar does not automatically mean faulty. Some wines are intentionally oxidative, earthy, sweet, sparkling or strongly aromatic. If you are uncertain, ask the server or sommelier to assess it rather than rejecting it simply because it is not your preferred style.

Australian restaurant details to confirm

If you are considering BYO, check the restaurant’s current policy first. Ask whether BYO is allowed, whether corkage applies, whether food must be ordered, which alcohol types are accepted and whether there are limits on bottles.

These conditions vary between venues and jurisdictions. The same applies to service charges, glass sizes, bottle formats and minimum purchases. The restaurant’s current terms are more useful than assuming there is one national rule.

Your table-side checklist

Before ordering, ask:

  • What are we eating, including the sauce and seasoning?
  • What style does the group enjoy?
  • What is our maximum budget in Australian dollars?
  • Would glasses give us more flexibility?
  • Which details does the list provide?
  • Can staff suggest two or three suitable options?

When the bottle arrives:

  • Check the producer, wine, vintage where relevant and format.
  • Taste calmly for obvious faults.
  • Ask staff to check a concern politely.
  • Remember that “not my style” is different from “the wine is faulty”.

The best restaurant choice is usually not the most impressive-sounding name. It is the wine that fits the meal, the people, the occasion and the amount you want to spend.

Frequently asked questions

Should I choose the cheapest wine?

Not automatically. Price is not a simple ranking of enjoyment or suitability. Compare wines by style, format and price, and choose one that fits the group and meal. See Why Wine Prices Vary.

Do I need to know the grape variety?

No. You can describe a preference such as “crisp and dry”, “lighter-bodied red” or “not too sweet”, then ask staff to translate that into suitable options.

Can I reject a wine after tasting it?

The first taste is intended to identify whether the wine seems sound and matches the order. If you dislike the style but it appears sound, that is different from identifying a fault. If you are unsure, ask the staff member to check it.

Is BYO available at Australian restaurants?

Some venues allow BYO and others do not. Corkage, food requirements, permitted alcohol types and bottle limits vary. Check the restaurant’s current policy before arriving.
