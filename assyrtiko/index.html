What Is Assyrtiko? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what Assyrtiko is, where it comes from, how its dry white wines taste and why Australian drinkers may encounter this Greek grape.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Assyrtiko is a white wine-grape variety, not a wine style or place.
  • It comes from Greece and is traditionally associated with Santorini.
  • It is best known for dry white wines with pronounced acidity and freshness.
  • Citrus, savoury, stony and textured descriptions are common, but no bottle tastes exactly the same.
  • Assyrtiko is also grown elsewhere in Greece and in small Australian plantings.
  • It can be used for both dry wines and traditional sweet wines such as Santorini Vinsanto.

Quick answer: what is Assyrtiko?

Assyrtiko is a white wine grape from Greece, traditionally associated with the island of Santorini. It is most commonly used to make dry white wine with lively acidity, freshness and a firm or textured palate.

A typical Assyrtiko may suggest citrus, fresh fruit or savoury flavours. Wines from Santorini are also often described using stony or saline language. These are tasting descriptions, however—not fixed properties that appear in every bottle. Climate, site, ripeness, harvest timing and winemaking can all change the result.

For an Australian wine learner, the simplest way to remember Assyrtiko is this: it is a Greek white grape known for holding onto freshness while still giving the wine flavour and texture.

Assyrtiko and Santorini: grape versus place

Assyrtiko is the name of the grape variety. Santorini is the place most closely associated with it, as well as a protected wine designation.

That distinction matters when reading a label:

  • Assyrtiko identifies the grape variety.
  • Santorini identifies a geographical context and protected designation.
  • A wine labelled Assyrtiko may come from outside Santorini, provided the labelling complies with the relevant rules.

Under the current Santorini PDO specification, dry white Santorini wine must contain at least 85% Assyrtiko. The remainder may include permitted local white varieties such as Athiri and Aidani.

Santorini is Assyrtiko’s best-known traditional home, but the grape is also grown elsewhere in Greece. It has travelled beyond Greece too, including to Australia.

What does Assyrtiko taste like?

The most useful way to understand Assyrtiko is to separate its different parts.

Acidity

Assyrtiko is commonly associated with pronounced acidity. Acidity is the mouth-watering, fresh sensation that gives wine energy and shape. In a tasting, it may make the wine feel crisp or refreshing.

Dryness

Most Assyrtiko is made as dry white wine. “Dry” means the wine has little perceived sweetness from residual sugar. It does not mean the wine lacks flavour.

A dry Assyrtiko can still taste strongly of citrus, fresh fruit or savoury notes. This is why dryness, flavour and acidity should not be treated as the same thing.

Body and texture

Body is the impression of weight or fullness in the mouth. Assyrtiko can be light- to medium-bodied, but ripe fruit, warmer sites, alcohol, lees contact and other winemaking choices can give it a broader or fuller feel.

Some examples have a firm, slightly grippy or textured palate. Others are leaner and more direct. The grape provides tendencies, not a template that every winemaker must follow.

Aroma and flavour

Citrus and fresh-fruit character are useful starting points. Depending on the wine’s origin and making, you may also notice savoury, stony or saline impressions.

“Mineral” and “salty” are common wine-tasting terms for some Assyrtiko expressions, particularly those associated with Santorini. They should be understood as descriptions of how a wine seems to smell or taste, not as universal chemical properties of the grape.

What kinds of wine are made from Assyrtiko?

Dry still white wine is Assyrtiko’s principal use. The familiar style is fresh, dry and acidity-led, with enough flavour and texture to avoid seeming thin.

The grape can also be blended with other white varieties. It has a documented role in Santorini Vinsanto, a traditional sweet wine made using sun-dried grapes. This is a different style from dry Assyrtiko, not simply a sweeter version of the same wine.

The variety’s expression can broaden outside Santorini. A warmer site, later harvest or different maturation approach may produce more generous fruit, weight or alcohol, while other choices may emphasise freshness and tension.

Why can Assyrtiko vary so much?

A grape variety does not determine a wine’s final character by itself. The result may be influenced by:

  • climate and water availability;
  • vineyard site and vine management;
  • when the grapes are harvested;
  • yield and ripeness;
  • fermentation vessel and temperature;
  • lees contact, oak or other maturation choices;
  • blending and residual sugar.

Assyrtiko is often discussed in connection with hot or dry conditions because reported examples can retain freshness. That does not make it drought-proof or automatically suitable for every Australian vineyard. Performance depends on the particular site, season and growing decisions.

Why might Australians encounter Assyrtiko?

Assyrtiko has been imported and planted in Australia, particularly in South Australia. Wine Australia has identified it as an alternative variety of interest and reported in 2026 that it was appearing increasingly on Australian labels.

ABC Rural reported that an early Australian planting was established in South Australia in 2012, with a first commercial wine released in 2016. Australian plantings remain a developing part of the country’s wine sector, and current national production or vineyard figures should not be assumed from these early examples.

An Australian Assyrtiko should not be expected to taste exactly like a Santorini wine. Local climate, site, irrigation, harvest timing and winemaking may create a different balance of acidity, fruit, body and texture.

How to taste Assyrtiko

When tasting a bottle, ask four simple questions:

  1. How fresh does it feel? Notice the mouth-watering effect of acidity.
  2. Does it taste dry? Separate the absence of sweetness from the intensity of flavour.
  3. How much weight does it have? Consider whether the wine feels light, broad or full.
  4. What flavours and textures stand out? Look for citrus, fruit, savoury notes, firmness or breadth without expecting a fixed checklist.

Frequently asked questions

Is Assyrtiko a dry wine?

Assyrtiko is a grape variety, not a sweetness category. It is commonly used for dry white wine, but it can also be used in sweet styles such as Santorini Vinsanto.

Is all Assyrtiko from Santorini?

No. Santorini is its principal traditional association, but Assyrtiko is also grown elsewhere in Greece and in Australia.

Is Assyrtiko always salty or mineral?

No. Those are tasting descriptions associated with some wines, especially some Santorini expressions. Assyrtiko can also show citrus, fresh fruit and savoury character.

How do you pronounce Assyrtiko?

An approximate English pronunciation is ah-SER-tee-koh. Pronunciation can vary, so treating this as a helpful guide rather than an absolute rule is sensible.

Is Australian Assyrtiko the same as Santorini Assyrtiko?

It is the same grape variety, but not necessarily the same style. Growing conditions and winemaking can produce meaningful differences between Australian and Santorini examples.
