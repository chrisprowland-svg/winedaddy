Cava wine regions: where Spanish Cava comes from

Understand Cava’s four recognised zones, the Penedès heartland, key subzones, grapes and what geographic terms mean on a bottle.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Cava is a protected Spanish designation for quality sparkling wine.
  • It is not a synonym for every sparkling wine made in Spain.
  • Cava’s legal territory covers four current zones: Comtats de Barcelona, Valle del Ebro, Viñedos de Almendralejo and Requena.
  • More than 95% of production is concentrated in Comtats de Barcelona, especially around Penedès and Sant Sadurní d’Anoia.
  • Cava is not limited to Catalonia or one famous town.
  • Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada are the varieties most closely associated with classic Cava, but other grapes are authorised.
  • A zone can help explain a wine’s growing conditions, but it cannot predict the exact taste of every bottle.
  • Cava classification terms such as Reserva and Gran Reserva describe ageing or quality categories, not regions.

Quick answer: what are the Cava wine regions?

Cava is a protected designation of origin for quality sparkling wine from a legally defined area of Spain. The wine is made under specific rules, including traditional-method production, and must come from the recognised Cava territory.

That territory is divided into four current zones:

  1. Comtats de Barcelona
  2. Valle del Ebro
  3. Viñedos de Almendralejo
  4. Requena

Comtats de Barcelona is the historic and commercial heartland. It includes the Penedès area and the well-known municipality of Sant Sadurní d’Anoia, generally around 45–60 minutes south-west of Barcelona by road, depending on the route and traffic. The Cava authority reports that more than 95% of production is concentrated in this zone.

But Cava does not simply mean “wine from Catalonia”. The designation also includes recognised areas in the Ebro Valley, Extremadura and the Valencia region. That distinction matters when reading a label: Penedès, Catalonia, a town name and Cava are related, but they are not interchangeable geographic terms.

Where is Cava made?

Comtats de Barcelona

Comtats de Barcelona is in north-eastern Spain and includes the Penedès heartland. Sant Sadurní d’Anoia is the best-known locality associated with Cava, while Vilafranca del Penedès is a useful regional centre for orientation.

The zone contains five recognised subzones:

  • Valls d’Anoia-Foix
  • Serra de Mar
  • Conca del Gaià
  • Serra de Prades
  • Pla de Ponent

Conditions vary within the zone. Coastal or inland influence, elevation, exposure, wind and humidity can all affect how grapes ripen. This helps explain why Cava from the same broad zone does not necessarily taste identical from bottle to bottle.

Valle del Ebro

Valle del Ebro refers to inland Cava-growing areas associated with the Ebro River and surrounding territories, including parts of the wider areas linked with Rioja, Navarra and Aragón.

Its conditions are generally more inland and continental than those of the Mediterranean-influenced Penedès heartland, with cold winters and hot, dry summers. The zone contains two recognised subzones:

  • Alto Ebro
  • Valle del Cierzo

“Ebro Valley” is a useful English orientation, but the official Spanish names may be more relevant when considering formal geographic wording.

Viñedos de Almendralejo

This zone centres on the municipality of Almendralejo in Tierra de Barros, in Extremadura in south-western Spain. It is a separate Cava zone, not part of Comtats de Barcelona.

The area is described as relatively flat, dry and warm, with vineyards generally between about 200 and 450 metres above sea level. Those conditions may influence ripeness and freshness, but they do not dictate one universal style.

Requena

Requena is the current Cava zone in eastern Spain, in the Valencia region. Older Cava communications referred to this area as “Levante” while the final naming was being settled. Current consumer-facing Cava material uses Requena.

Because official designation wording can change, treat Requena as the current zone name but check the latest specification when interpreting formal label details.

Which grapes are associated with Cava?

The principal traditional association is the blend of:

  • Macabeo, also known as Viura
  • Xarel·lo
  • Parellada

Macabeo is associated with freshness and aroma, Xarel·lo with body and structure, and Parellada with finesse and freshness. These are useful starting points, not guarantees of what every Cava will taste like.

Other authorised varieties include Chardonnay, Subirat Parent, Garnacha tinta, Trepat, Monastrell and Pinot Noir. The permitted grapes are not necessarily used in the same proportions in every zone or bottle.

Place can influence how a grape ripens, but variety, vineyard site, vintage, harvest decisions, ageing and winemaking also matter.

How should you read a Cava label?

Start with the word Cava. It identifies a protected Spanish sparkling-wine designation, rather than generic Spanish sparkling wine.

You may also encounter more specific geographic wording, such as a zone or subzone, where the applicable rules permit it. A producer description can offer useful context, but it should not automatically be treated as a legal region.

Other terms describe ageing or quality segmentation rather than geography:

  • Cava de Guarda
  • Cava de Guarda Superior
  • Reserva
  • Gran Reserva
  • Paraje Calificado

For example, Paraje Calificado refers to a recognised smaller geographic unit. It is not another broad zone equivalent to Comtats de Barcelona or Valle del Ebro.

For an Australian shopper, the simplest approach is to separate three questions:

  1. Is the wine labelled Cava?
  2. Does the label give more specific geographic information?
  3. Are terms such as Reserva or Gran Reserva describing ageing rather than place?

Cava’s structure is comparable to Australia’s use of protected geographic indications in one broad sense: both use defined geographic names. But the systems are not identical, so a Cava zone should not be treated as though it were automatically the same as an Australian zone, region or subregion.

What does the region tell you about the wine?

Cava’s regions can provide clues about growing conditions. Mediterranean influence, inland climate, elevation, soils, exposure and wind may contribute to differences in ripeness, acidity, freshness, aroma, texture and structure.

The important word is contribute. A region does not guarantee a single flavour profile. Two Cavas from the same zone may differ because of their grape varieties, vineyard sites, harvest timing, ageing and winemaking choices.

This is why geography is best used as a guide, not a promise. It helps you ask better questions about a bottle without pretending that a place name tells you everything.

Cava, Champagne and Prosecco: what is the difference?

Cava, Champagne and Prosecco are separate protected designations with different territories and rules.

  • Cava is a protected Spanish quality sparkling-wine designation.
  • Champagne is a protected French designation.
  • Prosecco is a protected Italian designation.

Cava is not “Spanish Champagne”, and not all Spanish sparkling wine is Cava. The production method may be an important point of comparison, but the geographic names refer to different legal systems and places.

Frequently asked questions

Does all Cava come from Catalonia?

No. Catalonia, particularly the Penedès area, is the principal heartland, but the Cava territory also includes Valle del Ebro, Viñedos de Almendralejo and Requena.

Is Sant Sadurní d’Anoia the Cava region?

No. It is a municipality strongly associated with Cava and its history, but it is not the whole Cava designation.

Is Cava the same as Spanish sparkling wine?

No. Cava is a protected designation with defined geographic and production rules. Other Spanish sparkling wines may use different protected names or indications.

Do Reserva and Gran Reserva identify Cava regions?

No. They are ageing or quality-segmentation terms, not geographic zones.

Can a Cava label tell me exactly what the wine will taste like?

Not by itself. Geographic information can suggest growing conditions, but grape varieties, site, vintage, ageing and winemaking also shape the wine.

What should an Australian buyer remember?

Look for Cava as the protected name, treat zone information as useful context, and do not confuse ageing terms with regional names. A Cava label can tell you where the wine belongs within a protected Spanish system, but it cannot tell you everything about the bottle.
