What is cover cropping in vineyards?

Cover cropping means growing or managing plants around grapevines. Learn why vineyards use it, its benefits and trade-offs, and why Australian conditions matter.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Cover cropping means deliberately growing or managing plants around grapevines, usually between vine rows.
  • The plants may be grasses, legumes, native vegetation or a mixture.
  • Growers use cover crops to protect soil, compete with weeds, improve vineyard access and sometimes moderate vine vigour.
  • Cover crops can also compete with vines for water and nutrients.
  • Their value depends on the vineyard’s climate, soil, rainfall, irrigation, vine age and seasonal conditions.
  • Cover cropping is a farming practice—not proof that a wine is organic, biodynamic, regenerative or sustainably certified.

Quick answer

Cover cropping in vineyards is the deliberate growing or management of plants in the soil around grapevines, usually between vine rows and sometimes beneath the vines, instead of keeping every part of the vineyard floor bare.

The plants are not grown as a second harvest. They are managed for purposes such as reducing erosion, protecting soil, suppressing weeds, supporting soil structure and organic matter, providing habitat, improving machinery access and—in some vineyards—moderating excessive vine growth.

But cover crops are not automatically beneficial. Living plants use water and nutrients, so they can compete with grapevines. That competition may be useful in a very vigorous vineyard, but damaging in a young, weak or water-limited vineyard. In Australia, where many vineyards experience hot, dry conditions or depend partly on stored soil moisture, water competition is an especially important consideration.

In other words, cover cropping is a management choice and a balancing act, not a universal recipe.

What grows between the vines?

Cover crops can be established in different parts of a vineyard:

  • Inter-row or mid-row cover grows between the rows of vines.
  • Under-vine cover grows beneath the vines or along the vine row.
  • Permanent swards are maintained over several seasons.
  • Annual cover crops grow for part of a season before being mown, rolled, grazed, cultivated or otherwise ended.

Common plant groups include grasses and cereals, legumes such as clovers or vetches, native or locally adapted plants, and mixed stands.

A vineyard’s existing vegetation may also be deliberately managed as ground cover. This is sometimes called resident vegetation: plants that emerge naturally from the soil seed bank or surrounding landscape rather than being sown. The practical distinction is whether the vegetation is being managed for a defined vineyard purpose, not simply whether someone planted it.

A cover crop is living vegetation. That makes it different from mulch, which is dead or detached material placed on the soil surface. Mown cover-crop residue can later act like mulch, but the two practices are not the same.

Why do vineyards use cover crops?

Protecting soil and reducing erosion

Plants and their roots help hold soil in place. Vegetation also softens the impact of rain and slows water moving across the ground, which can reduce erosion—particularly on sloping vineyards or during intense rainfall.

This is one reason a vineyard floor may look green rather than freshly cultivated and bare.

Supporting soil structure

Roots and plant residues can add organic matter and support soil biological activity. Ground cover may also help water enter the soil rather than running across a sealed surface.

These are potential benefits, not guarantees. Results vary with the plants used, the soil, rainfall and how the cover is managed. Australian trials in South Australia found changes in some carbon and nitrogen indicators under particular under-vine treatments, but those results should not be treated as a promise for every vineyard.

Competing with weeds

A dense cover crop can compete with weeds for light, water and nutrients. Once living plants are mown or die back, their residue may continue to shade the soil surface and make weed establishment more difficult.

However, cover crops do not eliminate weeds in every vineyard. Their effectiveness depends on species, density, weather and management.

Improving access

A vegetated vineyard floor can provide a firmer surface for workers and machinery after rain. It may reduce mud and dust and help machinery travel between rows.

That benefit comes with its own work: cover crops may need to be sown, mown, rolled, grazed or otherwise managed.

Supporting habitat

Flowering or diverse vegetation can provide food and shelter for some insects and other organisms. In the right setting, this may support functional biodiversity, including insects that help with natural pest-management processes.

The outcome depends on the plant mix, flowering periods, surrounding habitat and wider vineyard management. Green rows alone do not prove that biodiversity has increased.

Managing vine vigour

Vine vigour means the strength of vegetative growth—such as shoots and leaves. In a vineyard with abundant water and excessive growth, competition from a cover crop may help moderate that vigour.

The same competition can be unhelpful where vines are already weak or short of water. Cover cropping can therefore affect vine growth and yield in different directions, depending on the site and season.

What is the main trade-off?

The central trade-off is simple: cover crops can protect the vineyard floor, but they also use resources.

Actively growing plants may compete with vines for:

  • water, especially in the upper soil layers;
  • nutrients, including nitrogen;
  • root-zone space; and
  • sometimes sunlight or access, depending on where they grow.

Research from Wine Australia’s drought-resilience work found significant water competition in Australian vineyard systems. Ground-cover plants can deplete soil water during spring as dry conditions extend. Irrigation may buffer some shorter dry periods, but it does not remove all competition during prolonged water shortages.

The balance can differ sharply between an irrigated vineyard and a dry-grown, or rain-dependent, vineyard. It can also differ between a cooler or wetter region and a hotter or drier one. Soil depth, rainfall, irrigation, vine age and root development all matter.

Young vines may be particularly vulnerable because their root systems are still developing. In a vigorous, well-watered site, however, carefully managed competition may be part of the grower’s objective.

Other considerations can include frost, fire and pests. Tall or poorly timed vegetation may be undesirable in a frost-prone vineyard, while dry plant material can require attention where fire risk is important. Vegetation can provide habitat for beneficial organisms, but may shelter unwanted pests in some situations.

How are cover crops managed?

There is no single timetable that suits every vineyard. Depending on the site and the season, growers may:

  • mow the vegetation;
  • roll it flat;
  • graze it;
  • cultivate it;
  • allow it to senesce naturally; or
  • terminate it using another vineyard-floor method.

The aim is to keep the useful ground cover for as long as it helps, while preventing excessive competition with the vines. In a dry season, that may mean managing the cover crop earlier to conserve soil water. In a frost-prone site, keeping vegetation short may be important.

This is why the presence of a green vineyard floor does not tell you, by itself, whether the practice is helping or hurting the vines. Timing and local conditions matter as much as the plants themselves.

Does cover cropping mean a wine is sustainable?

No. Cover cropping is one vineyard practice. It does not, by itself, prove that a wine is organic, biodynamic, regenerative or certified sustainable.

Those descriptions involve broader systems, rules or standards covering areas such as inputs, biodiversity, soil, water, people and business practices. For example, a sustainability programme may require evidence across environmental, social and economic areas—not simply vegetation between vine rows.

A vineyard can use cover crops without holding a particular certification. Equally, a certified producer may use a range of practices beyond cover cropping.

Frequently asked questions

Are cover crops always planted?

No. Some growers sow selected plants, while others deliberately retain and manage suitable vegetation that appears naturally.

Do cover crops save water?

Not necessarily. They can improve soil protection and infiltration, but living plants also use water. In dry conditions, they may compete with vines for the water available.

Are legumes always better than grasses?

No plant group is best for every vineyard. Selection depends on the site, season and management goal. Legumes and grasses have different growth and nutrient effects, and mixtures also behave differently.

Can cover crops improve wine quality?

The supplied evidence supports talking about effects on vineyard conditions, vine growth, yield and grape quality only with careful qualification. It does not support a universal claim that cover cropping makes wine taste better.

What should a visitor look for in a vineyard?

A green vineyard floor may indicate a sown cover crop, resident vegetation or simply seasonal growth. It is reasonable to ask what the grower is trying to achieve and how the plants are managed—especially in relation to water, soil protection and vine growth.

Ultimately, cover cropping is a balancing decision: the vegetation must provide enough soil, weed, biodiversity or operational benefit without competing with the vines more than that particular site and season can support.
