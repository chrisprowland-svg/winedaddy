What Is Tannat? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what Tannat is, where it comes from, what it tastes like, why it can feel tannic, and how Australian drinkers may encounter this distinctive red grape.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Tannat is a dark-skinned red wine grape variety.
  • It is historically associated with south-west France, especially Madiran and Saint-Mont.
  • Uruguay is its strongest contemporary international association.
  • Tannat commonly makes deeply coloured, concentrated wines with noticeable tannin and acidity.
  • Tannin can make a wine feel firm, grippy or drying; it is not the same as sweetness, acidity or alcohol.
  • Australian Tannat is established but niche, with plantings recorded across several regions.
  • Not every Tannat tastes heavy or aggressively tannic. Climate, ripeness, blending and winemaking matter.

Quick answer: what is Tannat?

Tannat is a red wine-grape variety. It is best known for producing deeply coloured, structured red wines with firm tannins, high acidity and flavours that may include plum, cherry, raspberry, currant and spice.

The grape is historically linked to south-west France, where it is particularly associated with the Madiran and Saint-Mont wine areas. It became especially important in Uruguay after arriving there in the nineteenth century. Today, Uruguay treats Tannat as an emblematic national variety.

For an Australian wine learner, the simplest way to think about Tannat is as a full-flavoured red grape with plenty of structure—but not a grape that always produces the same style. A fresher Tannat can show bright red fruit and lively acidity, while a riper, oak-aged or more extracted example may feel darker, richer and more powerful.

Where does Tannat come from?

Tannat is historically associated with the Hautes-Pyrénées and the wider south-west of France. Its exact origin is best described cautiously: it is believed to have originated in that part of France rather than being assigned to one certain location.

In France, Tannat is strongly associated with Madiran, a red-wine appellation where it is an important grape in blends. It is also used in Saint-Mont, another south-west French wine context. Madiran is a place and protected wine name, not simply another word for every bottle made from Tannat.

Uruguay is the other essential place to recognise. Tannat was introduced there around the 1870s and became central to the country’s wine identity. INAVI, Uruguay’s national viticulture institute, describes it as the country’s emblematic variety. Its 2019 vineyard statistics recorded 1,631.7 hectares of Tannat, or 26.6% of Uruguay’s vineyard area. That is a dated figure, but it shows how important the variety has been in Uruguay.

What does Tannat taste like?

Tannat commonly produces red wines with deep colour, concentrated fruit and a firm shape. Possible aromas and flavours include:

  • plum and other dark fruit
  • cherry and raspberry
  • currant or blackcurrant
  • spice
  • savoury characters, particularly with maturation

These are tendencies, not a checklist. A Tannat from a cooler site or picked earlier may taste fresher and more red-fruited. Riper fruit can give darker fruit, greater weight and a rounder impression. Oak and bottle age may add further savoury or spicy notes.

Tannat’s fruitiness does not necessarily mean the wine is sweet. A dry wine can smell strongly of ripe fruit while containing little or no perceptible sweetness. Dry does not mean flavourless is a useful distinction when tasting any red wine.

Why can Tannat feel firm and drying?

The key word is tannin. Tannins are compounds found mainly in grape skins and seeds. In red wine, they can create a drying, puckering or grippy sensation on the gums and cheeks.

Tannat is naturally capable of contributing substantial tannin, which is why young examples can feel firm. But the grape name does not guarantee that every Tannat will be harsh or aggressively drying. The final texture also depends on fruit maturity, skin contact, extraction, blending, oxygen management and ageing.

It helps to separate three sensations:

  • Tannin: grip, firmness and a drying sensation
  • Acidity: freshness, lift and a mouth-watering feeling
  • Alcohol: warmth and weight

Tannat may have both high tannin and high acidity, but those sensations are different. For a fuller explanation, see What Are Tannins? and What Is Acidity in Wine?.

How can winemaking change Tannat?

A winemaker can guide Tannat towards different expressions.

More extraction from the grape skins can increase colour and tannin. Blending with varieties such as Cabernet Franc or Cabernet Sauvignon can change the wine’s balance, aroma and texture. In France, blending is an important part of some Tannat-based regional wines.

Micro-oxygenation—a controlled exposure to small amounts of oxygen—is sometimes used in France and Uruguay to help soften young tannins. It is a winemaking choice, not an inherent feature of Tannat.

Oak maturation and bottle age can also help integrate tannin and develop additional flavours. However, ageing is not a guarantee of improvement: the result depends on the individual wine, its balance, its vintage and how it has been stored.

Tannat can also be made in fresher, blended, sparkling, rosé or sweet styles. These are less central to the beginner’s usual picture of the grape, but they are a useful reminder that Tannat is not limited to one heavy red-wine template.

Tannat in Australia

Australian Tannat is a small but established alternative variety rather than a mainstream national planting.

An AWRI snapshot from 2015 recorded at least 31 Australian producers across 19 regions. McLaren Vale, New England and Queensland’s Granite Belt were the locations with the most producers in that snapshot. South Australia had approximately nine hectares planted, mostly in McLaren Vale.

Wine Australia research has also evaluated Tannat in regional trials at Irymple, Wagga Wagga and Orange. This demonstrates Australian interest in assessing alternative varieties, but it does not establish large-scale commercial production. No current national Tannat planting figure was available in the supplied research, so older figures should not be presented as current totals.

For Australian drinkers, that means Tannat is most likely to appear as an alternative red variety from a range of regions, rather than as a familiar everyday category like Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon.

What food goes with Tannat?

Tannat’s tannin generally suits foods with protein and richness, which can make the wine feel less drying. Starting points include:

  • grilled or braised beef
  • beef cheeks and other slow-cooked meats
  • lamb and roast meats
  • sausages
  • mushroom-rich dishes
  • hard, savoury cheeses

The best match depends on the wine’s style. A dense, oak-aged Tannat may suit a richer meal, while a fresher and lighter version can work with less substantial food. Pairing is a starting point, not a rule.

Serve a structured Tannat slightly below warm room temperature. A young, firm bottle may also benefit from decanting, although decanting does not automatically improve every wine or guarantee softer tannins.

How should a beginner approach Tannat?

If you are trying Tannat for the first time, pay attention to three things: fruit, freshness and grip.

First, notice whether the fruit feels more like plum and dark berries or brighter cherry and raspberry. Then look for acidity: does the wine feel lively and mouth-watering? Finally, notice the tannin. Does it feel smooth, chalky, grippy or drying?

This is more useful than asking whether Tannat is “good” or “bad”. The variety has a recognisable structural reputation, but region, vintage, producer and winemaking can move it in very different directions. How to Taste Wine and What Is Wine Body? can help put those observations into words.

Frequently asked questions

Is Tannat always a full-bodied red wine?

No. Tannat is commonly concentrated and structured, but body varies. Some examples can be medium-bodied, fresher or made in alternative styles.

Is Tannat the same thing as tannin?

No. Tannat is a grape variety. Tannin is a group of compounds that can create texture and astringency in wine.

Is Tannat sweet?

Usually, Tannat is made as a dry wine, but the grape can be used in different styles. Dark fruit flavour or ripe aromas do not automatically mean sweetness.

Is Tannat the same as Madiran?

No. Madiran is a French wine appellation strongly associated with Tannat. The two names should not be treated as interchangeable in ordinary wine-label language.

Is Tannat grown in Australia?

Yes. Historical AWRI evidence recorded Tannat producers across several Australian regions, including McLaren Vale, New England and the Granite Belt. Australian Tannat remains niche rather than a mainstream national variety.

Does Tannat need ageing?

Not necessarily. Some Tannat wines have good ageing potential, but whether a particular bottle will improve depends on its balance, quality, vintage and storage. Young Tannat can also be enjoyable when served appropriately.

What is Tannat known for?

Tannat is best known for deeply coloured, structured red wines with pronounced tannin and acidity, and for its strong association with Uruguay.
