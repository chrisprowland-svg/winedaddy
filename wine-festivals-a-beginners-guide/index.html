Wine Fundamentals

Wine festivals: a beginner’s guide

Learn what to expect at an Australian wine festival, how to prepare, taste confidently, ask useful questions and plan a safe journey home.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • A wine festival is an organised event where you can taste wines and learn about them in a social setting.
  • Festivals vary: some focus on a region, others on several producers, a wine style, food, education or entertainment.
  • Your ticket may cover some tastings, but inclusions such as food, glassware and transport depend on the event.
  • You do not need expert wine vocabulary, and you do not need to taste everything.
  • A simple approach is to look, smell, taste, compare and note what you enjoy.
  • Plan your way home before you begin tasting. If you drink, do not drive.

Quick answer: what is a wine festival?

A wine festival is an organised wine-focused event where visitors may taste wines from several producers, regions or themes. It may also include food, talks, demonstrations, entertainment or other activities.

There is no single Australian wine-festival format. A regional festival might highlight producers from a particular area. A city event may bring many exhibitors together in one venue. A themed event could focus on a grape variety, wine style or seasonal occasion. A wine show might be a public tasting, a trade event or a judged competition, so the name alone does not tell you exactly what to expect.

That variety is part of the appeal—but it means you should read the specific event information before booking. Check what your ticket includes, whether tastings are unlimited or allocated, how glassware works, the age and identification rules, accessibility information and your transport options.

Most importantly, you can attend as a beginner. Choose a manageable number of wines, ask straightforward questions, take breaks and stop when you have had enough.

How is a festival different from a winery visit?

A winery visit or cellar-door appointment is usually centred on one venue or producer. You might taste a selection of wines, learn about that winery and perhaps see part of the property.

A festival commonly brings multiple producers, activities or exhibitors together. It may be busier and less personal than an appointment, but it gives you the chance to compare different wines in one place.

Neither format is automatically better. A festival suits discovery and variety; a winery visit may offer a quieter, more focused conversation. Both depend on the organiser’s format and the venue’s rules.

What might happen at a wine festival?

A typical visit could look like this:

  1. Arrival and check-in: Follow the entry instructions and collect a tasting glass or tokens if the event provides them.
  2. Orientation: Find the tasting areas, water, food, toilets, first aid or welfare support and transport information.
  3. Tasting: Move between producers or stations at your own pace.
  4. Learning and breaks: Listen to a talk, ask questions, eat something or sit down.
  5. Departure: Leave using the transport plan you arranged before tasting.

Your ticket might include entry, selected tastings, a glass, talks or entertainment. It might not include all of these. Food, extra tastings, take-home bottles and transport may cost more or be unavailable. Treat every inclusion as event-specific rather than assumed.

Before you go: a beginner’s checklist

Read the event page and ticket terms. Check:

  • date, opening time and closing time;
  • what the ticket includes and excludes;
  • whether tastings are unlimited, token-based, allocated or separately paid;
  • glassware arrangements;
  • food, water and non-alcoholic options;
  • age and identification requirements;
  • accessibility, seating and toilet information;
  • weather and cancellation arrangements;
  • parking, public transport, shuttles or other transfers.

For an outdoor or regional event, also consider the forecast, shade, sun protection, footwear and uneven ground. Confirm the practical details rather than assuming that a rural venue will have frequent transport, strong mobile coverage or accessible paths throughout the site.

Wear comfortable clothing and shoes. A festival can involve standing and walking, and a good experience is easier when you are not distracted by sore feet or the weather.

How should a beginner taste wine?

You do not need formal tasting language. Try this simple sequence:

Look

Notice the colour and clarity. You do not need to identify a grape variety from appearance; simply observe what is in the glass.

Smell

Give the wine a gentle smell. Does it remind you of fruit, flowers, herbs, spices, toast or something else? There is no single correct answer.

Taste

Take a small sip and notice whether it seems fresh, soft, dry, sweet, light, rich, smooth or firm. If those words do not help, “I like it” or “not for me” is perfectly useful information.

Compare

Rather than trying every wine, compare two or three. Which one feels lighter? Which has more aroma? Which would you rather drink with food?

Note

Write down a few words, take a permitted photograph of the label or use the event’s tasting guide. Notes are for helping you remember—not for proving expertise.

If spittoons or disposal facilities are provided, you may spit rather than swallow every sample. Spitting can reduce the amount consumed, but it does not make tasting risk-free.

How many wines should you taste?

There is no prize for tasting everything. A large list can quickly become tiring, confusing or uncomfortable.

Choose a small plan. You might start with wines you already understand, compare a familiar style with something new, or focus on one theme. Drink water, eat when food is available and take regular breaks. You can also skip a tasting without explanation.

Ask producers questions such as:

  • “What style is this?”
  • “What should I notice first?”
  • “How is this different from the wine beside it?”
  • “What food would suit it?”
  • “Is it dry, sweet, fresh, rich or sparkling?”
  • “What temperature would you serve it at?”

Telling someone that you are new to wine is not an apology. It gives them a useful starting point.

Responsible tasting and getting home safely

A wine glass is not automatically one standard drink. The amount of alcohol depends on both the pour size and the wine’s strength. In Australia, one standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol. Government examples show that a 100 ml serve can be close to one standard drink for some wines, while a larger serve may contain considerably more.

Count standard drinks where possible, rather than counting glasses. Do not use health guidance as a calculation for whether you can drive. Food, water, coffee and time estimates cannot guarantee that driving is safe.

The safest plan after drinking is not to drive. Arrange transport before you start: a non-drinking driver, public transport, a confirmed shuttle, taxi or rideshare, an organised transfer or nearby accommodation. Check that the option actually operates at the event’s location and finishing time.

Australian Government guidance states that people must be 18 or older to buy alcohol or drink in a licensed venue. Event entry, identification checks and access for minors can also depend on the organiser, venue and state or territory rules. Check the current conditions before attending.

Wine festivals are shared spaces. Be patient in queues, avoid blocking a tasting station, listen when staff are explaining a wine and give producers room to speak with others. If you feel unwell or uncomfortable, stop tasting and ask event staff for help.

FAQs

Do I need to know a lot about wine?

No. Curiosity is enough. You can describe what you smell or taste in everyday language, ask questions and decide whether you enjoy a wine without using specialist terms.

Does a festival ticket include wine and food?

Not necessarily. Tickets vary. Some may include entry and selected tastings; others may use tokens, limits or additional charges. Food, glassware and transport also depend on the individual event.

Is spitting necessary?

No. It is an optional tasting technique when suitable facilities are provided. You can also choose fewer tastings, take breaks or stop altogether.

Can I drive after tasting?

Do not plan to drive after drinking. Arrange safe transport before the event and do not rely on the number of samples, food, water or coffee to predict whether you are safe or within the law.

What should I take?

Bring only what the event permits. Useful items may include comfortable shoes, sun protection, weather-appropriate clothing, a phone, water if allowed and a way to record notes. Check the organiser’s bag, outside-food and entry rules first.

A wine festival is best approached as an opportunity to explore, not an examination. Taste at your own pace, ask simple questions and leave with a clearer sense of what you enjoyed.
