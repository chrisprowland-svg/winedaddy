How to order wine by the glass

A practical Australian guide to reading a by-the-glass wine list, describing your preferences, checking pour sizes and handling service questions confidently.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Ordering wine by the glass is an easy way to try something new, match wine with food or choose different wines for different people at the table. You do not need to know every grape variety or wine region. Start with your budget and a broad idea of what you enjoy, then ask a clear question if you need help.

Highlights

  • Check the wine list for style, grape, region, vintage, pour size and price.
  • “Small glass” and “large glass” can mean different things at different venues.
  • Look for the amount in millilitres, such as 100 mL or 150 mL.
  • Describe what you like in ordinary language: dry, sweet, light, rich, crisp, fruity or savoury.
  • Staff can help you choose, but you do not need to use technical wine language.
  • A taste, when the venue offers one, is mainly a check that the wine is in good condition.
  • Not liking a wine is different from receiving a faulty wine or the wrong order.
  • Australian venues must follow responsible-service obligations, and service practices vary.

The simple way to order wine by the glass

1. Decide what you want

Before studying the list, decide a few basics:

  • red, white, rosé, sparkling or fortified;
  • dry or sweet;
  • light or full-bodied;
  • fresh and crisp or rich and rounded;
  • an approximate budget;
  • one glass, several glasses, a tasting flight or a bottle.

That is enough to begin. “I usually like fresh whites” or “I’d prefer a light red” gives a server useful information.

If you are eating, you can also mention the dish you would like the wine to suit. You do not need a detailed pairing theory. Try:

“I’m having seafood and would like something fresh and not too heavy.”

2. Scan the wine list

A by-the-glass list may be arranged by colour, grape variety, region, country or broad style. Some lists use approachable descriptions such as “fresh”, “fruity” or “full-bodied”. These can help you find a starting point, although they are not exact guarantees of flavour.

Look for whatever information the list provides:

  • wine name or producer;
  • grape variety;
  • region or country;
  • vintage, if shown;
  • colour or style;
  • serving size in millilitres;
  • price per glass;
  • whether the listing is a tasting pour, larger pour or part of a flight.

Not every list includes every field. An online list may also differ from what is currently available in the venue, so it is reasonable to check with staff.

A registered visual such as an annotated wine-label guide can help explain some of these fields, but a restaurant wine list is not the same as a bottle label. The list may leave out information or describe the wine in a different way.

3. Use simple preference words

You can ask about the wine using familiar terms:

  • dry or sweet;
  • light-bodied or full-bodied;
  • crisp or rich;
  • fresh or soft;
  • fruity or savoury;
  • oaky or not too oaky;
  • sparkling or still.

These are conversation starters, not a test of wine knowledge. If you are unsure what a description means, ask. For example:

“Which of these whites is the driest and freshest?”

“I like light-bodied reds with bright fruit and not much oak. What would you suggest?”

“Could you recommend something around $15 a glass that would work with this dish?”

It is also helpful to state your budget plainly. A good recommendation needs to fit what you want to spend.

4. Place the order clearly

A clear order includes the wine, the number of glasses and, where relevant, the listed pour size.

For example:

“One 150 mL glass of the Clare Valley Riesling, please.”

Or:

“Could I have two small glasses of the sparkling wine?”

If a venue offers more than one size, check what “small” or “large” means on that particular list. The safest comparison is the amount in millilitres and the corresponding price.

You do not need to invent a size if the list shows only one glass option. Simply name the wine and quantity.

What does a glass mean in Australia?

There is no single customer-facing meaning that applies everywhere to “glass”, “small glass” or “large glass”. Venues may list the volume in millilitres, often as part of the price information.

The Australian Government uses 100 mL as an example of a standard wine serve and 150 mL as an example of an average restaurant serving. These are useful reference points, not a universal rule for every venue. Alcohol strength also changes the number of Australian standard drinks in a serve.

One Australian standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol. For example, the Government’s guide gives different standard-drink amounts for 100 mL and 150 mL servings of wine at stated alcohol strengths. If this matters to you, check both the listed volume and the wine’s alcohol percentage.

A larger pour may cost more, but pricing differs between venues. Do not assume that a bottle, flight or glass is automatically the best value. Compare the listed prices, the number of drinkers and how much wine you actually want.

Glass, flight or bottle?

A single glass is one selected serving.

A tasting flight usually contains several smaller pours chosen by the venue or customer. The number and size of pours vary, so ask what is included before ordering. Not every venue offers flights.

A bottle can suit a group drinking the same wine, while glasses allow people to choose differently or explore several styles. Compare the bottle price with the listed glass price, but remember that value depends on pour sizes and how much will be consumed.

When the wine arrives

First, check that the wine appears to match what you ordered. The server may name the wine or show the bottle, particularly when the glass has been poured from a bottle at the table.

If the venue offers a taste, try it calmly. A sample is generally an opportunity to check the wine’s condition, rather than a promise that you can exchange it simply because you do not enjoy the style.

There are three useful distinctions:

  • Preference: “This is more acidic than I expected.”
  • Condition concern: “This smells or tastes wrong. Could someone please check it?”
  • Incorrect order: “I ordered the Pinot Gris, but this appears to be a different wine.”

A wine that is unfamiliar or not to your taste is not automatically faulty. If you are uncertain, ask the server or sommelier to check it. Venues may have different policies about replacements, refunds and substitutions, so do not assume that a glass must be replaced because you changed your mind.

Opened bottles are also handled differently from venue to venue. Some businesses use preservation systems; others use different service routines. There is no single freshness period to assume for every by-the-glass wine.

Responsible service in Australia

Alcohol service in Australia is governed by state and territory laws, venue policy and responsible-service obligations. A venue may refuse service, including where a person is intoxicated. Detailed requirements differ between jurisdictions.

For example, NSW guidance says staff must refuse further alcohol service once they become aware that a person is intoxicated. This is a jurisdiction-specific example, not a complete summary of Australian liquor law.

The practical approach is simple: respect venue decisions, ask about the listed serve size, and choose an amount that suits your circumstances.

A useful script for your next order

If you want one sentence to remember, try:

“I’m looking for one glass under $18: dry, fresh and not too oaky. What would you suggest, and what size is the pour?”

That tells the staff member your quantity, budget and preferences while leaving room for a helpful recommendation. Ordering wine by the glass is not an examination. Clear, ordinary language is all you need.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to know the grape variety?

No. You can describe a style, colour or preference instead. Staff can explain the options or suggest a wine from the list.

Can I ask for a taste before ordering?

You can ask, but whether a taste is available depends on the venue. There is no general promise that every venue must provide a complimentary sample.

Can I return a wine if I do not like it?

A personal preference is different from a faulty wine or an incorrect order. Replacement and refund practices vary by venue, so speak with staff promptly and explain the issue clearly.

Is a 150 mL glass the standard size?

Not necessarily. Serving sizes vary. Check the millilitre amount and price shown by the venue.
