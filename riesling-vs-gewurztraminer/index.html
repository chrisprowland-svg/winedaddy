Riesling vs Gewürztraminer: what’s the difference?

Riesling and Gewürztraminer are different aromatic white grapes. Learn how they differ in aroma, acidity, body and sweetness, with Australian context.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

Riesling and Gewürztraminer are different white grape varieties. The simplest distinction is structure and aroma:

  • Riesling is typically lighter-bodied, high in acidity and focused on citrus and fresh fruit.
  • Gewürztraminer is typically more floral, spicy and aromatic, with a broader, more enveloping texture.
  • Both can be dry, off-dry or sweet. The grape name alone does not tell you how sweet the wine will be.
  • A wine can smell strongly of fruit, rose or lychee and still be dry.

If you enjoy lime, lemon, freshness and a clean finish, Riesling may be the better starting point. If you enjoy rose, lychee, perfume, spice and a fuller texture, Gewürztraminer may appeal more.

These are useful tendencies, not guarantees. Climate, ripeness, residual sugar, alcohol, winemaking and serving temperature all affect the finished wine.

Riesling vs Gewürztraminer at a glance

Riesling Gewürztraminer
Common aromas Lime, lemon, grapefruit, apple and other fresh fruit Rose, lychee, apricot, tropical fruit, musk and spice
Acidity Usually pronounced and refreshing Often softer or less sharply fresh, but can retain freshness
Body Usually light to medium Often medium to fuller and broader-textured
Sweetness Dry, off-dry and sweet styles are all made Dry, off-dry and sweet styles are possible
Overall impression Precise, citrusy and fresh Perfumed, expressive and enveloping

The biggest difference is not sweetness

Riesling is often described through its acidity. In Australian examples, that acidity commonly gives the wine a bright, energetic quality, even when the wine contains some residual sugar.

Gewürztraminer is usually recognised first by its aroma. Rose, lychee, ripe stone fruit, tropical fruit and spice can make it seem sweet, even when the wine itself is dry. Its palate may feel broader and more textured than a lean Riesling.

That is why “Riesling is dry” and “Gewürztraminer is sweet” are both unreliable shortcuts. They confuse a grape’s typical aroma or structure with the sweetness of a particular wine.

Fruitiness, perfume and sweetness are different things

  • Fruitiness is an aroma or flavour that reminds you of fruit.
  • Floral character includes aromas such as rose, blossom and perfume.
  • Sweetness is a sensation on the palate, influenced primarily by residual sugar.

A dry Riesling can smell of ripe apple or lime. A dry Gewürztraminer can smell intensely of lychee or rose. Neither needs to taste sugary.

Acidity can also make a wine feel fresher and more balanced, including in an off-dry or sweet style. For a fuller explanation, see our guides to wine aroma, acidity in wine and sweetness in wine.

How they usually taste

Riesling

Riesling commonly shows citrus aromas such as lime, lemon and grapefruit, alongside fresh apple and other green or orchard-fruit notes. Its acidity is often the most obvious structural feature, giving the wine lift and a clean, bright finish.

Australian Riesling is commonly made in crisp, dry styles, but off-dry, sweeter and late-harvest examples are also produced. A bottle’s style matters more than the grape name alone.

Gewürztraminer

Gewürztraminer is one of the more immediately recognisable aromatic varieties. Its fragrance may suggest rose, lychee, apricot, tropical fruit, musk or spice.

The palate often feels broader and more rounded than Riesling, particularly when the fruit is ripe or the wine has higher alcohol. However, Gewürztraminer can also be made in dry, fresh styles. It should not be treated as automatically sweet or low in acidity.

The spelling Gewurztraminer also appears in Australian variety terminology, while Gewürztraminer is the familiar spelling for readers.

Why the same grape can taste different

The grape is only one part of the finished wine.

  • Climate: Cooler conditions can help retain acidity and freshness.
  • Ripeness: Riper grapes can bring more body, alcohol and tropical or stone-fruit character.
  • Residual sugar: Sugar changes the palate, but does not create every fruit or floral aroma.
  • Winemaking: Fermentation choices, skin contact and lees ageing can affect texture and aroma.
  • Serving temperature: A very cold wine may show less aroma; a warmer wine may seem broader and more expressive.

This is why two Rieslings—or two Gewürtraminers—can taste noticeably different. Wine body and balance are useful concepts when comparing them.

The Australian perspective

Australia has a particularly strong Riesling tradition. Clare Valley is associated with light-bodied Riesling, vibrant acidity and classic lime character. Eden Valley is another important South Australian context, with lemon and lime flavours and a steely finish often associated with its higher-altitude vineyards.

Tasmania provides a cooler-climate setting for Riesling and is recognised for styles with freshness and ageing potential. These are regional tendencies, not promises about every bottle.

Australian Gewürztraminer is recognised and commercially produced, but it is a relatively minor variety nationally. Wine Australia’s 2025 snapshot recorded 6,920 tonnes crushed, representing 0.4% of Australia’s total crush. Riverina and Padthaway provided the largest listed shares in that vintage dataset. That does not mean those regions permanently define the variety or that every Australian Gewürztraminer tastes the same.

Which one should you try?

Choose Riesling if you want:

  • lime, lemon or grapefruit character;
  • pronounced freshness;
  • a lighter-bodied wine;
  • a clean, precise finish;
  • a wine that may be dry, off-dry or sweet but is often acid-driven.

Choose Gewürztraminer if you want:

  • rose, lychee or exotic fruit aromas;
  • a strongly perfumed wine;
  • spice and ripe-fruit character;
  • a broader, more enveloping texture;
  • an aromatic wine that may seem sweet even when it is dry.

If sweetness is important, check the wine’s description or residual-sugar information where supplied. Do not decide from the grape name or aroma alone.

Both varieties can work with food. Riesling’s acidity is a natural partner for lighter seafood, salads and goat’s cheese. Gewürztraminer’s perfume and possible touch of sweetness can suit aromatic or strongly seasoned dishes. For a more systematic approach, see how wine pairing works.

Frequently asked questions

Is Riesling sweeter than Gewürztraminer?

Not necessarily. Both grapes can be made into dry, off-dry or sweet wines. Sweetness depends on the finished wine, not simply the variety.

Is Gewürztraminer always sweet?

No. Its floral and lychee-like aromas can create a sweet impression, but a Gewürztraminer can be completely dry.

Is Riesling always dry?

No. Dry Riesling is common in Australia, but off-dry, sweeter and late-harvest styles are also made.

Which has more acidity?

Riesling is typically the more acid-driven and refreshing of the two. Gewürztraminer can retain freshness, but often feels broader and less sharply crisp.

Which is more aromatic?

Gewürztraminer is usually the more immediately floral and perfumed. Riesling can also be highly aromatic, particularly with citrus, apple and floral notes.

Are Riesling and Gewürztraminer the same grape?

No. They are separate white grape varieties with different typical aroma and structural profiles.
