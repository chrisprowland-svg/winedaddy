How much should you spend on wine?

There is no universal right price for wine. Learn how to set a comfortable Australian wine budget based on the occasion, quantity, preferences and value—not price alone.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • There is no universally correct amount to spend on wine.
  • Set your budget around your finances, the occasion, the number of people and the style you enjoy.
  • A higher price can reflect production costs, scarcity, reputation, packaging, tax and distribution—not just taste.
  • More expensive does not automatically mean more enjoyable.
  • The most useful comparison is between wines within your budget, not between the cheapest and most expensive bottles on the shelf.
  • Wine is discretionary spending. Never spend beyond your means to satisfy an occasion or impress someone.

Quick answer

Spend an amount that feels comfortable for your finances and makes sense for the occasion. Then choose the wine that best fits your food, guests and preferences within that limit.

That might mean buying a dependable bottle for a weeknight meal, allowing for several bottles when hosting, or spending more for a gift or milestone occasion because rarity, reputation or presentation matters to you. None of these situations has a universal dollar answer.

A simple rule is:

Set the budget first, then choose the best-suited wine you can find within it.

There is a relationship between wine price and quality when large groups of wines and ratings are studied. One analysis found a moderate association between price and sensory quality ratings. But that does not mean every expensive wine is better, or that you will enjoy it more. Other research has found no significant relationship between price and tasting scores in a small sample.

Price can also shape expectations. If you expect an expensive wine to be impressive, that expectation may influence how you perceive it. That is different from proving that the wine itself is better.

Start with what you can comfortably afford

Wine should fit around your financial commitments, not compete with them. Treat it as discretionary spending: decide what you can comfortably set aside, then avoid stretching that amount because of a label, a special occasion or social pressure.

It helps to decide what your budget covers:

  • one bottle;
  • several bottles for a gathering;
  • a weekly or monthly amount; or
  • the whole occasion, including food and delivery.

This prevents a common mistake: choosing a bottle price before working out how much wine you actually need.

Adjust the budget for the occasion

Everyday drinking

For a regular meal, prioritise reliability, food compatibility and whether you genuinely like the style. A wine does not need to be prestigious to do its job well.

If you often enjoy a particular grape variety, region or style, that information may be more useful than simply moving to a higher price. Keeping notes about bottles you enjoy can also make future buying easier.

Entertaining

When hosting, work out the likely quantity first. The total cost of several suitable bottles matters more than choosing one bottle at a price that makes the whole occasion uncomfortable.

Consider the food, the range of guests’ tastes and whether the wine is the main feature of the event. A balanced selection that suits the meal may be more successful than spending heavily on one bottle.

Buying a gift

For a gift, budget is only one part of the decision. The recipient’s preferences, the occasion and the presentation may matter just as much.

A higher price may reflect reputation, rarity, provenance or packaging. Those qualities can make a gift feel significant, but they still do not guarantee that the recipient will enjoy the wine. If you know what they like, that knowledge is often more useful than a higher price.

A milestone or special occasion

Spending more can make sense when you value something specific: a particular origin, a mature style, limited availability or the significance attached to the bottle.

The important question is not “How expensive can I go?” but “What am I paying for, and does that matter here?” If the answer is only status or an assumption that price guarantees quality, pause before increasing the budget.

What are you paying for?

The price on an Australian shelf can reflect many parts of the journey from vineyard to consumer, including:

  • grape growing and harvesting;
  • winemaking labour and equipment;
  • maturation and storage;
  • bottles, closures, labels and cartons;
  • freight, warehousing and distribution;
  • retailer operating costs and margins;
  • producer reputation, scarcity and demand; and
  • taxes.

These factors do not apply in the same way to every bottle. A more expensive wine may involve more costly production, but it may also cost more because it is made in small quantities, has a strong reputation or travels through a more expensive route to market.

Wine Equalisation Tax, or WET, is one part of the Australian pricing picture. The Australian Taxation Office says WET is charged at 29% of the wholesale sale value and is generally included in the price retailers pay. It is not accurate to describe WET as simply adding 29% to every bottle’s shelf price.

A bottle-shop or supermarket price is also not directly comparable with a winery cellar-door or restaurant price. Different channels can involve different distribution, service and operating costs. Restaurant pricing includes a hospitality setting, so it should not be used as a straightforward benchmark for what a bottle ought to cost at retail.

How to choose within your budget

Once you have set a limit, ask:

  1. What style do I want? Think about red, white, sparkling, sweet or another style, as well as body and sweetness.
  2. What food will it accompany? A wine that fits the meal may be better value than a more prestigious wine that clashes with it.
  3. What information helps me predict enjoyment? Grape variety, region, producer information and label details can provide useful clues.
  4. Does the vintage matter? It can be relevant for some wines, but it is not always the most important buying factor.
  5. Can I ask for guidance? A bottle-shop employee may help you compare suitable options within your stated budget.
  6. Can I taste before buying? A tasting opportunity can tell you more about your preference than a price tag alone.
  7. Am I paying for something I value? That might be provenance, rarity, ageability, presentation or simply a style you know you enjoy.

Think of value as a combination of enjoyment, suitability, reliability and comfort with the money spent. The cheapest bottle is not automatically the best value, and the most expensive one is not automatically the best wine for you.

The takeaway

There is no magic price at which wine becomes “good”. Set a comfortable budget, allow for the occasion and quantity, then compare wines within that limit using information, advice and your own taste.

The right amount to spend is the amount that gives you an enjoyable, suitable bottle without creating financial pressure.

Frequently asked questions

Does expensive wine always taste better?

No. Higher price has a moderate relationship with quality ratings across large groups of wines, but it is not a guarantee for an individual bottle or drinker.

Is cheap wine always poor quality?

No. A lower-priced wine can be enjoyable and appropriate for an everyday meal or gathering. Price alone cannot determine whether it suits your taste.

Should I spend more for a gift?

Only if the additional qualities matter: perhaps the recipient values a particular origin, reputation, rarity or presentation. A higher price does not guarantee a better gift.

Is winery cellar-door wine always cheaper?

Not necessarily. Prices vary according to production and distribution arrangements, so the channel alone does not establish better value.

How much should I spend when hosting?

Start with the number of guests and bottles needed, then choose suitable wines within a total amount you can comfortably afford. There is no universal per-bottle figure that works for every gathering.
