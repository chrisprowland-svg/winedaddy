How to order wine confidently

Highlights

  • You do not need to know every grape, region or producer to order wine well.
  • Tell the staff about your food, budget, group, quantity and preferred style.
  • Plain words such as “fresh”, “richer”, “lighter”, “drier” and “less tannic” are useful.
  • Ask for two or three options rather than asking for “the best wine”.
  • Check the bottle before it is opened, especially the producer, wine, vintage and size.
  • The tasting pour is mainly a check for condition and correctness—not a test of whether you personally love the wine.
  • If something seems wrong, explain it calmly and let the service team assess it.
  • Responsible drinking and your own limits matter more than finishing a bottle.

Quick answer: how do you order wine confidently?

Ordering wine confidently is mostly a communication skill. You do not need expert knowledge or a perfect vocabulary. Give the server or sommelier—if the venue has one—five useful pieces of information:

  1. What you are eating.
  2. How many people want wine.
  3. Whether you want glasses or a bottle.
  4. Your budget in Australian dollars.
  5. The style you enjoy, or want to avoid.

For example:

“We’re sharing seafood and would like a fresh, dry white, not too rich. We’d like to stay around $75 for a bottle. Could you suggest two options?”

A sommelier is a wine-focused professional who may manage a venue’s list and help guests choose and serve wine. Many Australian restaurants do not have a dedicated sommelier, so a waiter, supervisor, manager or other service staff member may help instead.

The aim is not to sound knowledgeable. It is to give enough information for the staff to narrow the list to a few realistic choices.

Before you look at the wine list

Start with the practical constraints. How many people are likely to drink? Would glasses give the group more flexibility, or would a bottle suit everyone? What food has been ordered? Is there a firm budget, such as “around $70” or “up to $100”?

It also helps to mention broad preferences:

  • light rather than full-bodied;
  • fresh or crisp rather than rich;
  • dry rather than sweet;
  • fruity but not sweet;
  • softer tannins;
  • lower or higher alcohol, where relevant.

These words describe different things. “Dry” generally refers to the absence of noticeable sweetness. “Fruity” describes an aroma or flavour impression, so a wine can be fruity and still be dry. “Body” describes how substantial the wine feels, while acidity can create freshness and tannin can create a drying or grippy sensation.

If you want to understand these terms more fully, see our guides to wine body, acidity, tannins and sweetness.

Ask for help in plain language

A focused question is easier to answer than “What’s good?” Try:

“Could you suggest something light and fresh, around $60, for our shared dishes?”

“We prefer a medium-bodied red with softer tannins. Which two options should we consider?”

“Is there an aromatic, fruity white that is still dry?”

You can also ask which option best suits the food you have ordered. Pairing is a useful starting point, not a test with one objectively correct answer. Our guide to how wine pairing works explains the basic idea without requiring you to memorise rules.

If the group has different preferences, say so:

“Some of us like lighter reds and some prefer richer styles. Is there a bottle somewhere in the middle, or would glasses be more practical?”

You are allowed to take a moment. A wine list may be organised by colour, grape, region, style, price or the venue’s own system. You do not need to decode every line.

What to check on the wine list

For most orders, look for only the information that affects your decision:

  • producer or brand;
  • grape variety or named style;
  • region;
  • vintage, where shown and relevant;
  • bottle size;
  • price;
  • whether it is available by the glass.

A vintage is the year the grapes were harvested. It matters more for some wines than others, so do not assume that every wine should be judged mainly by its year.

Wine lists and prices vary between venues. Bottle availability, glass pours, corkage, minimum spends, service charges and substitution policies may also differ. Confirm anything important directly with the restaurant. A higher price does not automatically mean a wine will be better for your group or more enjoyable with your meal. For more on this, read why wine prices vary and how to compare wine value.

By the glass or by the bottle?

Ordering by the glass can make sense when people want different wines, when only one or two people are drinking, or when the group wants to keep the quantity flexible.

Useful questions include:

“Which wines are available by the glass?”

“How large is the pour?”

“Would two glasses or a bottle make more sense for us?”

“Is this bottle likely to be finished today, or would glasses be better?”

There is no universal freshness period or pour size across Australian venues. If freshness matters, ask politely rather than assuming that every restaurant uses the same preservation method or turnover practice.

A bottle can be convenient for a group with similar tastes, but it may contain more alcohol than one person wants to drink. Consider the group’s plans, pace and limits before ordering.

Check the bottle before it is opened

When the bottle arrives, compare it with your order. Check the producer, wine or grape, region where relevant, vintage where relevant, bottle size and any important price difference.

If it is not what you ordered, say so before it is opened where possible:

“Sorry, could we check this against the wine we ordered? I thought we chose the 2022 vintage.”

This is a normal service check, not an accusation. Wine-list descriptions and availability can change, so clarify any discrepancy early.

What does the tasting pour mean?

The tasting pour is primarily a condition check. Swirl gently, smell the wine and take a small sip. Ask yourself whether it seems sound and whether it matches what was ordered—not simply whether it is your favourite style.

A wine can be sound but not suit your personal taste. Being unfamiliar with a grape, region or style does not necessarily mean the wine is faulty.

If the wine seems unusually musty, flat, oxidised or otherwise wrong, explain what you notice without pretending to diagnose it with certainty:

“This smells unusually musty to me. Could you please check whether the bottle is sound?”

Wine faults can include cork taint or oxidation. The service team can assess the bottle and decide how to proceed. Do not feel obliged to suffer in silence, but do not treat the tasting pour as permission to reject any sound wine simply because you have changed your mind.

If the bottle is clearly different from the order, say that directly. If it is merely not your preferred style, the outcome may depend on the venue’s policy and the circumstances.

Responsible service in Australia

Alcohol is never completely risk-free, and drinking less reduces risk. The Australian Government’s current health guidance explains standard drinks and recommended limits for healthy adults. Guidance can change, and those figures should not be treated as a personalised safe allowance.

An Australian standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol, but a restaurant glass may contain more than one standard drink depending on its size and the wine’s alcohol percentage. You can check the current advice at health.gov.au.

Eat, pace yourself, drink water and consider a non-alcoholic option if that suits you. Australian liquor laws and responsible-service obligations are administered through state and territory systems. Venues must follow the rules that apply where they operate, and service practices vary.

A reusable ordering script

Keep this template ready:

“We’re eating [food]. We’d like [glasses or a bottle], prefer [style or descriptors], and want to stay around [$X]. Could you suggest two suitable options?”

That is enough. Confidence comes from explaining what you want, asking clear questions and checking the service—not from performing expertise.
