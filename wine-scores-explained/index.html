Wine Scores Explained: What Do Wine Ratings Really Mean?

Learn what wine scores mean, how critics and judging panels create them, why ratings vary, and how to use scores alongside tasting notes, price and personal preference.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • A wine score is an evaluator’s judgement, not an objective measurement of enjoyment.
  • The 100-point scale is common, but 20-point, five-star and other systems also exist.
  • Score bands are not universal: each critic, publication or competition may define them differently.
  • Blind tasting can reduce some bias, but it does not make wine judging perfectly objective.
  • A medal or trophy is a competition award, not automatically the same as a critic’s score.
  • The best way to use a score is alongside the tasting note, wine style, vintage, price and your own preferences.

Quick answer: what is a wine score?

A wine score is a numerical summary of how a critic, publication, judging panel, competition or user platform evaluates a wine under particular conditions.

It may reflect qualities such as aroma, flavour, balance, structure, texture, intensity, complexity, typicity, faults and overall impression. But there is no single checklist or weighting system used everywhere. A score only makes full sense when you know who gave it, which scale they used and how the wine was assessed.

That is why a 95-point score is not a scientific measurement of a wine, and why two reputable evaluators can score the same bottle differently. A score can be useful for comparing wines within one source or tasting context, but it cannot guarantee that you will enjoy the wine, that it will suit dinner, or that it represents good value.

How wine scores are created

A typical evaluation begins with a tasting context. Wines may be grouped by variety, region, style or vintage, or they may be assessed more independently.

The evaluator then examines and tastes the wine, recording sensory observations and an initial judgement. In a panel, judges may compare impressions, discuss differences, reach a consensus, average scores or conduct another tasting. The final result might be published as a number and tasting note, or recorded as points, a medal or a trophy.

This is a useful general outline, not a universal procedure. A critic’s individual score, a publication’s editorial result and a wine-show panel’s outcome are different kinds of evidence.

What does blind tasting change?

Blind tasting limits selected identity information—often the producer or brand—to reduce the influence of reputation, price or packaging. It does not always mean that judges know nothing. Depending on the system, they may still know the region, variety, style or price bracket.

Blind tasting can make an assessment fairer, but it does not remove every source of variation. Tasters have different sensory sensitivities, experience and ideas about what makes a wine successful.

Tasting order matters too. Flight order, palate fatigue, adaptation, serving temperature, glassware, bottle condition and the number of wines being assessed can all affect the experience. Coded glasses, randomised order, controlled presentation and regular breaks are ways tasting organisers may reduce some of these effects.

What do 90, 95 and 100 points mean?

The number depends on the source. Halliday Wine Companion and Decanter both use 100-point systems, but their published descriptions of score bands are not identical. An Australian wine show may use either a 20-point or 100-point system under Australian Society of Viticulture and Oenology guidance, with different medal thresholds for each scale.

So there is no universal dictionary in which every score has one fixed meaning. Do not assume that a 90-point wine means exactly the same thing everywhere, or that a 20-point score can always be converted precisely into a 100-point score.

A five-star rating or a user score also should not be mathematically converted unless the source clearly explains how its system works. Read the rating in context and look for the source’s own scale description.

Score, tasting note, medal or user rating?

These terms are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Wine score

A numerical evaluation that condenses an assessor’s judgement within a particular framework.

Tasting note

A written description of the wine’s aromas, flavours, structure, texture, balance, development and other characteristics. It gives you information that a number cannot provide by itself.

Medal

An award such as bronze, silver or gold, given under the rules of a particular competition or wine show. The threshold and judging process can vary.

Trophy

A higher-level award within a particular show or competition, usually selected from eligible medal or class winners under that event’s rules. It is not a universal measure of quality.

User rating

An opinion or aggregate rating submitted by consumers. It may reflect enjoyment, value, purchase satisfaction or perceived quality rather than a professional sensory assessment. User ratings can be useful, but they answer a different question from a critic or judging panel.

Australian readers may encounter all of these in specialist wine media, bottle shops, retailer listings, producer material, wine-show results and online review platforms. A medal or trophy from an Australian wine show means recognition under that show’s rules. It is not automatically equivalent to a critic’s numerical review score.

Why can the same wine receive different scores?

Score variation is normal. It may result from:

  • different preferences or sensory sensitivities;
  • different views about balance, intensity, complexity or typicity;
  • blind versus non-blind tasting;
  • the wine’s position in a flight;
  • panel discussion, averaging or consensus;
  • bottle variation or the wine’s stage of development;
  • different scoring scales and definitions;
  • different expectations for the style.

A disagreement does not necessarily mean that one evaluator is careless. It may show that wine assessment combines observation with interpretation.

How should you use a wine score?

Treat a score as one helpful clue, not the final answer.

Before buying a wine, ask:

  1. Who gave the score? Is it a critic, publication, judging panel, retailer, producer or user community?
  2. What scale is being used? Does the source explain its score bands?
  3. What does the tasting note say? Look for descriptions that match flavours and textures you enjoy.
  4. Does the style suit you? A high score cannot make a full-bodied red feel light, or a dry white taste sweet.
  5. Does vintage or release stage matter? The same wine can show differently over time.
  6. Does the price fit the occasion? A score does not automatically establish value.
  7. Do you trust the source’s palate? If its recommendations have suited you before, its scores may be more useful to you.

A high score can suggest that an evaluator found notable qualities in the wine. It cannot predict your personal enjoyment with certainty. Your preferences, food, company, serving temperature and occasion remain part of the experience.

Common questions

Is a 95-point wine always better than a 90-point wine?

Not in any universal sense. The numbers may come from different sources, scales or tasting contexts. Even within one source, a higher score does not guarantee that the wine will suit your taste or occasion.

Is a gold medal the same as a 95-point score?

No. A gold medal is an award from a particular competition. A 95-point score is a numerical evaluation from a particular reviewer, publication or judging framework. Some competitions connect points with medals, but the systems should not be treated as interchangeable.

Do wine scores predict value?

No. Value involves price, expectations, availability and personal priorities. A high score does not automatically mean a wine is good value.

Are user ratings reliable?

They can be useful for understanding consumer reaction, but user ratings vary in sample size, reviewer experience, verification and purpose. They should be read as a different type of evidence from professional judging.

Should I ignore wine scores?

Not necessarily. A score can help you discover wines and compare bottles within a trusted source. Just read the note, check the context and use the number as one part of your decision.
