Amontillado vs Oloroso: What’s the Difference?

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Amontillado and Oloroso are both styles of Sherry, the fortified wine associated with the Jerez-Xérès-Sherry protected designation in southern Spain. They are not grape varieties.

The simplest difference is how they age:

  • Amontillado begins under flor, a layer of yeast that grows on the wine’s surface, and later continues ageing with oxygen.
  • Oloroso is aged oxidatively from the outset, without an active flor phase.

That difference creates two distinctive directions in the glass. Amontillado is often savoury, nutty and relatively lifted, while Oloroso is commonly fuller, darker and more powerful. These are useful tendencies, not rigid rules.

What is flor?

Flor is a layer of yeast that develops across the surface of wine under suitable cellar conditions. It limits the wine’s direct contact with oxygen and contributes flavours associated with biological ageing.

In Amontillado, the wine spends an initial period beneath flor. Later, the flor disappears, is removed or is no longer maintained, and the wine continues ageing oxidatively. In other words, Amontillado carries the story of two ageing environments: first biological, then oxidative.

Oloroso follows a different path. Its wine is fortified for oxidative ageing, commonly to around 17% alcohol at that stage, which prevents flor from developing. The wine then matures in contact with oxygen. The official style definition is the important point: Oloroso is aged exclusively oxidatively.

Oxidative ageing is not the same as a wine accidentally becoming stale or spoiled. In Sherry production, oxygen exposure is a deliberate part of the style.

How do they taste?

Amontillado often sits between the lighter, flor-driven character of biologically aged Sherry and the deeper character of oxidative ageing. You might notice savoury, saline, hazelnut-like, herbal, tobacco or spice notes. It can feel dry, firm and finely textured.

Oloroso usually has more weight and breadth. Its aromas may suggest walnut, roasted flavours, spice, dried fruit or caramelised notes. On the palate, it often feels rounder, fuller and more powerful.

But colour and body are not legal definitions. A mature Amontillado can be deeply coloured and richly flavoured, while an Oloroso may show considerable freshness. Ageing time, blending, producer decisions and sweetness all influence the final wine.

So “Amontillado is light and Oloroso is heavy” is a helpful first approximation, not a rule.

Are Amontillado and Oloroso sweet?

Not necessarily. This is one of the most useful distinctions for a new Sherry drinker.

Traditional Amontillado and Oloroso in the regulated dry category are dry wines. Dryness refers mainly to the amount of residual sugar; it does not mean the wine must taste light or delicate. A dry Oloroso can taste rich, nutty and intense without being sweet.

Some Sherries are sweetened or blended and may carry additional terms such as Medium or Cream. Those words provide important label clues, but they should not be treated as perfect one-to-one translations of a particular dry style. Check the full label or importer’s description rather than guessing from the words Amontillado or Oloroso alone.

A rich flavour is not proof of sweetness. This is the same reason an intensely flavoured dry wine can still be dry.

Which one should you choose?

Choose Amontillado if you enjoy:

  • savoury, nutty and more layered flavours;
  • a dry wine with a finer or more lifted feel;
  • mushrooms, poultry, cured meats, richer fish or umami-rich dishes.

Choose Oloroso if you enjoy:

  • fuller-bodied, broader wines;
  • roasted, walnut-like and oxidative flavours;
  • braises, smoked foods, roast meats or stronger cheeses.

These are starting points rather than strict pairing laws. The dish’s preparation, salt, sweetness and intensity matter just as much as the main ingredient.

Both styles are often served cool or chilled, although the best temperature can depend on the wine and your preference. For more practical guidance, see WineDaddy’s guide to wine serving temperature and wine and cheese pairing.

What will an Australian drinker see on the label?

Imported Sherry may be found through bottle shops, restaurants and online retailers, but availability varies by importer, retailer, state and time. Product descriptions may mention terms such as dry, Medium, Cream, solera or other ageing information.

Treat these terms as separate pieces of information:

  • Amontillado or Oloroso describes the Sherry style and ageing pathway.
  • Dry, Medium or Cream gives information about sweetness or category.
  • Solera describes a fractional blending and maturation system used for some Sherry wines; it does not, by itself, tell you whether the wine is Amontillado or Oloroso.

Australian fortified wines, including Rutherglen Muscat and tawny-style wines, may share ideas such as fortification or oxidative ageing. They are not equivalent to Amontillado or Oloroso. They can use different grapes and belong to different regional and regulatory traditions.

The short answer

Amontillado starts with flor and finishes with oxidative ageing. Oloroso is oxidative from the beginning. Amontillado often tastes more savoury and finely textured; Oloroso commonly tastes fuller and more overtly nutty or roasted. Neither name automatically means sweet, and neither style should be judged by colour alone.

If you are comparing two bottles, begin with the ageing pathway, then check the sweetness information and choose the profile that sounds more appealing. There is no correct winner—only the style that better suits your palate and the food in front of you.

Frequently asked questions

Is Amontillado a grape variety?

No. Amontillado is a regulated Sherry style.

Is Oloroso always sweet?

No. Traditional Oloroso is commonly a dry style. Some Sherries are sweetened or blended, so check the full label.

Is Amontillado lighter than Oloroso?

Often, but not always. Amontillado commonly feels finer and more lifted, while Oloroso often feels fuller. Ageing and sweetness can change the comparison.

Does Oloroso contain flor?

Oloroso is defined by exclusive oxidative ageing rather than an active flor-aged phase. Fortification commonly prevents flor from developing.

Are Australian tawny wines the same as Oloroso?

No. They may share broad production ideas, but they come from different grapes, places and regulatory traditions.
