What is flor ageing?

Flor ageing is wine matured beneath a living film of yeast. Learn how flor works, how it changes wine and why it is associated with Fino and Manzanilla.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Flor is a living film of yeast that grows on the surface of some wines.
  • Flor ageing is a type of biological ageing, not simply ordinary barrel ageing.
  • The yeast changes the wine while limiting its direct contact with air.
  • Flor needs suitable wine and cellar conditions; it will not develop in every wine.
  • Fino and Manzanilla are the best-known styles associated with flor ageing.
  • Flor ageing is different from oxidative ageing, lees ageing and solera ageing.

Quick answer

Flor ageing is the maturation of wine beneath a living surface layer of yeast called flor. After alcoholic fermentation has finished, the yeast can rise to the wine’s surface and form a film when the wine and cellar conditions are suitable.

The flor remains in contact with the wine during maturation. It uses oxygen at the surface and interacts with components of the wine, while reducing the wine’s direct exposure to air. This biological activity can alter the wine’s aroma, flavour and texture, and often helps white wines retain a relatively pale appearance.

Flor ageing is most strongly associated with Sherry styles such as Fino and Manzanilla.

How does flor ageing work?

First, the wine completes alcoholic fermentation. It is then kept in a suitable vessel and environment, where flor yeast may rise and form a veil across the wine’s surface.

The yeast needs access to oxygen, but the resulting film changes how the wine interacts with air. Once established, flor reduces the wine’s direct contact with air and consumes some dissolved oxygen. At the same time, the yeast remains biologically active, transforming some wine components as maturation continues.

The result is not just wine stored in a cask. It is wine being changed by a living layer at the wine–air interface.

Flor growth and persistence depend on several factors, including the wine’s alcohol, nutrients and composition, as well as temperature, humidity, oxygen availability, vessel conditions and cellar management. There is no single universal recipe that makes flor develop in every wine.

What does flor ageing do to wine?

Flor can contribute to distinctive changes in aroma, flavour and texture. It can also help preserve a pale colour in white wines by limiting the direct oxidation that would otherwise tend to deepen their colour.

These are broad effects, not guaranteed tasting notes. Flor-aged wines do not all taste identical, because the wine, yeast, vessel, environment and length of maturation can vary.

The word biological refers to the active role of the yeast. Flor is not merely a layer of sediment left behind after fermentation.

Flor ageing versus other kinds of ageing

Oxidative ageing deliberately allows wine to mature in direct contact with air without a protective flor layer. Flor ageing still involves oxygen—the yeast needs it—but the film changes the wine’s exposure and protects it from direct contact with air. The two methods can therefore produce quite different colours, aromas and flavours.

Lees ageing means maturing wine on settled yeast deposits, usually at the bottom of a vessel. Flor is different: it is a living surface film.

Solera ageing refers to a system of fractional blending and replenishment between groups of casks or containers. A solera system can be used alongside flor ageing, particularly in the Sherry context, but solera is not the yeast layer itself.

Where might you encounter flor?

Fino and Manzanilla are the clearest examples. In their recognised Sherry context, these wines are aged beneath a veil of flor. Some other styles may involve biological ageing for part of their maturation before moving to oxidative ageing.

Australian drinkers may see terms such as “flor”, “biological ageing”, “Fino”, “Manzanilla” or “Sherry” on wine lists, in education or on imported-wine labels. “Sherry” is connected with protected wine geographical-indication rules, so a wine made in Australia cannot automatically use that name simply because it has been aged under flor. Exact labelling terms should be checked against the current Australian Wine Register.

The simplest way to remember flor ageing is this: the wine matures under a living veil of yeast, rather than being left fully exposed to air.

Frequently asked questions

Is flor a mould?

No. Flor is a film of living yeast. It is not mould or bacteria.

Does flor ageing prevent all oxidation?

No. Flor needs oxygen and interacts with dissolved oxygen. It reduces direct air contact and direct oxidation; it does not create an oxygen-free system.

Can every wine be aged under flor?

No. Flor develops and persists only when the wine and surrounding conditions are suitable.

Are flor ageing and solera ageing the same?

No. Flor is the biological yeast film. A solera is a fractional blending and replenishment system that may be used during maturation.
