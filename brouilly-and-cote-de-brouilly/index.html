Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly: understanding two Beaujolais appellations

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly are two separate protected French appellations, not two names for the same wine.
  • Both are in Beaujolais, in France’s Rhône department, and are centred on Mont Brouilly.
  • Brouilly covers the broader area around the hill; Côte de Brouilly is the more restricted appellation on the hill’s slopes.
  • Both appellations are reserved for still red wine, principally made from Gamay N.
  • The names describe geographic origin and production rules, not sweetness, grape variety or a guaranteed flavour profile.
  • Australian Gamay can resemble these wines, but it cannot be labelled Brouilly or Côte de Brouilly simply because it uses the same grape.

Quick answer: what is the difference?

Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly are separate Beaujolais cru appellations. In French wine, an appellation is a legally protected name linked to a defined place and a set of production rules.

The easiest way to picture the relationship is this:

  • Brouilly is the larger appellation around Mont Brouilly, including lower slopes and surrounding areas.
  • Côte de Brouilly is the smaller, slope-focused appellation associated with vineyard parcels on Mont Brouilly itself.

They overlap across some of the same communes, but they do not have identical boundaries. Côte de Brouilly is not a sub-appellation of Brouilly, and it is not legally a superior version. Each is its own protected appellation.

Both sit within the broader Beaujolais wine region of France. On a bottle, the name Brouilly or Côte de Brouilly therefore tells you more about origin than about a fixed taste. Both are red-wine appellations, and both are principally associated with Gamay.

Where are Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly?

For an Australian reader, the geographic hierarchy is:

France → Beaujolais → Brouilly or Côte de Brouilly

Both appellations are in the Rhône department, around the distinctive hill of Mont Brouilly in southern Beaujolais. Useful local orientation points include Odenas, Saint-Lager, Quincié-en-Beaujolais and Cercié. Belleville-en-Beaujolais is a practical nearby regional centre for orientation.

From Lyon, the area is roughly 50 kilometres to the north by road, or around 40–45 minutes in favourable traffic. That is a travel orientation, not a precise promise: the route and starting point will affect the time.

Brouilly includes parts of six communes:

  • Cercié
  • Charentay
  • Odenas
  • Quincié-en-Beaujolais
  • Saint-Étienne-la-Varenne
  • Saint-Lager

Côte de Brouilly includes parts of four:

  • Cercié
  • Odenas
  • Quincié-en-Beaujolais
  • Saint-Lager

The shared commune names can make the relationship seem simple, but the legal appellation areas are separately defined at vineyard and parcel level. “Côte de Brouilly is on the hill” is useful shorthand for orientation; it should not be treated as a replacement for the official boundaries.

What do the names mean on a label?

If a French label says Brouilly, it refers to wine made within the defined Brouilly appellation under its relevant rules.

If it says Côte de Brouilly, it refers to the separate Côte de Brouilly appellation.

Neither name is:

  • a grape variety;
  • a sweetness level;
  • a winemaking method;
  • a general name for any Gamay from France;
  • an Australian geographical indication.

This is where French AOC and AOP terminology helps. AOC, or Appellation d’Origine Contrôlée, is the French controlled-origin designation. AOP, Appellation d’Origine Protégée, is the corresponding protected-origin terminology used within the European Union.

The name on the bottle is therefore a promise about protected origin and compliance with appellation rules. It is not a guarantee that every Brouilly will taste the same.

For an Australian comparison, an Australian wine made from Gamay may use the grape name, an Australian geographical indication or other permitted descriptive wording. It should not be presented as Brouilly or Côte de Brouilly merely because it resembles a French example. This is the practical meaning of protected wine names.

What grapes and wines are permitted?

Both Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly are reserved for still red wines.

Gamay N is the principal grape variety for both appellations. The regulations also permit limited accessory varieties, including Aligoté B, Chardonnay B and Melon B, subject to the relevant restrictions. It is therefore safer to say “principally Gamay” than to assume every qualifying wine must be described simply as 100% Gamay.

This distinction matters because the appellation name and the grape name answer different questions:

  • Gamay tells you about the principal grape.
  • Brouilly or Côte de Brouilly tells you about protected place and rules.

You can learn more about the grape separately in the Gamay guide.

How do Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly tend to taste?

Regional descriptions commonly present Brouilly as fruit-forward, rounded and supple, with red-fruit character and relatively approachable tannins. Côte de Brouilly is often described as showing more freshness and firmness, with possible floral, mineral or peppery notes alongside red or darker fruit.

These are useful starting points, not tasting laws.

Wine character can vary with vineyard parcel, aspect, vine age, farming, harvest conditions, producer decisions and winemaking. A Côte de Brouilly is not automatically more powerful or longer-lived than a Brouilly, just as Brouilly is not always light or simple.

The hill, slopes, soils and exposures provide useful context for understanding why wines from nearby places may differ. Brouilly’s landscape includes varied geological materials, including granite, calcareous stones, marl, clay and other slope or sedimentary materials. Côte de Brouilly is also geologically varied rather than being defined by one uniform soil.

It is better to say that these physical differences may contribute to wine character than that a particular soil automatically creates a particular flavour.

How do they compare with Beaujolais more broadly?

“Beaujolais” can refer to the wider wine region and to appellation names within its system. Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly are among the region’s named crus: more geographically specific protected appellations rather than generic descriptions of red wine made anywhere in the region.

A useful label-reading sequence is:

  1. Beaujolais tells you the broader regional context.
  2. Brouilly or Côte de Brouilly identifies a specific protected appellation.
  3. Gamay identifies the principal grape associated with these wines.
  4. The producer, vintage and other label details help explain the individual bottle.

Do not confuse these names with Burgundy’s Côte de Nuits, Côte de Beaune or Côte Chalonnaise. They are separate geographic wine designations. For a first understanding, lead with Beaujolais, not Burgundy.

Serving and food

The regional style descriptions make these wines useful with food, especially when you want a red that does not overwhelm the dish. A slightly cool serving temperature—about 13–15°C—can suit many examples, particularly fruit-driven bottles.

Broad pairing starting points include:

  • roast chicken and other poultry;
  • charcuterie;
  • mushrooms;
  • roast vegetables;
  • lighter grilled foods.

These are starting points rather than rules. A more structured Côte de Brouilly may suit a richer dish, while a supple Brouilly can be enjoyable with lighter food. The individual wine matters more than the appellation name alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is Côte de Brouilly a better version of Brouilly?

No. They are separate appellations, not a simple quality ladder. Côte de Brouilly is the more restricted, slope-focused appellation, while Brouilly covers a broader area around Mont Brouilly. Style and quality vary within both.

Are Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly made from Gamay?

Gamay N is the principal permitted grape for both. Limited accessory varieties are also allowed under the appellation rules, so “principally Gamay” is the most useful general explanation.

Are Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly the same as Beaujolais?

No. They are specific Beaujolais cru appellations within the broader Beaujolais region and appellation system.

Can an Australian wine be called Brouilly?

Not simply because it is made from Gamay or tastes similar. Brouilly and Côte de Brouilly are protected French geographic names connected to defined areas and production rules.

Should I expect Côte de Brouilly always to be more structured?

Not always. It is often described as fresher or firmer than Brouilly, but producer, parcel, vintage and winemaking choices can produce considerable variation.

Are these sweet wines?

No. Both appellations are reserved for still red wines. The appellation name does not indicate sweetness.
