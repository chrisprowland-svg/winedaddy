Champagne styles and classifications: a beginner’s guide

Understand Champagne’s principal styles and label terms, including vintage, blanc de blancs, rosé, dosage categories and producer codes, with clear Australian context.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Champagne is a protected French appellation, not a generic word for sparkling wine.
  • Non-vintage Champagne can blend wines from several harvest years; vintage Champagne comes from one stated harvest year.
  • Blanc de blancs is made only from white-skinned grapes; blanc de noirs is white Champagne made only from dark-skinned grapes.
  • Rosé Champagne describes colour and production method, not sweetness.
  • Brut, extra-brut and demi-sec describe residual sugar levels, not quality.
  • RM, NM and CM identify different producer or sourcing models, not quality rankings.
  • A Champagne label gives you useful clues, but no single term guarantees a particular taste or level of quality.

Quick answer: what do the terms mean?

The principal Champagne styles and classification terms describe four things: where the wine comes from, what grapes or colour it has, whether it comes from one harvest year, how sweet it is, and who made or sourced it.

Champagne is the name of a protected French appellation. In other words, it identifies qualifying sparkling wine from the Champagne region made under its appellation rules. Australian sparkling wine can use similar grapes, blending and bottle-fermentation techniques, but it is not Champagne. For Australian readers, the appropriate terms are Australian sparkling wine or, where accurate, traditional-method sparkling wine. Wine Australia identifies Champagne as a protected geographical indication and states that “Champagne method” is not permitted as an Australian descriptor.

The easiest way to understand a label is to read each term as a clue rather than a score. For example, a bottle could be a vintage blanc de blancs brut, or a non-vintage rosé extra-brut. Each word adds a different piece of information.

Champagne’s main style terms

Non-vintage and vintage

Non-vintage Champagne may blend wines from more than one harvest year. This allows a producer to create a consistent house or producer style despite differences between growing seasons. “Non-vintage” does not mean inferior or immature.

Vintage Champagne is made from grapes harvested in one stated year. If a year appears on the label, it indicates a vintage wine. Vintage status can make the character of that harvest more apparent, but it is not an automatic quality grade.

Champagne’s minimum maturation requirements also differ. Non-vintage Champagne must mature for at least 15 months, while vintage Champagne must mature for at least three years before release. Producers may mature wines for longer.

For a broader explanation of the distinction, see vintage wine versus non-vintage wine.

Blanc de blancs

Blanc de blancs means “white of whites”. It is Champagne made exclusively from white-skinned grape varieties. Chardonnay is the principal variety associated with this style, although other authorised white varieties may also be used.

Blanc de blancs often suggests freshness, citrus, floral notes and a lifted feel. With extended ageing, it may also develop creamier, nuttier or toast-like characters. These are broad tendencies, not rules: a blanc de blancs is not automatically delicate, austere or superior.

Blanc de noirs

Blanc de noirs means “white of blacks”. It is white Champagne made exclusively from dark-skinned grapes, principally Pinot Noir and/or Meunier.

Careful pressing can produce white juice from dark grapes before substantial colour is extracted. Blanc de noirs may give an impression of greater body, structure or red-fruit character than some blanc de blancs, but the final result also depends on blending, ageing, dosage and producer decisions.

It is not red Champagne. The term describes a white wine made from dark grapes.

Rosé Champagne

Rosé Champagne can be made in two principal ways:

  • through limited contact between dark grape skins and juice; or
  • by blending white Champagne with a proportion of red Champagne wine.

The Comité Champagne describes the blending method as involving approximately 5–20% red wine. Rosé is a colour and production distinction, not a sweetness category. A rosé can also be brut nature, extra-brut, brut or demi-sec.

Dosage and sweetness terms

Dosage is the final adjustment made before the bottle is closed. It can involve adding a small amount of dosage, or expedition, liqueur containing wine and sugar. The finished wine’s residual sugar determines its sweetness designation.

The sequence runs from driest to sweetest:

Label term Broad residual-sugar category
Brut nature, pas dosé or dosage zéro Less than 3 g/L, with no added dosage liqueur
Extra-brut Approximately 0–6 g/L
Brut Less than 12 g/L
Extra-dry 12–17 g/L
Sec 17–32 g/L
Demi-sec 32–50 g/L
Doux More than 50 g/L

The exact presentation of the extra-brut boundary varies between Comité Champagne consumer materials, so the table uses a cautious “approximately” wording.

Two points often surprise beginners:

  1. Extra-dry is sweeter than brut. The name sounds drier, but it sits further along the sweetness scale.
  2. Brut does not mean sugar-free. It is a low-sugar category, but it can contain measurable residual sugar.

Dosage is not a quality ranking. Acidity, fruit ripeness, alcohol, serving temperature and bottle age also affect how sweet a wine tastes. Brut nature is not automatically more authentic or better; it is simply made without added dosage liqueur and has very little sugar.

Producer and sourcing codes

Champagne labels may include codes describing the organisation that sourced and made the wine. The most useful for beginners are:

  • RM — Récoltant Manipulant: a grower-producer making Champagne from their own harvest on their premises.
  • NM — Négociant Manipulant: a producer or company that may use its own grapes but can also buy grapes, must or wine, then make Champagne on its premises.
  • CM — Coopérative de Manipulation: a cooperative making wine from grapes supplied by its members.

Other codes include RC for a récoltant coopérateur, ND for a négociant distributeur, MA for a buyer’s or client’s brand, and SR for a société de récoltants.

These are sourcing and producer-status descriptions. They are not official quality tiers. An RM wine is not automatically better than an NM or CM wine, and the code cannot tell you whether you will prefer the wine.

How blending, ageing and dosage shape style

Champagne is strongly associated with assemblage, or blending. Wines may be combined across grape varieties, vineyard sources and harvest years to create a finished cuvée. The term cuvée can refer broadly to a particular blend or bottling; see what does cuvée mean?.

Grape varieties provide broad starting points. Chardonnay may contribute freshness, citrus and floral impressions. Pinot Noir may contribute structure and body, while Meunier may contribute fruitiness and roundness. These are tendencies rather than formulas.

Blending wines from different years can help establish a consistent non-vintage style. A vintage wine instead places more emphasis on one harvest. Maturation on lees—the yeast sediment left after fermentation—can contribute texture and developed bready, biscuit or autolytic notes. Dosage then helps set the finished wine’s sugar level and balance.

How to read a Champagne label

Try this sequence:

  1. Look for “Champagne”. This identifies the protected appellation. It should not be used as a generic name for Australian sparkling wine.
  2. Find the dosage term. Brut, extra-brut, demi-sec and the other terms indicate the residual-sugar category.
  3. Check for a harvest year. A stated year indicates vintage Champagne; no year generally indicates non-vintage.
  4. Look for style terms. Blanc de blancs, blanc de noirs and rosé tell you about grape composition or colour.
  5. Read the producer code. RM, NM, CM and related abbreviations indicate sourcing or producer structure.
  6. Check the back label. Grape varieties, disgorgement information and cuvée notes may provide additional context, although they are not universal.
  7. Treat the label as a guide, not a guarantee. These terms help set expectations but do not promise quality, flavour or value.

Champagne and Australian sparkling wine

For Australian shoppers, the practical distinction is simple: Champagne is a protected geographic name for qualifying French wine from Champagne. Australian sparkling wine may be made with similar varieties and by a comparable traditional method, but it remains Australian sparkling wine.

Technique and place are related but not interchangeable. Two wines can share bottle fermentation and lees ageing while having different geographic identities, growing conditions and legal names. This is why Australian labels and retailers should use lawful terms such as Australian sparkling wine or traditional-method sparkling wine, not Champagne or “Champagne method”.

Frequently asked questions

Is vintage Champagne always better?

No. Vintage identifies the harvest year and comes with a longer minimum maturation requirement, but it does not guarantee quality or suit every preference.

Is brut Champagne dry?

Brut is a low-sugar category, but it is not sugar-free. Extra-brut and brut nature contain less residual sugar.

Is blanc de blancs always made from Chardonnay?

No. Chardonnay is the principal variety, but other authorised white grape varieties may also be used.

Is blanc de noirs red Champagne?

No. It is white Champagne made exclusively from dark-skinned grapes.

Are grower and merchant Champagne quality classifications?

No. Codes such as RM and NM describe producer or sourcing models, not quality rankings.

Can Australian sparkling wine be called Champagne?

Champagne is a protected name. Australian products should be labelled Australian sparkling wine or another lawful generic description. This article is educational rather than legal advice; precise compliance questions should be checked against current Australian and French/EU requirements.
