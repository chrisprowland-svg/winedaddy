VDP Classifications: A Beginner’s Guide to German Wine Labels

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

VDP classifications are a voluntary German system for communicating the origin of wine. They are created and administered by the Verband Deutscher Prädikatsweingüter, usually called the VDP—a selective association of German wine estates.

The current VDP hierarchy has four levels:

  1. VDP.GUTSWEIN — estate-level wine
  2. VDP.ORTSWEIN — locality or village-level wine
  3. VDP.ERSTE LAGE® — first-class classified vineyard site
  4. VDP.GROSSE LAGE® — the VDP’s highest classified vineyard-site category

The key idea is simple: the more specific the stated origin, the higher the VDP category is intended to be. The system also includes rules around grape varieties, yields, harvesting, production, controls and release.

VDP is not part of German wine law. It is also not the same as German statutory terms such as Qualitätswein, Prädikatswein, Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese.

What the four VDP levels mean

VDP.GUTSWEIN

VDP.GUTSWEIN is the foundation level. The grapes must come from the member estate’s own vineyards. The VDP permits all grape varieties at this level, although at least 80% of the varieties grown should be typical for the region.

The maximum yield is 75 hectolitres per hectare.

For a label reader, VDP.GUTSWEIN broadly says: this is an estate wine from a VDP member, with its origin beginning at the estate or regional level. The designation may not always appear as a prominent front-label term.

VDP.ORTSWEIN

VDP.ORTSWEIN narrows the origin to one locality or village area. It comes from selected, traditional vineyards within that locality and uses regionally typical grape varieties.

The maximum yield is also 75 hectolitres per hectare.

“Village wine” is a useful explanation for Australian readers, but VDP.ORTSWEIN is the official term. It is a VDP category, not automatically a separate German legal category.

VDP.ERSTE LAGE®

VDP.ERSTE LAGE® identifies first-class classified vineyard sites with distinctive site character. The permitted grape varieties are defined regionally under VDP rules.

This is where the site itself becomes especially important. Rather than simply naming a broad region, the label points to a more tightly defined vineyard origin.

The VDP notes that some rules can vary according to regional criteria and vintage conditions. The four-level structure is consistent, but not every technical detail should be assumed to operate identically in every German growing area.

VDP.GROSSE LAGE®

VDP.GROSSE LAGE® is the highest VDP classified vineyard-site category. These sites are precisely defined at parcel level and are subject to further requirements, including a maximum yield of 50 hectolitres per hectare and selective hand harvesting.

A VDP.GROSSE LAGE® wine can be dry, fruity-sweet or noble-sweet. A dry wine from a VDP.GROSSE LAGE® site may be labelled VDP.GROSSES GEWÄCHS®, commonly shortened to GG.

That distinction matters:

  • VDP.GROSSE LAGE® describes the classified vineyard site.
  • VDP.GROSSES GEWÄCHS® or GG describes a dry wine from that site.

Not every VDP.GROSSE LAGE® wine is therefore a GG.

VDP is not German wine law

VDP wording and German statutory wording can appear on the same bottle, but they answer different questions.

What you are trying to understand Relevant wording
What does the VDP say about the wine’s estate, locality or vineyard origin? VDP.GUTSWEIN, VDP.ORTSWEIN, VDP.ERSTE LAGE® or VDP.GROSSE LAGE®
Does the wine meet a German legal quality category? Qualitätswein or Prädikatswein
What statutory ripeness or production category applies? Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese and other Prädikat terms
Is the finished wine dry? trocken

Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese are not VDP ranks. They are statutory German Prädikat terms associated with grape or must ripeness and specified production conditions. They should not be treated as synonyms for VDP.GUTSWEIN, VDP.ORTSWEIN or either of the classified vineyard levels.

Nor does a higher Prädikat automatically mean a higher VDP site classification. The two systems describe different aspects of a wine.

How to read a VDP label

Imagine an imported German wine label showing:

  • a locality or vineyard name;
  • a VDP eagle or capsule mark;
  • VDP.GROSSE LAGE®;
  • GG or VDP.GROSSES GEWÄCHS®; and
  • trocken.

A sensible reading would be:

  1. The wine comes from a VDP member estate.
  2. Its stated origin is within the VDP’s highest classified vineyard-site category.
  3. GG identifies the dry expression from that category.
  4. Trocken indicates a dry style.

You should not conclude that:

  • VDP is a government seal;
  • every VDP wine is dry;
  • every VDP.GROSSE LAGE® wine is a GG;
  • the classification guarantees a particular flavour;
  • the wine is automatically better than every non-VDP German wine; or
  • the wine must suit your taste.

The VDP classification can make a label easier to interpret, but it does not replace tasting, producer information or the wine’s own vintage and style details.

What VDP means for Australian wine drinkers

Australian Geographic Indications and VDP classifications are not direct equivalents.

An Australian GI is part of a regulated geographic-origin framework. VDP classification is a voluntary framework created by a private German association for participating member estates. It combines an origin philosophy with additional internal production standards.

A useful comparison is:

  • Australian GI: regulated geographic origin.
  • VDP classification: voluntary member-association classification based chiefly on origin, supported by internal rules.
  • German statutory categories: legally defined German wine categories that may also appear on the label.

You may sometimes see VDP compared broadly with systems such as Burgundy’s Premier Cru and Grand Cru categories. Such comparisons can help explain the general idea of increasingly precise vineyard origin, but they are not legal or exact equivalents.

Why does the system exist?

The VDP traces its institutional origins to an association founded in 1910. It developed its own origin-focused classification partly in response to concerns about how changes to German wine law represented historically important vineyard sites. The current four-level classification was adopted in 2012.

That history helps explain why VDP places such emphasis on where grapes come from, not only on grape ripeness or finished sweetness.

Common questions

Does VDP mean the wine is high quality?

It means the wine is made under the VDP’s voluntary membership and classification framework. The hierarchy expresses the VDP’s view that more specific origin and stricter standards belong at higher levels. It does not guarantee universal superiority or personal enjoyment.

Is GG the same as Grosse Lage?

No. Grosse Lage is the classified vineyard-site category. GG is the dry-wine designation for a wine from a VDP.GROSSE LAGE® site.

Is Spätlese the same as Erste Lage?

No. Spätlese is a statutory Prädikat term. Erste Lage is a VDP vineyard-origin category.
