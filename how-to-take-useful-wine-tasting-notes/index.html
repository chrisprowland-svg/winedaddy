Wine Fundamentals

How to take useful wine tasting notes

Learn a simple, repeatable way to record wine’s appearance, aroma, palate, structure and finish—and separate observation from judgement and preference.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Record what you notice before deciding whether you like the wine.
  • Use the same sequence each time: context, appearance, aroma, palate, structure, finish and conclusion.
  • Specific descriptions are more useful than impressive-sounding language.
  • “Lemon-like” or “vanilla-like” describes an association, not proof that the wine contains lemon or vanilla.
  • A good note can be short. It needs to help you remember, compare and explain the wine later.
  • Your preference is personal; it is not the same thing as a quality judgement.

Quick answer

Useful wine tasting notes are structured records of your observations. Start by writing down what the wine is and when you tasted it. Then look at it, smell it and taste it in a consistent order.

Separate four things:

  1. Observation: what you notice, such as “pale lemon colour” or “medium acidity”.
  2. Interpretation: what those observations suggest about the wine’s style.
  3. Quality judgement: whether the wine seems balanced, persistent or well made.
  4. Preference: whether you enjoy it and when you would choose to drink it.

You do not need specialist equipment or a professional vocabulary. A useful note is clear, repeatable and honest about uncertainty.

A simple tasting-note method

1. Record the context

Before tasting, note the producer or brand, wine name, variety or blend, vintage and region if those details are available. Australian wine labels can present these details in different combinations, so record what you can rather than expecting every bottle to show everything.

Also note the date, setting, whether you are tasting with food and whether you are comparing the wine with another. Serving temperature and glassware can be useful context too.

This information matters because your memory of a wine is influenced by where and how you tasted it.

2. Look at the wine

Begin with appearance. Record whether the wine is clear, its colour and its approximate intensity. For sparkling wine, you might also note the presence and persistence of bubbles. Sediment can be worth recording when it is visible.

Keep the conclusion modest. Colour alone cannot reliably identify a grape variety, region, age or quality. It is an observation, not a diagnosis.

3. Smell the wine

Smell the wine before swirling, then gently swirl and smell it again. Ask:

  • Is the aroma clean or unusual?
  • How intense is it?
  • Which broad aroma families do you notice?
  • Does anything change after swirling?

Start broadly: fruit, floral, herbal, spice, earthy, savoury or oak-like. Move to a more specific association only if it genuinely fits. For example, you might move from “citrus” to “lemon” or “lime”.

It is perfectly acceptable to write “fresh-fruit aroma” or “something herbal” if a more precise description does not come to mind. Do not force an answer because a tasting wheel suggests one.

Words such as “lime”, “violet”, “toast” or “vanilla” are sensory associations. They do not prove that the wine contains those ingredients, or that you have identified its grape variety, region or winemaking method.

If you use categories such as primary, secondary and tertiary aromas, treat them as a helpful teaching framework rather than a diagnostic test. Aroma origins can overlap.

4. Taste the wine

Take a small sip and first record your immediate impression. Then consider the wine’s flavours and sensations separately.

Flavour intensity describes how strongly the flavour registers. It does not mean that you like the wine.

Flavour associations might include citrus, stone fruit, red fruit, herbs, spice, earth or toast. Use phrases such as “reminds me of” or “suggests” when you want to make the personal nature of the association clear.

Then consider the wine’s structure:

  • Sweetness: whether you perceive sweetness. Fruity does not automatically mean sweet.
  • Acidity: the fresh, tart or mouth-watering sensation that gives a wine lift.
  • Tannin: a drying, gripping or chalky sensation, particularly noticeable in many red wines.
  • Alcohol: warmth or heat, although sensation alone cannot reliably tell you the exact alcohol level.
  • Body: the apparent weight or fullness of the wine in your mouth.
  • Texture: sensations such as creamy, silky, firm, angular or grippy.

Terms such as “smooth”, “rich” and “fresh” can mean different things to different people. Make them more useful by adding an observation: “smooth, with gentle tannin” or “rich in flavour but lifted by firm acidity”.

5. Consider the finish

The finish is what remains after you swallow or spit the wine. Does the flavour disappear quickly, or does it persist? Which flavour or sensation remains?

You can use “short”, “medium” or “long”, but plain language works just as well: “the citrus flavour remains for a while, with a dry finish”.

A long finish is not automatically a sign of higher quality. It is one observation that contributes to your overall assessment.

6. Write a conclusion

Now bring the observations together. You might describe the wine’s broad style, whether its components seem balanced and whether the flavour persists.

Then add your personal response:

  • What did you enjoy or not enjoy?
  • Would you drink it again?
  • Would you choose it with food or in a particular setting?
  • How confident are you in the note?

This is where interpretation, quality judgement and preference belong. They should not be confused with the observations that support them.

A tasting note records evidence and impressions. A score compresses an evaluation into a number or category. A buying recommendation adds information about occasion, price, audience or personal taste. They are different things.

Make comparisons more reliable

When comparing two wines, taste them in a consistent order and write your first impressions before discussing the labels or hearing someone else’s opinion. If possible, use similarly clean glasses and pour modest samples.

A reasonably neutral-smelling room helps. Avoid strong perfume, smoke and strongly scented food. Water and pauses between wines can help you reset your palate. If your palate becomes tired, stop: more tasting does not necessarily produce better notes.

Spitting is a normal option when tasting several wines, especially at a winery or organised tasting. It distinguishes tasting from drinking, but it does not mean alcohol exposure is eliminated or guarantee sobriety or safe driving. Follow responsible Australian alcohol guidance and make your own safe arrangements.

The point is to remember the wine

There is no single objectively correct tasting-note format. Your best format is the one you can use consistently and understand later.

Over time, your notes may become more specific. That is useful, but technical language is not the goal. “Fresh, light-bodied and dry, with lemon-like flavour and a clean finish” may tell you more than a paragraph of impressive but vague terms.

Write what you notice first. Explain what you think second. Then say whether you enjoyed it. That simple separation makes your notes more useful—to you and to anyone reading them.

FAQs

Can a tasting note identify a wine’s grape variety or region?

Not with certainty. Tasting notes can describe patterns and associations, but expectation, context and individual sensory perception affect what people notice.

Do I need a wine-tasting wheel?

No. A wheel can help you move from broad categories to more specific descriptors, but it should support observation rather than make you search for a word you do not perceive.

Is a longer note a better note?

No. A short note that clearly records the wine’s structure, flavours, finish and your preference can be more useful than a long, vague description.

Should I score every wine?

Only if scoring helps you. A score is not a substitute for describing what you noticed, and it should not be presented as an objective verdict.
