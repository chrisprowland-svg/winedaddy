Styria wine region: an Australian beginner’s guide

Highlights

  • Styria, or Steiermark, is a wine-growing region in southeastern Austria, near the Slovenian border.
  • Graz is the most useful city for understanding where Styria is; the main southern and eastern wine areas sit around it.
  • The formal wine designation Steiermark is an EU Protected Designation of Origin.
  • Styria contains three current DAC areas: Südsteiermark, Vulkanland Steiermark and Weststeiermark.
  • Sauvignon Blanc is the region’s strongest modern grape association, but Styria produces a much broader range of white wines.
  • Weststeiermark is especially associated with Schilcher, a fresh rosé made from Blauer Wildbacher.
  • “Styria” tells you about origin. It does not, by itself, tell you the grape, sweetness, vintage or exact style.

Quick answer: what is Styria known for?

Styria is an Austrian wine region in the country’s southeast, close to Slovenia and centred practically around the wider area of Graz. Its vineyards are commonly found among rolling to steep hills, with varied soils, warm growing conditions and rainfall that helps create a notably green landscape.

The region is best known for fresh, aromatic, generally dry white wines. Sauvignon Blanc is the leading modern association, while Welschriesling, Weißburgunder (Pinot Blanc), Gelber Muskateller, Morillon (the Styrian name commonly used for Chardonnay), Grauburgunder (Pinot Gris), Traminer and Riesling are also important.

Styria is not one uniform wine style. The broader region includes three current DAC areas: Südsteiermark DAC, Vulkanland Steiermark DAC and Weststeiermark DAC. Each has its own landscape, local associations and emphases. Weststeiermark, for example, is particularly linked with Schilcher, a pink-to-rosé wine made from Blauer Wildbacher.

For an Australian wine learner, the useful starting point is to separate three ideas: the place called Styria, the formal designation Steiermark, and the more specific subregional names that may appear on a label.

Where is Styria?

Styria is in southeastern Austria, near the Slovenian border. The principal wine-growing areas are concentrated mainly in the southern and eastern parts of the Austrian province, rather than across every part of the wider administrative region.

Graz is the nearest useful major city and practical reference point. The main southern and eastern wine areas lie generally south, southwest and east of Graz. Southern Styrian wine areas are approximately 40–55 kilometres south of Graz, commonly around a 40–60 minute drive depending on the destination, route and traffic. That is orientation, not a fixed travel promise.

Useful regional reference points include:

  • Leibnitz for Südsteiermark
  • Deutschlandsberg for Weststeiermark
  • Feldbach for Vulkanland Steiermark

Localities such as Gamlitz, Ehrenhausen, Leutschach, Kitzeck im Sausal, Riegersburg, Klöch, Straden, Stainz and Eibiswald can help you picture the region. They are orientation points, not interchangeable names for the whole GI.

Styria can also be confusing because Steiermark is the German name for Styria. The Austrian wine designation Steiermark should not be confused with Styria in Slovenia, a separate historical and geographic context across the border.

Styria, Steiermark and DAC: what do the terms mean?

The EU recognises Steiermark as a Protected Designation of Origin for wine and quality sparkling wine. A protected designation is a formal origin category with rules defining the area and products that may use the name.

This is broadly comparable to the way an Australian learner might think about a geographic indication, but Austrian and European designation systems have their own legal categories and labelling rules. They should not be treated as identical to Australian GIs.

Within the wider Styrian wine identity are three current DAC areas:

  1. Südsteiermark DAC
  2. Vulkanland Steiermark DAC
  3. Weststeiermark DAC

DAC stands for Districtus Austriae Controllatus, Austria’s controlled regional designation system. In simple terms, a DAC name gives a more specific regional signal than Styria or Steiermark alone.

The area historically known as Südoststeiermark is now designated Vulkanland Steiermark. It should not be presented as a separate current DAC alongside the newer name.

The three Styrian wine areas

Südsteiermark

Südsteiermark is associated with steep, green hills and vineyard slopes that can require considerable manual work. Its strongest grape associations include Sauvignon Blanc, Welschriesling, Weißburgunder, Gelber Muskateller and Morillon.

Gamlitz, Ehrenhausen, Leutschach and Kitzeck-Sausal are useful names for picturing this southern part of Styria. Wines from the area are commonly discussed as fresh, fragrant and lively, but those are broad tendencies rather than guarantees in every bottle.

Vulkanland Steiermark

Vulkanland Steiermark occupies the eastern and southeastern part of the Styrian wine region. Its name reflects the presence of weathered volcanic and basaltic material in parts of the area, alongside sedimentary soils.

Morillon, Grauburgunder, Gelber Muskateller, Sauvignon Blanc, Traminer, Weißburgunder, Riesling and Welschriesling are among the varieties associated with its formal regional descriptions. Feldbach, Riegersburg, Klöch and Straden are useful orientation points.

The area’s wines may show freshness, fruit, herbal notes or spice, depending on variety, site and winemaking. The region should not be reduced to one soil type or one flavour profile.

Weststeiermark

Weststeiermark is the smallest and most distinctive of the three areas in terms of its best-known regional style. It is strongly associated with Blauer Wildbacher, the grape used for Schilcher.

Schilcher is a fresh pink-to-rosé wine style, not a grape variety. Weststeiermark also produces white wines, so it would be misleading to treat the area as only a rosé region. Deutschlandsberg, Stainz, Ligist and Eibiswald are useful local reference points.

How do place and climate influence Styrian wine?

Styria has hilly and often steep vineyard sites, varied exposures and a mixture of volcanic, basaltic, calcareous, sandstone, gravel, slate and other soils. These conditions vary considerably between sites and subregions.

Official descriptions refer to partly Illyrian-Adriatic climatic influence, with a transition towards the warmer, drier Pannonian climate in the east. Warm conditions can support ripeness and aromatic development, while elevation and cooler nights may help retain freshness and acidity. Rainfall, slope, exposure and soil also contribute to local variation.

That does not mean every Styrian wine will taste high-acid, light-bodied, mineral or intensely aromatic. Climate and landscape help create possibilities; grape variety, vineyard site and winemaking still matter. For more background, see How Climate Affects Wine and What Is Terroir?.

What should you expect in the glass?

A good beginner’s shorthand is:

  • mostly white wine
  • often dry
  • fresh and lively
  • fragrant or aromatic
  • sometimes herbal, mineral or spicy
  • variable in body and texture

If Sauvignon Blanc is your reference point, Styrian examples may be a useful reminder that the same grape can express different regional identities. They should not automatically be assumed to taste like Sauvignon Blanc from the Adelaide Hills, Marlborough or another Australian region. You can learn more about the grape in What Is Sauvignon Blanc?.

Also watch for a few names that can trip up Australian readers:

  • Morillon is the Styrian name commonly used for Chardonnay, not a wholly different grape.
  • Welschriesling is a separate variety from Riesling.
  • Schilcher is a regional rosé style associated with Blauer Wildbacher, not a grape.

How is Styria different from Wachau?

Styria and Wachau are separate Austrian wine regions. Wachau is a sibling region in Lower Austria around the Danube, not a subdivision of Styria.

Styria is particularly associated with southeastern hills, aromatic dry whites and Sauvignon Blanc, along with several Burgundy-family varieties and Weststeiermark’s Schilcher. Wachau has a different geography and regional identity. Neither should be ranked above the other, and the broader name Austria does not imply that all Austrian regions use the same grapes or taste alike.

For national context, see Austria. The key takeaway is simple: a Styria label identifies origin, while the grape, DAC area and other label details tell you more about what is in the bottle.

Frequently asked questions

Is Styria the same as Steiermark?

Yes, in this wine context, Styria is the English name and Steiermark is the German and official regional term.

Is Styria known only for Sauvignon Blanc?

No. Sauvignon Blanc is the strongest modern regional association, but Welschriesling, Weißburgunder, Gelber Muskateller, Morillon, Grauburgunder, Traminer and Riesling are also important.

What is Schilcher?

Schilcher is a fresh pink-to-rosé wine style associated with Weststeiermark and made from Blauer Wildbacher.

Is DAC the same as an Australian GI?

Not exactly. Both communicate geographic origin, but DAC is part of Austria’s own European designation system and should not be treated as a direct legal equivalent of an Australian GI.

The simplest way to remember Styria

Think of Styria as a southeastern Austrian wine region of steep, varied hills and aromatic, generally dry white wines, with Sauvignon Blanc at the centre of its modern identity. Then add the qualification that makes the region more interesting: Südsteiermark, Vulkanland Steiermark and Weststeiermark are distinct areas, and Weststeiermark brings the distinctive Schilcher rosé into the picture.
