Madeira grape varieties: a beginner’s guide

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Madeira is a place, a protected wine name and a fortified wine style—not a grape variety.

The island of Madeira, along with Porto Santo, forms part of Portugal’s Região Autónoma da Madeira, or Autonomous Region of Madeira. Wine made under the protected Madeira designation can be produced from several recognised grape varieties. The names you see on a bottle can therefore tell you about the grape, the traditional style, the sweetness or the wine’s maturation—but these are not the same thing.

Highlights

  • Sercial is traditionally associated with the driest classic style.
  • Verdelho usually sits between Sercial and Boal/Bual, with a medium-dry association.
  • Boal or Bual is commonly linked with a richer, medium-sweet style.
  • Malvasia, often called Malmsey, is traditionally associated with the richest and sweetest classic style.
  • Tinta Negra is a black grape that can be used across a broad range of sweetness styles.
  • Terrantez, also identified as Folgasão in official material, is a rarer traditional white variety.
  • These associations are useful guides, not guarantees. Fermentation, fortification, blending and maturation also shape the finished wine.

Quick answer: which grapes are used in Madeira wine?

The four classic named varieties are Sercial, Verdelho, Boal/Bual and Malvasia/Malmsey. They are sometimes called Madeira’s “noble four” and are traditionally used as broad style clues, from drier to sweeter.

Two other important names are Tinta Negra and Terrantez. Tinta Negra is a black grape associated with flexible styles, while Terrantez is a less frequently encountered traditional variety.

The official Madeira specification recognises more recommended and authorised varieties than this short consumer list. So the “classic four” are not the complete legal variety list. They are the names most useful for understanding traditional Madeira style language.

The classic four varieties

Sercial

Sercial is a white grape traditionally associated with the driest or near-driest classic Madeira style. It is also known by the documented synonym Esgana-cão.

In the glass, Sercial-associated Madeira is commonly described as leaner, savoury and marked by pronounced acidity. For an Australian learner, Sercial is the most useful traditional starting point when looking for a drier Madeira.

That does not mean the grape name alone guarantees a particular sweetness level. The finished wine also reflects how fermentation and fortification were managed, as well as blending and maturation.

Verdelho

Verdelho is a white variety traditionally associated with a medium-dry style. It is often positioned between Sercial and Boal/Bual: generally richer than Sercial, but not as sweet in traditional style terms as the varieties that follow it.

Verdelho is also used as a grape name outside Madeira. When reading an imported-wine description, look at the full context. “Verdelho” may refer to the variety, while “Madeira” identifies the place and protected wine designation being discussed.

Boal or Bual

Boal and Bual are spelling and naming forms you may encounter in wine writing and imported-wine descriptions. In the official Madeira material, the name is linked with Malvasia-Fina as a recognised synonym in the relevant context.

Boal/Bual is traditionally associated with a richer, medium-sweet style than Verdelho. It can be a helpful reference point when a reader wants to move from a drier Madeira towards a fuller, sweeter wine.

The name should still be treated as a style clue rather than an absolute promise. A grape variety and a sweetness category describe different aspects of wine.

Malvasia or Malmsey

Malvasia is not necessarily one single variety in the Madeira context. Official material recognises several Malvasia forms, including Malvasia-Cândida, Malvasia-de-São-Jorge and Malvasia-Fina. The English-language form Malmsey is also commonly encountered.

Traditionally, Malvasia or Malmsey is associated with the richest and sweetest of the classic four styles. It is therefore a useful name to recognise when reading a Madeira list or specialist retailer description.

The spelling matters. “Malvasia” can refer to a group of related variety names, while “Malmsey” is a traditional English form associated with Madeira wine. They should not be treated as a guarantee that every bottle has exactly the same composition or flavour.

Tinta Negra and Terrantez

Tinta Negra

Tinta Negra is a black or red grape of Madeira. Official material also records Molar and Saborinho as recognised synonyms in the Madeira designation.

Unlike the classic four, Tinta Negra is not tied to one traditional sweetness position. It can be used for wines across the dry-to-sweet range. That makes it especially important to understand as a grape variety rather than a sweetness label.

Avoid assuming that an unnamed Madeira is necessarily Tinta Negra. The available evidence supports describing Tinta Negra as an important flexible variety, but not making a universal claim about every bottle without a named variety.

Terrantez or Folgasão

Terrantez is a traditional white variety that is less commonly encountered than the better-known names. The official specification identifies Folgasão as a recognised synonym.

Specialist references generally position Terrantez somewhere between Verdelho and Boal/Bual in broad traditional style terms, but the safest beginner-friendly point is simply that it is a rarer traditional Madeira variety. Exact claims about plantings, production or commercial availability require current production data and are outside this guide.

Variety, sweetness and fortification are different things

A grape variety is the type of vine used to make the wine. Sweetness describes how sweet the finished wine tastes, or its residual sugar. Fortification is the addition of grape spirit during production. Maturation describes how the wine develops over time.

These factors interact, but they are not interchangeable.

That is why it is better to say:

“Sercial is traditionally associated with dry Madeira”

rather than:

“Sercial means dry.”

Fermentation and the timing of fortification can affect how much sugar remains in the wine. Blending and maturation can also influence the final style and sensory character. Madeira’s grape names are therefore useful signposts, not a complete recipe printed in one word.

How to read a Madeira label in Australia

An Australian learner may encounter Madeira in an imported-wine section, a specialist bottle shop, a restaurant wine list or wine-course material. Names may appear in Portuguese or in an English-influenced form:

  • Boal or Bual
  • Malvasia or Malmsey
  • Terrantez or Folgasão
  • Sercial or Esgana-cão

Treat these as documented naming relationships, but check the exact wording on the bottle or in the accompanying description. Do not assume that alternate names mean every wine is identical.

When choosing between styles, use the traditional sequence as a starting point:

  • Sercial: drier reference point
  • Verdelho: medium-dry reference point
  • Boal/Bual: richer, medium-sweet reference point
  • Malvasia/Malmsey: sweetest and richest classic reference point

Then check whether the label names a variety, a blend, a general Madeira category or a maturity or quality term. A bottle may communicate more than one of these, and it may not name a grape at all.

The simplest rule is: Madeira tells you where the wine comes from and what protected style it belongs to; the grape name gives you another clue about its traditional character.

Frequently asked questions

Is Madeira a grape variety?

No. Madeira is a Portuguese autonomous region, an island wine area and the protected name of a fortified wine. Sercial, Verdelho, Boal/Bual, Malvasia and Tinta Negra are grape names associated with Madeira wine.

Does Sercial always mean dry?

No. Sercial is traditionally associated with the driest classic Madeira style, but grape variety alone does not determine the finished wine’s sweetness.

Are Boal and Bual the same thing?

They are documented naming forms associated with the Madeira context, with official material linking Boal/Bual to Malvasia-Fina in the relevant specification. The exact use of “Boal” elsewhere should not automatically be transferred to Madeira.

Is Malmsey a different grape from Malvasia?

Malmsey is a common English-language name associated with Malvasia-style Madeira. Madeira’s official material recognises several Malvasia forms, so the precise variety context can matter.

Are the noble four all the grapes allowed in Madeira?

No. The official specification recognises a broader set of recommended and authorised varieties. The noble four are the classic consumer-facing group, not the complete legal list.
