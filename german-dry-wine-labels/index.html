Quick answer: what does trocken mean?

On a German wine label, trocken means dry within the relevant German and European labelling framework. For the still-wine category covered by the German Wine Institute’s explanation, it generally allows up to 4 grams per litre of residual sugar, or up to 9 grams per litre where the wine’s acidity is sufficiently close to its sugar level.

That does not mean a trocken wine will taste flavourless, austere or free from fruit character. Residual sugar is a measurable part of wine; perceived sweetness is a sensation influenced by acidity, alcohol, body and flavour intensity. A dry Riesling, for example, can still smell of ripe fruit and feel lively and refreshing.

The most useful rule for Australian shoppers is simple: look for the taste indication, rather than guessing from the grape, region or harvest classification.

German sweetness terms at a glance

These terms describe the finished wine’s taste or sweetness category. The figures below apply to the relevant still-wine context, not automatically to every sparkling or semi-sparkling German wine.

Trocken

Trocken means dry. The wine may contain a small amount of residual sugar while still fitting the defined dry category. It is the clearest German label clue when you are looking for a dry style.

Halbtrocken

Halbtrocken literally means semi-dry and is best explained in Australian English as off-dry. It allows more residual sugar than trocken. Depending on the relationship between sugar and acidity, the relevant still-wine limit is up to 12 grams per litre, or up to 18 grams per litre under the specified acidity condition.

You may notice some sweetness, but the wine should not automatically be assumed to taste rich or dessert-like.

Feinherb

Feinherb is commonly seen on German labels and can suggest a dryish style. However, it is not legally defined, so it does not provide a fixed residual-sugar range.

Treat feinherb as a helpful style indication rather than a precise measurement. If the exact sweetness matters, do not assume that feinherb means the same thing as trocken or halbtrocken.

Lieblich

Lieblich indicates a medium-sweet style. In the still-wine framework described by the German Wine Institute, it is sweeter than halbtrocken and does not exceed 45 grams per litre of residual sugar.

Süß or süss

Süß means sweet and indicates more than 45 grams per litre of residual sugar in the relevant still-wine framework. You may also see süss, without the ß character, particularly in international contexts. The spelling difference does not create a different sweetness category.

Prädikat terms are a separate layer

German labels can also include terms such as:

  • Kabinett
  • Spätlese
  • Auslese
  • Beerenauslese
  • Eiswein
  • Trockenbeerenauslese

These are Prädikat classifications. They primarily describe the ripeness, selection or harvesting conditions of the grapes and must at harvest. They are not direct synonyms for dry, off-dry or sweet.

That is why a label such as “Riesling Spätlese trocken” contains several different kinds of information:

  • Riesling identifies the grape variety.
  • Spätlese identifies a later-harvest Prädikat classification.
  • Trocken gives the clearest answer about the wine’s dry-style indication.

A Spätlese is not automatically sweet. Similarly, Kabinett does not automatically mean sweet. The taste indication and the Prädikat are different information layers.

There is one particularly confusing example: Trockenbeerenauslese. Here, trocken refers to dried or shrivelled berries, not the dry-wine taste indication. The term describes a very high-ripeness Prädikat category and is characteristically associated with very sweet wine.

For a fuller explanation of these classifications, see the German Prädikat system.

Why can a dry wine taste fruity?

Fruitiness and sweetness are not the same thing.

A wine can have aromas or flavours reminiscent of citrus, stone fruit or other ripe fruit while containing little residual sugar. Those fruit characteristics come from the grape and the way the wine develops; sweetness is a taste sensation associated primarily with sugar.

Acidity also changes the impression. High acidity can make a wine feel fresh and energetic, and it can balance the effect of residual sugar. A wine with some sugar may therefore taste less sweet than expected if its acidity is high. Conversely, a very dry, high-acid wine may feel sharper because there is little sugar to soften the impression.

Alcohol and body can also contribute to a softer or sweeter impression, but neither is a reliable shortcut for identifying sweetness.

The useful takeaway is: fruity does not necessarily mean sweet, and dry does not necessarily mean flavourless.

How to read a German label in practice

When you encounter an imported German bottle in Australia, read the label in this order:

  1. Find the taste indication: trocken, halbtrocken, feinherb, lieblich or süß/süss.
  2. Separate it from the Prädikat: Kabinett, Spätlese and Auslese describe a different aspect of the wine.
  3. Identify the grape and origin: Riesling, Mosel, Rheingau, Pfalz or another term provides variety or geographic context, not a guaranteed sweetness level.
  4. Check the complete label and technical information: alcohol and other details may be presented differently from familiar Australian labels.

German and European terminology should not be treated as automatically identical to the word “dry” on an Australian label. Nor should German Prädikat categories be equated with Australian Geographic Indications or Australian quality classifications.

Frequently asked questions

Is trocken the same as completely sugar-free?

No. Trocken is a defined dry-style indication, and the relevant wine may contain a small amount of residual sugar.

Is German Kabinett wine sweet?

Not necessarily. Kabinett is a Prädikat classification based on harvest ripeness, not a direct sweetness indication. Check the rest of the label for terms such as trocken or halbtrocken.

Does feinherb mean off-dry?

It can suggest a dryish style, but feinherb has no fixed legal definition or residual-sugar range. Treat it as a broad clue rather than a precise category.

Is all German Riesling sweet?

No. German Riesling is made in both dry and sweet styles. The label’s taste indication is more useful than assuming sweetness from the grape variety.

What is the difference between süß and süss?

They are spelling variants. Süß is standard German spelling; süss may appear where the ß character is not used.
