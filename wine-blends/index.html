Wine Fundamentals

Wine blends: what Australian wine drinkers need to know

Understand what wine blends are, why winemakers blend, how blends differ from field blends and single-varietal wines, and what Australian labels can tell you.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • A wine blend combines two or more wine components.
  • Those components may be different grape varieties, vineyard parcels, batches, vessels or vintages.
  • Blending is a deliberate winemaking tool, not a sign of poor quality.
  • A blend can be dry, sweet, inexpensive, highly regarded—or anything in between.
  • A field blend begins with different grape varieties planted together in one vineyard block.
  • A single-varietal wine is presented around one principal grape variety, but it is not necessarily 100% that variety under Australian labelling rules.
  • The word “blend” alone does not tell you how a wine will taste.

Quick answer: what is a wine blend?

A wine blend is made by combining two or more wine components. Most commonly, those components come from different grape varieties, such as Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon. But winemakers can also blend wines from separate vineyard sites, parcels, batches, vessels or vintages.

The wines may have been fermented or matured separately before being combined. This is different from a field blend, where different grape varieties are planted together in the same vineyard block and may traditionally be harvested and fermented together.

Blending is a way to shape the finished wine. It can help balance acidity, tannin, alcohol, colour, aroma, flavour, texture and structure. It may also add complexity, maintain consistency between vintages or help create a recognisable house style.

Most importantly, “blend” is not a quality warning. A blend is not necessarily sweet, cheap, smooth or inferior. The word describes how components are combined; it does not predict the wine’s flavour or quality.

Why do winemakers blend wine?

To improve balance and structure

Different wines bring different strengths. One component may contribute acidity and freshness, while another adds colour, body, alcohol or tannin. Combining them can produce a more balanced result.

This does not mean every blend will taste balanced. The outcome depends on the varieties, proportions, vintage, sites and winemaking choices. Blending is a tool that can improve balance, not a guarantee.

To add complexity and texture

Separate components may contribute different aromas, flavours and sensations in the mouth. Bringing them together can create a broader or more layered wine than any one component would provide alone.

The same applies to texture. A wine matured in different vessels, for example, may contain components with different textural qualities before the final blend is made.

To maintain consistency

Growing conditions vary from year to year. Blending wines from different parcels, batches or vintages can help a winemaker maintain a broadly recognisable style, even when individual components change.

This is one reason a blend’s proportions may vary while its intended house style remains similar.

What can be blended?

Wine blending can involve:

  • Grape varieties, such as Cabernet Sauvignon with Shiraz.
  • Vineyard parcels or sites, where fruit is made separately before the wines are combined.
  • Batches or lots, including separate fermentations.
  • Vessels, such as wines held or matured separately.
  • Vintages, although Australian vintage claims have specific labelling requirements.

A wine can also be a blend even when it comes from one grape variety. For example, separate batches of Shiraz from different vineyard parcels may be combined. So “blend” does not always mean “a mixture of grape varieties”.

Blend versus field blend

A wine blend is the broad category: two or more wine components have been combined.

A post-fermentation blend usually means that wines were made separately and combined later. The components might come from different varieties, sites, batches, vessels or vintages.

A field blend is associated with a vineyard block where two or more grape varieties are planted together. Traditional field blends are often harvested together and co-fermented, meaning the fruit is fermented in the same vessel. However, shared harvesting and co-fermentation are traditional associations, not universal requirements.

The useful distinction is this:

A field blend begins with mixed planting in the vineyard. A winery blend begins with the decision to combine wine components.

All field blends can be understood as blends in the broad sense, but not every wine blend is a field blend. A separately made Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon blend is not automatically a field blend.

Blend versus single-varietal wine

A single-varietal wine is presented around one principal grape variety. That makes it different from a wine that openly presents several varieties as part of its identity.

However, “single-varietal” should not be understood as an absolute guarantee of 100% compositional purity. In Australia, variety claims are regulated, and a named variety generally needs to account for at least 85% of the relevant wine when the claim is made under the applicable rules.

That means a wine labelled around one variety may contain permitted inclusions. The exact legal treatment depends on the claim and applicable requirements. The term is best understood as describing the wine’s principal declared variety.

What Australian labels can tell you

Australian labelling rules regulate claims about variety, vintage and geographical indication, or GI. Wine Australia’s general guidance says that a relevant single variety, vintage or GI claim usually requires at least 85% of the wine or grapes concerned. Where multiple applicable components are declared, they are generally listed in descending order of proportion.

This is a useful beginner’s guide, not a complete summary of Australian wine law. The word “blend” itself should not be treated as a formal quality category, and not every blend must display exact percentages on the consumer label.

When reading a label:

  1. Look for any named grape varieties.
  2. Check whether percentages are supplied.
  3. Notice whether a vintage or GI is claimed.
  4. Read terms such as “cuvée” in context.
  5. Do not judge the wine simply because it says “blend”.

A blend may be dry or sweet, light- or full-bodied, simple or complex. Those qualities depend on the wine itself, not on the word “blend”.

Australian examples of blending

Australian wine history includes Cabernet Sauvignon–Shiraz blends and Grenache-led combinations involving Shiraz and Mourvèdre. Australian winemakers have also experimented with varieties such as Tempranillo, Touriga Nacional, Nebbiolo, Barbera and Sangiovese.

These examples show that Australian blending is not restricted to one formula. They should not be treated as legal categories, guarantees of style or rankings of quality. The varieties, proportions, vintage and production choices all matter.

What does “cuvée” mean?

“Cuvée” is a context-dependent wine term. It may refer to a particular lot, selection or blend, but it is not a universal synonym for “blend”.

Some cuvées are blends; others may be identified by a name that tells you little about their exact composition. For a fuller explanation, see cuvée meaning.

Frequently asked questions

Is a blend always sweet?

No. A blend can be dry, off-dry or sweet. Sweetness depends on the wine’s residual sugar and production choices, not simply on whether components were blended.

Are blends lower quality than single-varietal wines?

No. Blending is used in wines across the quality spectrum. The word “blend” alone cannot tell you whether a wine is good, poor, expensive or inexpensive.

Does a blend always contain different grape varieties?

No. Wines from different parcels, batches, vessels or vintages may be blended even when they come from the same variety.

Is a field blend the same as a wine blend?

No. A field blend is associated with different grape varieties planted together in one vineyard block. A wine blend is the broader category of combining wine components.

Does the first variety listed make up exactly 85% of an Australian blend?

Not necessarily. The 85% figure is a general threshold for relevant variety, vintage and GI claims, not a rule that every first-listed component makes up exactly 85%. Labelling requirements depend on the claim being made.

The main idea

Think of blending as a winemaker assembling a finished wine from different pieces. The aim may be balance, structure, texture, complexity, consistency or style. Once you understand that, the word “blend” becomes less of a mystery—and much less of a reason to make assumptions.
