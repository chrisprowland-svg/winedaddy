Does Vintage Matter in Wine?

Learn what a wine vintage means, when weather and region make it important, and why an older or highly rated vintage is not automatically better.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Vintage means the year the grapes were harvested, not necessarily the year the wine was bottled or released.
  • Weather during that growing season can influence ripeness, acidity, tannin, flavour, yields and harvest timing.
  • Vintage matters more for some regions, varieties, producers and wine styles than others.
  • Australia does not have one single “vintage”. Conditions can differ greatly between regions in the same year.
  • An older vintage is not automatically better, more valuable or ready to drink.
  • For many early-drinking or non-vintage wines, producer, style, storage and current condition may matter more than the year.
  • Treat vintage as useful context—not a guarantee of quality or enjoyment.

Quick answer: should you pay attention to the vintage?

Yes, but not as a simple quality score.

The vintage is the year in which the grapes were harvested. Weather during that growing season can affect how the grapes ripen, including their sugar, acidity, tannin, flavour and overall balance. Those differences can be noticeable in the finished wine.

How much vintage matters depends on several things: the region, grape variety, vineyard site, producer, wine style and whether the wine is intended to age.

Vintage tends to matter more for wines from regions with considerable year-to-year variation, wines made to mature in bottle, and styles where the balance between ripeness and acidity is especially important. It tends to matter less for many inexpensive, high-volume wines made for relatively early drinking, and for non-vintage wines blended from multiple years.

The practical answer is simple: read the vintage alongside the producer, region, variety, style and storage history. Don’t let the number on the label make the decision by itself.

What does “vintage” mean?

In wine, vintage means the harvest year.

A 2022 vintage wine was made from grapes harvested in 2022, even if the wine was bottled or released later. This is different from:

  • Bottle age: how long the wine has been in the bottle.
  • Release date: when the producer first made the wine available for sale.

A wine might carry a 2022 vintage but not reach a shop until 2024. Conversely, a wine released in 2024 might be made from grapes harvested in 2023 or earlier.

Australian wine labels do not have to include a vintage in every case. When a producer makes a vintage claim, Australian labelling rules require it to meet applicable blending and traceability requirements. The four-digit year is therefore useful information about the grapes’ harvest, but it is not a promise that the wine is excellent—or that it will improve with age.

Why can weather change wine from year to year?

Vines respond to the conditions they experience during the growing season and harvest. Temperature, rainfall, frost, heat, drought, disease pressure and smoke exposure can all influence the crop.

For example, weather can affect:

  • how quickly grapes ripen;
  • the balance between sugar and acidity;
  • tannin and colour development in red grapes;
  • the amount of fruit harvested;
  • disease risk;
  • the timing of picking.

These effects are not straightforward. Heat can speed up ripening, but excessive heat may create challenges with freshness or uneven maturity. Rain may be helpful at one point in the season and increase disease pressure at another. A difficult season does not automatically produce poor wine, because vineyard and winery decisions also shape the result.

This is why “sunny year equals good vintage” is too simplistic. Wine is made from grapes grown in particular places, during particular seasons, and the details matter.

Australia does not have one national vintage

Australia has warm, cool, maritime, inland and elevated wine regions. Their climates, rainfall patterns, heat exposure, harvest dates and seasonal risks can be very different.

A year that suits one region or grape variety may be challenging for another. Even within the same region, vineyard sites and producers can experience or respond to conditions differently.

So when you see a vintage described as “excellent” or “difficult”, ask: excellent where, and for what wine?

A regional vintage assessment might apply to a particular variety and style, or to wines tasted at a particular stage of their development. It cannot guarantee that every Australian bottle from that year will taste the same. Wine Australia’s vintage reporting, for example, presents production information by region and variety rather than treating the country as one uniform vineyard.

When does vintage matter more?

Wines intended for ageing

Vintage can be more important when a wine is designed to develop in bottle. The relationship between fruit, acidity, tannin and concentration can influence how the wine changes over time.

That does not mean a celebrated vintage is automatically ready to drink. A wine can be high quality but still youthful, while another older bottle may have passed its best.

Regions with greater seasonal variation

In cool, maritime or otherwise climatically marginal regions, relatively small differences in the growing season may be more noticeable. Ripeness and acidity can be closely balanced, so the timing and conditions of harvest can influence the wine’s final shape.

This does not make cool-climate wine better or worse. It simply means the year may provide more useful context.

Styles closely connected to one harvest

A vintage can be informative for a wine that is made from a single harvest and aims to show that year’s conditions. Vintage sparkling wine is one example.

Non-vintage sparkling wine is commonly blended from wines from different years to create a consistent house style. Vintage sparkling wine instead focuses on the character of a particular harvest. That makes the year more relevant—but it does not make vintage sparkling wine automatically superior.

Vintage can also matter for certain sweet or fortified wines where a single harvest and extended maturation are central to the style. The word “vintage” can have a more specific meaning in those categories, so context remains important.

When does vintage matter less?

Vintage may be less decisive when a wine is:

  • made for relatively early drinking;
  • produced in high volume to a consistent style;
  • blended from several regions, vineyards or harvests;
  • labelled non-vintage;
  • inexpensive enough that current freshness and storage matter more than small year-to-year differences.

Many everyday wines are made to be enjoyed soon after purchase. For these bottles, you may learn more by checking the producer, variety, region and style than by searching for the perfect year.

Non-vintage does not mean low quality. Blending across years can be a deliberate way to maintain a reliable style.

Is an older vintage better?

No. Vintage year and age are different ideas.

A wine’s age tells you how long it has existed in bottle. Its vintage tells you when the grapes were harvested. Ageing can improve some wines, but most wine is made for relatively early consumption, and not every wine improves with time.

For an older bottle, storage and current condition may matter more than the prestige of the year. Consider:

  • whether it has been stored in suitable conditions;
  • whether the producer intended it to age;
  • whether the closure and bottle appear sound;
  • whether you enjoy mature wine styles;
  • whether the wine may already be past its best.

An older label is not a guarantee of quality, collectability or enjoyment.

How should you use vintage charts and reviews?

Vintage charts and reviews can be useful starting points, but they are summaries—not guarantees.

Before relying on one, ask:

  1. Which region is it assessing?
  2. Which grape variety or wine style?
  3. Is it judging young wines, mature bottles, quality, drinkability or ageing potential?
  4. Does it cover the particular producer you are considering?
  5. Has the bottle been stored well?
  6. Does the style suit what you enjoy?

A year described as difficult may still produce fresh, lighter or savoury wines that some drinkers prefer. A highly rated year may still contain disappointing bottles.

A simple buying checklist

When deciding whether vintage matters for a bottle, check:

  • Region: Is it a place where seasonal variation is likely to be useful context?
  • Variety and style: Is it a delicate, age-worthy or vintage-focused wine?
  • Producer: Does the winemaker have a consistent approach?
  • Intended drinking time: Are you opening it tonight, or planning to cellar it?
  • Storage: Is the bottle old, and do you know how it was kept?
  • Your preference: Do you like fresh and lighter wines, or richer and more developed ones?

Vintage is one piece of the puzzle. For many wines, the producer, region, style and condition of the particular bottle are just as important—or more important—than the number on the label.

Frequently asked questions

Does vintage mean the year the wine was bottled?

No. It means the year the grapes were harvested. Bottling and release may happen later.

Is a non-vintage wine worse than a vintage wine?

No. Non-vintage wines are often blended from multiple years to create a consistent style. That can be a deliberate quality decision.

Does a good vintage guarantee a good bottle?

No. Producer choices, vineyard site, storage and bottle condition all matter.

Should I avoid a difficult vintage?

Not necessarily. A difficult year may produce wines with a fresher, lighter or more savoury character, and individual producers can respond well to challenging conditions.

Does vintage matter for rosé?

It can, but many rosé wines are made for relatively early drinking. Freshness, producer, storage and current condition may matter more than small differences between years.

Is an old wine automatically better?

No. Some wines improve with age, some change without improving, and some are best enjoyed young.
