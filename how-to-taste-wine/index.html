How to Taste Wine: A Beginner’s Guide

A practical step-by-step guide to looking, smelling and tasting wine without the theatre.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Wine tasting is structured observation, not a test of expertise.
  • Use this simple sequence: prepare, look, smell, taste, notice the finish and reflect.
  • Swirl gently and smell more than once; the wine may reveal different aromas each time.
  • On the palate, notice sweetness, acidity, tannin, body, flavour and texture.
  • Your personal preference is valid, but it is different from a technical quality judgement.
  • You do not need to identify obscure aromas or produce the “correct” tasting note.
  • Tasting does not require finishing every pour. Spitting and drinking water are normal at organised tastings.

Quick answer: how do you taste wine properly?

To taste wine, begin with a clean glass, a reasonably neutral-smelling setting and a small pour. Look at the wine, smell it before and after a gentle swirl, then take a small sip. Notice how sweet, fresh, drying, full-bodied, warm or flavourful it seems. Pay attention to what remains after you swallow or spit: this is the finish. Finally, write down what you observed, whether you enjoyed it and—if you feel confident—whether the wine seems balanced for its style.

That is wine tasting. It is not an exam, and you are not expected to recognise every fruit, spice or flower in the glass. The aim is to pay attention and describe your experience clearly.

Prepare before tasting

You do not need elaborate equipment. A clean wine glass, water and a place without strong smells will take you a long way. Perfume, smoke, cleaning products and strongly flavoured food can compete with the wine’s aromas.

Taste when you are not rushed or distracted. Light-coloured surroundings can make it easier to observe the wine, and suitable serving conditions help, although there is no single setting or temperature that suits every wine.

Pour modest amounts, particularly if you are comparing several wines. At a cellar door, restaurant or Australian wine show, you can ask the host to repeat a wine’s name or explain its style. Water and a spittoon may also be available.

1. Look at the wine

Hold the glass against a pale background and notice the wine’s:

  • colour family, such as red, pink or yellow;
  • depth of colour;
  • clarity;
  • bubbles, if it is sparkling; and
  • sediment or other visible material.

These are observations, not verdicts. Colour alone does not reliably tell you a wine’s quality, price, age, grape variety or flavour. A cloudy appearance may have different explanations, including a winemaking choice or a possible fault, so avoid jumping to conclusions.

The same applies to Australian wines: colour and appearance can vary widely with grape variety, region, climate, winemaking and vintage.

2. Smell before and after swirling

Bring the glass towards your nose and take a gentle first smell. Start with the broad impression. Does it seem fruity, floral, fresh, spicy, savoury, earthy, oaky or fairly neutral?

Now swirl gently and smell the wine again. If you are new to tasting, keep the glass on a stable surface while swirling. You are looking for a change: perhaps the wine seems more expressive, or a different aroma becomes noticeable.

Smell is an important part of what people commonly call flavour. When wine is in your mouth, aromas can travel towards the nose from the back of the mouth, contributing to the overall impression.

Aroma descriptions are personal. One person’s “lemon” may be another person’s “citrus”, and someone else may notice no fruit at all. Memory, culture, sensitivity and surroundings affect what people recognise. Approximate, honest language is more useful than forcing a precise answer.

3. Taste with a small sip

Take a small sip and let the wine move naturally around your mouth. You do not need to swish aggressively. Notice the wine’s structure, flavour and texture.

Here are the main prompts:

  • Sweetness: Does the wine taste dry, or does it have a noticeable sweet impression? “Dry” here means not sweet; it does not mean flavourless.
  • Acidity: Does it feel fresh, tart or mouth-watering?
  • Tannin: Does it leave a drying, grippy or slightly rough sensation, particularly around your gums? Tannin is not simply bitterness, and it is more noticeable in many red wines.
  • Body: Does the wine feel light, medium or full in your mouth—in other words, what is its apparent weight or fullness?
  • Alcohol: Do you notice warmth or heat? Warmth is an observation, not automatically a fault.
  • Flavour intensity: How strongly does the wine register?
  • Flavour character: What does it remind you of? Broad descriptions such as fresh citrus, red fruit, dark fruit, herbs or spice are enough.
  • Texture: Does it feel crisp, smooth, silky, creamy, firm or otherwise distinctive?

These terms are useful prompts rather than boxes you must fill in. The planned WineDaddy guides to wine aroma, body, acidity, tannins and sweetness will explore these ideas in more detail when available.

4. Notice the finish

After swallowing or spitting, notice what remains. Does the flavour disappear quickly, or does it linger? Does the finish feel fresh, drying, warm or flavourful?

This remaining impression is called the finish. You might describe it as brief or lingering, but a longer finish does not automatically mean a better wine. It is one part of the overall experience, not a score.

Description, preference and quality are different

Wine tasting becomes less intimidating when you separate three questions:

  1. What do I notice?
    “It smells of citrus and feels fresh.”

  2. Do I enjoy it?
    “I like it because it is crisp.”

  3. Does it seem well made or balanced for its style?
    “The acidity, flavour and alcohol seem to work together.”

These answers can differ. You may dislike a technically sound wine because its style is not for you. You may enjoy a wine without wanting to make a formal judgement about its quality. Personal taste is not a failure of tasting technique.

How to compare two wines

Comparing two wines can make differences easier to notice. Use the same type of clean glassware, similar serving conditions and small pours. Taste each wine using the same sequence:

  1. look at both;
  2. smell each before swirling;
  3. swirl and smell again;
  4. take a small sip;
  5. notice the finish; and
  6. write notes before discussing price, brand, grape or region.

You can optionally hide the labels to reduce expectations, but this is not necessary for a relaxed comparison. Rather than declaring a winner, ask: What differs? Which wine feels fresher, fuller, more drying, more aromatic or more persistent? Which do I prefer?

A simple tasting-note checklist

You can copy this into your phone:

  • Wine:
  • Appearance: colour, depth and clarity
  • Aroma: broad smells before and after swirling
  • Palate: sweetness, acidity, tannin, body, flavour and texture
  • Finish: brief or lingering; what remains?
  • Preference: what did I enjoy or not enjoy?
  • One-sentence summary:

Try tasting the same wine again later. Your description may change as the wine, your surroundings or your attention changes. That does not mean your first note was wrong.

Taste responsibly

Tasting wine does not mean finishing every sample. At organised tastings, spitting is normal and can help you compare wines without consuming every pour. Drink water, keep pours modest and check the label: a restaurant serve or bottle may contain more than one Australian standard drink.

Australian Government guidance says that healthy adults should drink no more than 10 standard drinks per week and no more than four on any one day to reduce alcohol-related risk. Less alcohol means lower risk, and some people should not drink at all. One Australian standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol. Never drive after drinking. People under 18, people who are pregnant or planning pregnancy, and others advised not to drink should not taste alcohol.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to swirl wine?

No. Gentle swirling can help release or redistribute aromas, but it is optional. If you do swirl, keep the glass on a stable surface until you feel comfortable.

Is there a correct tasting note?

No single wording is correct. Tasting notes describe perception, and people differ in memory, sensitivity and experience. Aim for language that is honest and understandable.

Can wine colour tell me whether it is good?

No. Colour can provide clues, but it does not prove quality, price, age, grape variety or flavour.

Is a long finish a sign of quality?

Not by itself. A lingering finish can be worth noting, but quality, balance and enjoyment involve more than duration.

Should I swallow wine when tasting?

Not necessarily. You can swallow or spit, depending on the setting and how much alcohol you want to consume. Spitting is common at organised tastings.
