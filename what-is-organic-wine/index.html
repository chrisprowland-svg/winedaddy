What Is Organic Wine?

Learn what organic wine means in Australia, how certification works and why organic does not automatically mean natural, vegan or preservative-free.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Primary topic: Organic wine
Reading time: About 7 minutes

Highlights

  • Organic wine begins with grapes grown under organic-farming requirements.
  • Depending on the claim and standard, organic rules may also apply to winemaking.
  • Organic wine is not automatically natural, biodynamic, vegan, additive-free or sulphite-free.
  • Australian wine sold domestically does not have to be certified to carry an organic claim, but the claim must be truthful and supported.
  • “Certified organic” and “made with organic grapes” are not necessarily the same claim.
  • Organic status does not create one particular flavour. Organic wine can be red, white, rosé or sparkling, in many different styles.
  • The label wording and any certification mark are more useful than assumptions about what “organic” must mean.

Quick Answer: What Does Organic Wine Mean?

Organic wine is wine made from grapes grown according to organic-farming requirements. Depending on the wording on the bottle and the applicable standard, organic requirements may also cover cellar practices such as permitted additives, processing aids, winemaking methods and labelling.

In simple terms, organic wine is about how the grapes and, in some cases, the wine are produced. It is not a grape variety or a flavour style.

An organic wine might be a bright Australian Riesling, a full-bodied Shiraz, a delicate Pinot Noir or a sparkling wine. Its taste is still shaped by grape variety, region, climate, vintage and winemaking decisions.

The important point for Australian shoppers is that the word “organic” can describe different levels of assurance. A bottle may be certified organic, made with organic grapes, or carry another organic-related claim that the producer must be able to substantiate. Read the exact wording rather than assuming every claim means the same thing.

How Is Organic Wine Produced?

Organic grape growing

Organic viticulture—the organic growing of wine grapes—uses a defined production system for managing vines, soil, pests, diseases and biodiversity.

Organic systems generally restrict or exclude specified synthetic agricultural inputs and allow only approved inputs or methods under the relevant standard. That does not mean the vineyard uses nothing. Weeds, pests and diseases still need to be managed, and organic growers may use permitted materials and cultivation techniques.

The emphasis is on managing the vineyard as an ecosystem: maintaining soil health, supporting biodiversity, encouraging vine health and selecting inputs according to the applicable organic rules.

The precise requirements vary between standards and certification schemes. That is why a short definition such as “grown without chemicals” is misleading. Everything in agriculture involves substances and processes that can be described chemically, and “organic” does not mean substance-free.

Organic winemaking

Some organic claims relate mainly to the grapes. Others describe the finished wine as organic or certified organic, which can involve additional rules in the winery.

Those rules may cover matters such as yeast, clarification, filtration, processing aids, additives, sulphur dioxide and labelling. The details depend on the standard, certification scheme and market in which the wine is sold.

This creates an important difference between:

  • Wine made with organic grapes: a claim about the grapes used.
  • Certified organic wine: a certification claim about the product or production system under a named standard.
  • Organic farming claim: wording that may focus on how the vineyard was managed rather than certifying every step in the winery.

These phrases should not be treated as interchangeable.

Is Organic Wine Sulphite-Free?

No. Organic wine is not automatically sulphite-free or additive-free.

Sulphur dioxide may be used in wine for protection against oxidation and unwanted microbial activity. Sulphites can also occur naturally during fermentation. Organic standards may permit sulphur dioxide and other substances within defined conditions or limits.

A “no added sulphur” claim is different from an organic claim. It means sulphur dioxide was not deliberately added; it does not necessarily mean the wine contains no naturally occurring sulphites.

For Australian packaged wine, sulphites must be declared when present at or above the applicable food-labelling threshold. If this is a particular concern when you are buying wine, check the label and the producer’s technical information rather than assuming that organic means no sulphur has been used.

What Does “Certified Organic” Mean in Australia?

Australia has an important distinction between organic wine sold domestically and organic wine exported overseas.

For wine sold within Australia, certification is not mandatory before a business makes an organic claim. However, the business must be able to substantiate the claim, and it must not be false or misleading under Australian consumer law.

Many producers voluntarily use private certification bodies. A certification mark can give shoppers a clearer indication that the producer’s practices have been assessed against a particular standard. It is still worth checking which certifier is named and what the certification covers.

Exported Australian organic goods operate under a more formal framework. Organic exports must be certified through an approved certifying body and meet the relevant export requirements, including government export documentation where required. Export requirements may also depend on the destination market.

So, a bottle sold in an Australian shop is not necessarily certified under the same system as an organic wine prepared for export. Nor does the absence of a certification logo automatically prove that a wine was not made using organic practices.

How Should You Read an Organic Wine Label?

When choosing a bottle, look for four things:

1. The exact claim

Does the label say “certified organic”, “made with organic grapes” or simply refer to organic farming? These descriptions can have different scopes.

2. A certification mark

If certification matters to you, look for the certifier’s name or logo. An unfamiliar logo is not automatically meaningless, but its presence alone does not tell you which rules were applied.

3. Technical information

A producer’s website or technical sheet may explain whether the claim covers the vineyard, the winemaking, or both. It may also provide information about sulphur dioxide and other cellar practices.

4. What the claim does not say

Organic status does not automatically tell you that the wine is vegan, natural, low-intervention or free from all additives. Those are separate questions.

Is Organic Wine the Same as Natural, Biodynamic or Sustainable Wine?

No. These terms overlap, but they describe different ideas.

Organic and natural wine

Organic wine is connected to organic farming and, depending on the claim, regulated organic winemaking and certification.

Natural wine is generally used for a broad, low-intervention approach to growing grapes and making wine. It does not have one universal definition in Australia, and a natural wine is not automatically organic or certified.

Organic and biodynamic wine

Biodynamic wine is associated with a distinct biodynamic agricultural system. Under Australia’s National Standard, certified biodynamic production also meets organic requirements, while adding further biodynamic requirements.

That means biodynamic and organic can overlap, but “biodynamic” is not simply another word for organic.

Organic and sustainable wine

Sustainable wine is a broad term that may refer to environmental, social or economic practices, depending on the programme or claim. Sustainability can be a valuable goal, but the word alone does not prove organic certification.

Does Organic Wine Taste Different?

There is no single organic-wine flavour profile.

Organic wine can be fruity, savoury, light-bodied, full-bodied, oaked, unoaked, dry, sweet or sparkling. Grape variety, Australian region, climate, vintage, harvest timing, fermentation and maturation still shape what ends up in the glass.

Organic status tells you about production requirements, not whether the wine will taste “cleaner”, more expressive or better. The best way to choose is to consider the variety and style you already enjoy, then use the organic claim as additional information about how the wine was produced.

Is Organic Wine More Expensive?

It can be, but not always.

Organic production and certification may involve additional management, labour, record-keeping, lower yields, packaging or distribution costs. Price can also reflect region, scale, grape variety and the producer’s overall approach.

A higher price does not prove that a wine is organic, and an affordable bottle is not automatically less genuinely organic. Use the label wording and supporting certification or producer information instead.

Practical Buying Advice

If you are buying organic wine in Australia:

  1. Decide whether certified organic status is important to you.
  2. Read the exact claim rather than relying on the front label’s general impression.
  3. Look for a named certification body if certification is part of your decision.
  4. Check the producer’s technical information if you want to know about sulphur dioxide or cellar practices.
  5. Choose the variety and style you like—organic wine is not one taste category.
  6. Ask a retailer or cellar-door team what the certification claim covers if the label is unclear.

The most useful question is often not “Is this wine organic?” but “What exactly has been certified or claimed?”

Frequently Asked Questions

Is organic wine healthier?

Organic status is a production and labelling claim, not a health guarantee. This article does not make health claims about organic wine.

Is organic wine vegan?

Not necessarily. Organic certification does not automatically establish that a wine is vegan. Vegan status can depend on cellar materials such as fining agents, so check the producer’s specific statement.

Is organic wine always natural?

No. Organic wine can be made using a range of cellar practices. Natural wine is a separate and less consistently defined term.

Can organic wine contain additives?

Yes. Depending on the applicable standard, some additives and processing aids may be permitted under defined conditions.

Does organic wine have to carry a certification logo?

Not for every organic wine sold domestically in Australia. Domestic certification is voluntary, although organic claims must be substantiated and must not mislead. Export organic wine follows a more formal certification framework.

Can organic wine be made from any grape variety?

Yes. Organic describes the production method, not a particular variety. Organic wine can be made from many grape varieties and in many styles.
