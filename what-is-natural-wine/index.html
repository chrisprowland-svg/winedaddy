What Is Natural Wine?

Natural wine usually means a low-intervention approach, but there is no single universal definition. Learn what the term does and does not tell you.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Natural wine usually means wine made with a low-intervention approach.
  • There is no single, universally accepted definition of “natural wine” in Australia.
  • Natural wine is not automatically organic, biodynamic, vegan, additive-free, unfiltered or sulphur-free.
  • Native yeast, limited additions, minimal filtration and little or no added sulphur are common associations, not compulsory rules.
  • Cloudiness, sediment and unusual aromas can occur, but they are not guarantees of quality or authenticity.
  • The best way to understand a bottle is to look beyond the front label and check the producer’s notes.

Quick answer: what is natural wine?

Natural wine is generally made with a low-intervention approach. That often means the winemaker aims to let the grapes and fermentation speak for themselves, using fewer additions, adjustments and processing steps than many conventionally made wines.

The difficulty is that natural wine does not have one fixed definition or universal production checklist. One producer’s natural wine might be made with organically farmed grapes, native yeast and no filtration. Another might use some different combination of farming or cellar practices. The word itself does not tell you exactly how the wine was made.

Natural wine is therefore best understood as a broad winemaking movement or philosophy, rather than a grape variety, region or single flavour style.

What does “low intervention” mean?

Low intervention describes a relative approach: the winemaker chooses to do less at certain points from vineyard to bottle. Possible choices include:

  • farming with organic, biodynamic or other low-input methods;
  • allowing fermentation to begin with naturally occurring, or ambient, yeast;
  • using fewer additions;
  • limiting fining, which removes selected particles or compounds;
  • using little filtration or no filtration;
  • adding little or no sulphur dioxide.

These are common natural-wine associations, not rules that every natural wine follows. “Low intervention” also does not mean “no intervention”. Growing grapes, managing fermentation, controlling temperature, choosing when to bottle and storing the finished wine all involve decisions.

Native yeast is still yeast

A spontaneous or ambient fermentation uses yeast naturally present on the grapes, winery equipment or in the cellar rather than relying solely on a selected commercial yeast culture. It does not mean the wine ferments without yeast.

Native-yeast fermentation can create distinctive results, but it can also be less predictable. Commercial yeast is not automatically bad, and native yeast is not automatically better. They are different winemaking choices.

Natural wine versus organic, biodynamic and conventional wine

These terms describe different things, so they should not be treated as interchangeable.

Term What it mainly describes What it does not automatically tell you
Natural wine A broad, low-intervention approach to grape growing and winemaking A fixed list of practices, additives or sulphur level
Organic wine Organic farming and permitted production practices under a relevant standard or substantiated claim That the wine is unfiltered, additive-free or low intervention
Biodynamic wine Biodynamic agricultural practices and, where claimed as certified, a relevant certification framework That the wine follows every natural-wine convention
Conventional wine A broad comparison term for more established commercial winemaking practices A single method or guaranteed flavour profile

A wine can be natural and biodynamic, or organic and made with more cellar intervention than you might expect. A wine can also be described as natural without being certified organic or biodynamic.

In Australia, organic claims must be able to be substantiated and must not mislead consumers. Domestic certification is not universally mandatory. Certified organic and biodynamic claims can provide useful information about the standard being followed, but the absence of certification does not by itself prove that a wine is not low intervention.

Is natural wine additive-free or sulphur-free?

Not necessarily.

Natural wines may contain fewer additions, but “natural wine” does not establish that no additives were used. If a particular addition matters to you, look for the producer’s technical information rather than assuming from the label description.

Sulphur dioxide, often written as SO₂, is used in winemaking for antioxidant and antimicrobial protection. Some sulphites can also occur naturally during fermentation.

The phrase “no added sulphur” normally means that the winemaker did not deliberately add sulphur dioxide. It does not necessarily mean the wine contains zero sulphites.

Australian food-labelling rules require added sulphites to be declared in packaged food when present at or above the relevant threshold. If you are sensitive to sulphites, treat “no added sulphur” and “sulphur-free” as different claims and seek reliable product information.

Why can natural wine be cloudy or unusual?

A natural wine may be bottled with little filtration or no filtration. That can leave yeast, grape particles or other matter in suspension, creating a cloudy appearance or sediment at the bottom of the bottle.

Cloudiness is possible, not compulsory. Clear wine can be made with a low-intervention approach, and cloudy wine is not automatically more authentic or better.

Natural wine also has no single flavour profile. Depending on the grape variety, region, vintage and cellar choices, it may taste bright, fresh, savoury, earthy, fruity or highly aromatic. Some bottles can show characters associated with reduction, volatile acidity, oxidation or Brettanomyces.

Those characters are not automatically faults in every taster’s view, but “natural” is not a free pass for spoilage or technical problems. Vinegar-like aromas, stale flavours or medicinal and animal-like notes may indicate issues rather than proof that the wine is more genuine. A wine can be distinctive and well made without being fault-free in the conventional sense—or it can simply be flawed.

Bottle variation can also be more noticeable in wines with less stabilisation or sulphur protection, particularly if storage has been inconsistent.

How should you choose and serve natural wine?

If you are buying natural wine in Australia, begin with the information that matters most to you:

  1. Check the producer’s notes. Look for details about farming, yeast, filtration, fining and sulphur additions.
  2. Separate certification from philosophy. “Certified organic” and “biodynamic” answer different questions from “natural” or “low intervention”.
  3. Ask about vegan suitability. A wine described as natural is not automatically vegan, because fining practices vary.
  4. Store it carefully. Wines with limited sulphur protection can be more sensitive to heat, oxygen and poor storage.
  5. Expect variation, not a guaranteed style. Natural wine might be fresh and clean, savoury and textured, cloudy, or something else entirely.
  6. Taste without pressure. You do not have to enjoy an unusual aroma simply because it is associated with natural wine.

At a winery or wine shop, a useful question is: “What did you do—or choose not to do—from fermentation through to bottling?” That usually reveals more than the word “natural” alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is natural wine healthier?

There is no basis for treating all natural wine as universally healthier. Production method alone cannot establish a health outcome, and natural wine still contains alcohol.

Does natural wine cause fewer hangovers?

No reliable general promise follows from the term “natural”. Hangovers cannot be ruled out because a wine is described as natural or has no added sulphur.

Is natural wine always organic?

No. Some natural wines use organically farmed or certified organic grapes, but organic farming is not a universal requirement of the term.

Is natural wine always biodynamic?

No. Biodynamic wine is associated with a particular agricultural approach and certification framework. It may overlap with natural wine, but the terms are not synonyms.

Is natural wine always vegan?

No. Some wines are fined with animal-derived products, while others are unfined or use different fining materials. Check with the producer.

Should natural wine be cloudy?

Not necessarily. Cloudiness can result from little or no filtration, but natural wine can also be clear.

Is unusual-smelling wine automatically a fault?

No—but it is not automatically a virtue either. Some natural wines show unusual aromas that a drinker may enjoy. Persistent vinegar-like, stale, medicinal or animal-like characters may instead indicate instability or a fault.

The simple takeaway

Natural wine usually means a low-intervention approach, but it is not a guarantee of a particular ingredient list, farming method, appearance or flavour. In Australia, the most confident way to read the term is to treat it as a starting point, then check the producer’s specific information about farming, fermentation, filtration, fining and sulphur.
