What is Bonarda?

Bonarda is usually Argentina’s name for Corbeau, a dark-fruited red grape with a useful freshness. Learn how its identity, style, regions and label meaning vary.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Bonarda is a red wine-grape name most strongly associated with Argentina. In Argentine wine, it generally refers to the French variety Corbeau, also known as Douce Noire and, in some American wine contexts, Charbono.

It usually makes deeply coloured, fruit-forward red wines with flavours of red and dark fruit, useful freshness and moderate, often soft-feeling tannin. But there is an important catch: “Bonarda” does not mean exactly the same grape everywhere. In Italy, the name can refer to different local varieties, including Bonarda Piemontese.

So, when you see Bonarda on a bottle or wine list, check the country or region of origin before assuming what is in the glass.

Highlights

  • In Argentina, Bonarda generally means the grape Corbeau.
  • Corbeau is also associated with the names Douce Noire and Charbono.
  • The name Bonarda has different uses in Italy.
  • Argentine Bonarda is especially associated with Mendoza and the wider Cuyo region.
  • Wines are often dark in colour, generous in fruit and fresh enough to avoid feeling heavy.
  • Tannin is commonly soft to moderate, but winemaking and ripeness can change the texture.
  • Bonarda can be made as a single-variety wine or used in a blend.
  • In Australia, the name is most likely to be encountered in imported or specialist selections.

What grape is Bonarda?

The safest way to understand Bonarda is to separate the Argentine meaning from the wider family of names.

DNA-based grapevine identification from the University of California, Davis supports the connection between Argentine Bonarda and the French variety Corbeau. The same variety is known as Douce Noire in French references associated with Savoie. Charbono is another name used for this variety, particularly in California and some American wine contexts.

That does not mean every wine labelled Bonarda comes from the same grape. Italian wine has several local uses of similar names, including Bonarda Piemontese. These should not automatically be treated as synonyms for Argentine Bonarda. Nor should Bonarda be casually equated with Croatina, Dolcetto or Dolcetto Nero.

This is a useful wine-label lesson: a grape name can travel between countries and acquire different meanings. The name alone is not always enough to establish the variety’s identity.

Where is Bonarda grown?

Argentina is the principal modern context for Bonarda. Mendoza is the most useful regional reference for Australian drinkers, with the grape also found in other Cuyo provinces, including San Juan and La Rioja.

Bonarda has historically been valued for its colour, fruit and ability to contribute to blends. It is also bottled as a wine in its own right. Modern quality-focused examples may involve lower yields and more attention to concentration and balance than the grape’s earlier high-volume role suggests.

The underlying Corbeau variety has French Savoie associations, while California uses the name Charbono for the same DNA-identified variety. Those contexts are less central to most drinkers than Argentina, but they explain why Bonarda, Charbono and Douce Noire sometimes appear in discussions of the same grape.

What does Bonarda taste like?

Argentine Bonarda tends to produce intensely coloured wines, often showing ruby, purple or violet tones. Common flavour descriptions include:

  • cherry, raspberry and strawberry;
  • blackberry, plum and blueberry;
  • ripe, generous fruit;
  • occasional spice, floral or earthy notes, depending on the wine.

The typical structure is often medium-bodied to full-bodied, although lighter examples are possible. The grape can retain useful acidity, giving ripe fruit a fresher edge. Tannin is commonly described as soft or moderate, making many examples approachable when young.

Those are tendencies, not rules. A warm site, late picking, oak maturation or more extraction may produce a richer, darker and firmer wine. Earlier picking, a cooler site or gentler winemaking may give a brighter, fresher and more medium-bodied result.

Bonarda can therefore sit in an appealing middle ground: generous and dark-fruited, but not necessarily as tannic or imposing as some full-bodied red wines.

How winemaking changes Bonarda

Bonarda may be made in several different styles:

  • fresh and fruit-driven, for drinking relatively young;
  • deeply coloured and more extracted;
  • matured with oak, adding texture and spice;
  • blended with other varieties;
  • bottled as a varietal wine, where the grape name takes centre stage.

Oak can support a fuller style, but if it dominates, it may hide the grape’s fruit character. Similarly, a blended Bonarda may taste quite different from a varietal bottling. The vintage, climate, vineyard conditions and producer’s choices all matter.

This is why it is better to ask, “What style of Bonarda is this?” rather than expecting a single fixed flavour profile.

How should Australians read the label?

For an Australian drinker, start with the country or region:

  • Argentina + Bonarda: generally the Corbeau/Douce Noire variety described here.
  • Italy + Bonarda: potentially a different local grape, so do not assume it is Argentine Bonarda.
  • Charbono: may refer to the same Corbeau identity, particularly in Californian or related American contexts.
  • Mendoza: a geographic origin, not another name for the grape.

Bonarda is a grape name, while Argentina and Mendoza describe place. They are different kinds of information on a label.

The available Australian evidence does not establish significant domestic Bonarda plantings or a nationally important Australian Bonarda region. In Australia, you are most likely to encounter the name in imported Argentine wine or specialist selections. Do not assume that an Australian-labelled Bonarda follows the same rules or style as an Argentine example without checking its origin and producer information.

What food goes with Bonarda?

Bonarda’s ripe fruit, freshness and generally moderate tannin make it a flexible partner for hearty but not overwhelmingly rich food. Good starting points include:

  • grilled or barbecued meats;
  • sausages and hamburgers;
  • empanadas;
  • pizza;
  • tomato-based pasta;
  • roasted vegetables;
  • mildly spiced dishes.

These are starting points rather than strict rules. A lighter, fresher Bonarda may work well with roasted vegetables or tomato-based dishes, while a fuller, oak-influenced example can stand up to grilled meat.

How should Bonarda be served?

Try Bonarda slightly cool, around 15–18°C. Serving it too warm can make alcohol and ripe fruit feel heavier; a little coolness can highlight freshness.

A standard medium-to-large red-wine glass is sufficient. Young, fruit-driven Bonarda usually does not need decanting, although a concentrated or oak-aged example may benefit from brief aeration.

There is no universal ageing rule. Many approachable examples are enjoyable relatively young, while more concentrated or oak-aged wines may develop further. The individual wine, vintage and producer’s advice matter more than the grape name alone.

The simple answer

Bonarda is usually an Argentine name for the red grape Corbeau, also known as Douce Noire and Charbono. It commonly produces dark, fruit-forward reds with fresh acidity and soft-to-moderate tannin. But Bonarda is also used for different Italian grapes, so always read the name alongside the wine’s country or region of origin.

Frequently asked questions

Is Bonarda the same as Malbec?

No. They are different grape varieties, although both are strongly associated with Argentina and can produce dark-coloured, generous red wines.

Is Bonarda an Italian grape?

Sometimes the name refers to Italian grapes, but Argentine Bonarda generally refers to Corbeau. The country of origin matters.

Is Bonarda the same as Charbono?

In the Argentine and Californian contexts supported by grapevine DNA identification, Bonarda, Corbeau, Douce Noire and Charbono can refer to the same variety. Charbono and related names have also been used for different Italian grapes, so the context still matters.

Is Bonarda sweet?

Bonarda is generally made as a dry red wine. Ripe fruit flavours can taste sweet or plush even when the wine is not sweet.

Should Bonarda be cellared?

Not automatically. Many examples are approachable young, while fuller, more concentrated or oak-aged wines may benefit from time. Treat each bottle on its own merits.
