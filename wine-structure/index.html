Wine Fundamentals

Wine structure: how a wine gets shape, weight and tension

Learn what wine structure means, how acidity, tannin, alcohol, sweetness, body and texture interact, and how to assess structure in an Australian wine.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Wine structure is the combination of sensations that gives a wine shape and presence in your mouth.
  • Its main contributors are acidity, tannin, alcohol, sweetness, body and texture.
  • Structure is not the same as flavour, aroma, quality or body.
  • A wine’s structure comes from the relationship between its components, not from one component being “high”.
  • Balance describes how well those components work together.
  • You can assess structure by tasting in sequence and describing what you feel before deciding whether you like it.

Quick answer: what is wine structure?

Wine structure is the framework of physical and sensory sensations that gives a wine shape, weight, tension, grip, texture and persistence in the mouth.

The main contributors are acidity, tannin, alcohol, sweetness, body and texture. They interact constantly. Acidity can make a wine feel fresh and mouth-watering. Tannin can create drying grip. Alcohol can add warmth and weight. Sweetness can add roundness. Body describes the overall sense of fullness, while texture describes how the wine feels as it moves across your palate.

So, structure is not a scorecard with separate boxes. It is the impression created by the components working together. A wine can be full-bodied but poorly integrated if its alcohol feels hot or its tannin dominates. A lighter-bodied wine can still have excellent structure if its acidity, texture and finish work in harmony.

The term is useful because it helps you describe how a wine feels, rather than only what it smells or tastes like.

The building blocks of structure

Acidity: freshness and lift

Acidity creates sourness, freshness and salivation. It is the sensation that makes your mouth water after a sip. In a wine review, you may see words such as bright, crisp, lively, racy or firm.

Acidity can give a wine lift and direction, but high acidity is not automatically a flaw. Its effect depends on the rest of the wine. Sweetness can soften the impression of acidity, while alcohol, fruit and texture can make the palate feel broader.

For a detailed explanation, see what acidity in wine means.

Tannin: grip and drying texture

Tannin is most noticeable in red wine, although it can also occur in some white, rosé and skin-contact wines. It creates astringency: the drying, gripping or puckering sensation you may notice on your gums and cheeks.

Tannin can feel fine and powdery, silky, chalky, firm or coarse. It is not simply bitterness. Its amount and texture, along with alcohol, acidity, sweetness and other wine components, affect how drying it feels.

Explore the detail in what tannins are.

Alcohol: warmth and weight

Alcohol can contribute warmth, fullness and weight. A moderate amount may make a wine feel generous and rounded. If it stands out as heat or burning, the wine may feel less integrated.

Alcohol is not the same as body. A wine with moderate alcohol can feel substantial because of its sweetness, extract, tannin, texture or concentration. A high-alcohol wine can feel hot rather than full and balanced.

Sweetness: roundness and softness

Sweetness comes mainly from residual sugar: sugar left in the wine after fermentation. It can give a wine roundness, softness and apparent weight.

A key distinction is that dry does not mean flavourless. A dry wine can have intense fruit aromas and flavours. Sweetness, fruitiness and flavour are different things. The visual “Dry does not mean flavourless” can help make this distinction clear.

Sweetness may soften the perception of acidity or tannin, but it does not automatically make a wine balanced. The result depends on the whole wine.

Learn more about sweetness in wine.

Body: the overall sense of fullness

Body is the broad impression of weight or substance in the mouth. Wines are commonly described as light-, medium- or full-bodied.

Body is not one measurable ingredient. Alcohol, sweetness, tannin, acidity, flavour concentration and texture may all contribute to the impression. That is why body should be treated as a perception, not a simple prediction from the alcohol percentage.

A light-bodied wine is not inferior to a full-bodied wine. They simply create different experiences.

See what wine body means.

Texture: how the wine feels

Texture is the tactile quality of the wine. You might describe a wine as smooth, silky, creamy, waxy, angular, chalky or grippy.

These are descriptions of sensation, not proof of a particular soil, grape or winemaking method. Texture may overlap with tannin, acidity, alcohol, sweetness and dissolved or suspended material. Texture in wine is a useful related concept when you want to explore this further.

Structure is about relationships

The most useful question is not “Does this wine have high acidity?” It is “What is the acidity doing with everything else?”

Acidity may make a wine feel lean, fresh or linear, while alcohol and sweetness may make it feel broader and rounder. Tannin may add grip and persistence, but too much drying sensation can dominate the palate. Fruit concentration can help components feel integrated, but concentration alone does not guarantee balance or quality.

This is why two wines with similar acidity or alcohol can feel completely different. Structure is the shape created by the relationship among the components, not the size of any one component.

Structure and other wine terms

Structure is easy to confuse with several neighbouring ideas:

  • Aroma is what you smell, including aromas noticed through the nose after you sip. Structure is mainly about the wine’s physical and tactile presence in the mouth. See wine aroma.
  • Flavour describes the combined impression of taste and retronasal aroma. A wine can taste strongly of fruit without being full-bodied or heavily structured.
  • Body is one contributor to structural impression, not a synonym for structure.
  • Finish describes the sensations that remain after the wine has left your mouth. Structure can support a long finish, but finish is an outcome over time, not the same thing as structure. See wine finish.
  • Balance describes how the elements work together. A structured wine is not automatically balanced. See balance in wine.
  • Complexity concerns the diversity and development of aromas and flavours. A simple wine can be well structured, while a complex wine can be unbalanced. See complexity in wine.
  • Quality is a broader judgement. Structure may contribute to an assessment of quality, but it does not prove that a wine is better, more expensive or more ageworthy.

Terms such as elegance, freshness, concentration, minerality and typicity may help describe a wine, but they should not automatically be treated as fixed components of structure.

Australian examples: structure varies

Australian wine is a useful reminder not to treat grape variety or region as a guarantee of structure.

Wine Australia describes Clare Valley Riesling as light-bodied, with vibrant fruit and acidity. Cooling breezes and differences between daytime and nighttime temperatures are part of the regional context. In a glass, this style can illustrate a lighter, fresh and acid-driven structural impression. That does not mean every Clare Valley Riesling tastes the same, or that climate alone determines acidity.

Australian Shiraz shows another kind of variation. Wine Australia describes a broad range, from cooler-climate, medium-bodied and spicy styles to fuller-bodied, richly flavoured and textured warmer-climate styles. These are tendencies, not rules. Vineyard site, vintage, harvest decisions, viticulture and winemaking also matter.

The practical lesson is simple: use region and variety as clues, then assess the wine in front of you.

How to taste for structure

Try this sequence with a single wine or, even better, two wines side by side.

  1. Start with sweetness. Does the wine feel dry, slightly sweet or sweet? Does it feel lean or rounded?
  2. Notice acidity. Does your mouth water? Does the wine feel fresh, crisp, sharp, soft or flat?
  3. Check alcohol. Is there warmth? Does it add weight, or does it feel hot?
  4. Assess tannin. Is there drying, grip or roughness? Does it build towards the finish?
  5. Consider body and texture. Does the wine feel light, medium or full? Is it smooth, silky, creamy, angular or grippy?
  6. Follow its movement. How does it feel on entry, through the middle of the palate and after swallowing?
  7. Look for persistence. Which sensations remain: acidity, tannin, fruit, sweetness or warmth?
  8. Assess integration. Do the components support one another, or does one stick out?
  9. Separate observation from judgement. First say, “medium body, fresh acidity, fine tannin and moderate warmth.” Then decide whether that feels balanced or enjoyable.

How to taste wine provides a broader tasting method.

Temperature and context matter. Chilling can make freshness more apparent while muting some texture and sweetness; serving a wine too warm can make alcohol stand out. Food can also change the perception of tannin and other sensations. These effects change your experience of the wine, not necessarily its underlying composition.

Frequently asked questions

Does more structure mean better wine?

No. More structure is not automatically better. A wine can be light, delicate and well balanced, or powerful and firmly structured. The useful question is whether the components suit the wine and work together.

Is structure the same as tannin?

No. Tannin is one contributor to structure. Acidity, alcohol, sweetness, body and texture also matter, especially in white and sparkling wines.

Is full-bodied wine always highly structured?

No. Body is one part of structure. A full-bodied wine may lack integration, while a lighter-bodied wine may have clear acidity, fine texture and persistent shape.

Can I tell a wine’s structure from the label?

A label may offer clues through grape, region, style or alcohol information, but it cannot tell you exactly how the wine will feel. Taste remains the best test.

Does structure prove that a wine will age well?

No. Acidity, tannin and concentration may contribute to a wine’s development, but structure alone does not predict longevity.
