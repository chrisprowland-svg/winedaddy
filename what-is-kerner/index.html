What is Kerner?

Kerner is a German white wine grape related to Riesling. Learn how Kerner tastes, whether it is sweet, where it is grown and how it differs from Riesling.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Kerner is a white wine grape variety from Germany.
  • It was bred from Trollinger and Riesling in 1929.
  • Kerner is aromatic, fresh and often fruit-driven, but its style can vary.
  • It may be made as a dry, off-dry or sweet wine.
  • Kerner is related to Riesling, but it is not the same grape.
  • Germany is its principal country of association, with an important secondary connection to Alto Adige in northern Italy.
  • In Australia, Kerner has been grown and made commercially on a very small scale.

Quick answer

Kerner is an aromatic white wine grape variety bred in Germany by August Herold. Its parents are Trollinger — also called Schiava Grossa or Vernatsch — and Riesling.

Wines made from Kerner often combine Riesling-like freshness with a fruitier, broader palate. You might find aromas and flavours of apple, pear, citrus, apricot or tropical fruit, sometimes with a floral or lightly Muscat-like lift. These are tendencies rather than a fixed formula: climate, ripeness, harvest timing and winemaking all influence the finished wine.

Kerner is not a wine region, a legally defined style or a guarantee of sweetness. A bottle labelled Kerner may be dry, off-dry or sweet.

How was Kerner created?

Kerner was bred in 1929 at Weinsberg in Württemberg, Germany. August Herold crossed the red grape Trollinger with the white grape Riesling.

The variety was initially known as Weißer Herold. It was later named Kerner after Justinus Kerner, a poet and physician associated with Weinsberg. The variety was released or granted plant variety protection in 1969.

In technical records, Kerner may also appear as Weinsberg S 25-30. These names can help explain older or specialist references, but Kerner is the name most useful to look for on a wine list or label.

Kerner is a distinct Vitis vinifera wine grape. Although it has Riesling as one parent, it should not be treated as another name for Riesling.

What does Kerner wine taste like?

Kerner is commonly made into aromatic white wine with noticeable freshness and fruit character. Depending on the wine, you may find:

  • apple or pear;
  • citrus;
  • apricot and other stone-fruit notes;
  • tropical fruit;
  • floral aromas;
  • a light Muscat-like fragrance;
  • a broader texture than many Rieslings.

Kerner often has lively acidity and can feel fuller-bodied or rounder than Riesling. However, the difference is not absolute. A cool-climate, early-picked Kerner may feel particularly crisp, while riper fruit, residual sugar or different winemaking choices can make the wine seem broader and richer.

The grape name does not determine exactly what every bottle will taste like.

Is Kerner sweet?

No. Kerner is not inherently sweet.

It can be made as a dry wine, where the grape’s acidity and fruit character take centre stage. It can also be made in off-dry or semi-sweet styles, which may taste rounder and more expressive. Late-harvest and higher-quality-level examples can be sweeter still.

A wine’s sweetness depends largely on how much sugar remains after fermentation, along with the fruit’s ripeness and the wine’s acidity. A strongly fruity aroma does not necessarily mean the wine is sweet.

If you want a dry Kerner, look for a label or retailer description that clearly states the wine is dry. If the sweetness is not obvious, ask the retailer or sommelier rather than assuming from the variety name.

Kerner versus Riesling

Kerner and Riesling are separate grape varieties, but they are closely connected: Riesling is one of Kerner’s parents.

The simplest way to think about the relationship is that Kerner can share Riesling’s freshness and aromatic energy, while often bringing more fruitiness and breadth. That does not make the two wines interchangeable. Site, ripeness and winemaking can make the difference between them more or less noticeable.

Where is Kerner grown?

Germany remains Kerner’s principal country of association. The German Wine Institute recorded about 1,944 hectares planted there in 2023, particularly in Rheinhessen and the Palatinate, with additional plantings in Württemberg, Mosel, Nahe and Franconia.

That figure needs a date: plantings have declined substantially from their historical peak, so Kerner is established in Germany without being one of the country’s dominant varieties.

Kerner also has a notable association with Alto Adige, or South Tyrol, in northern Italy. It is particularly connected with the Isarco Valley and Val Venosta, where vineyards at higher elevations can help preserve freshness. Alto Adige Kerner may show an aromatic, fruity style with a lightly spicy character.

The grape is also grown in smaller quantities elsewhere, including Switzerland and South Africa.

Is Kerner grown in Australia?

Australian wine-industry evidence records small-scale plantings and commercial production of Kerner, including examples in South Australia and the Murray–Darling area.

This is a niche Australian presence rather than evidence of broad national importance. Current plantings, producer activity and retail availability can change, so an Australian reader is more likely to encounter Kerner occasionally in a specialist domestic wine or as an import from Germany or Alto Adige than as a mainstream Australian variety.

What food suits Kerner?

A dry or off-dry Kerner can be a useful match for foods that benefit from freshness and aromatic lift. Try it with:

  • lightly spiced Asian dishes;
  • seafood;
  • roast chicken;
  • salads with fruit or herbs;
  • mildly sweet or savoury dishes.

The wine’s acidity can refresh the palate, while a little residual sugar can soften the effect of chilli or complement gently sweet ingredients. Match the food to the actual bottle: a crisp dry Kerner will behave differently from a richer, sweeter example.

Serve it chilled but not ice-cold — around 8–12°C is a useful starting range. Very cold serving can mute the aromas that make Kerner interesting.

Frequently asked questions

Is Kerner a grape or a wine?

Kerner is a grape variety. Wines made from it may differ in sweetness, texture and intensity depending on where and how they are produced.

Is Kerner the same as Riesling?

No. Riesling is one of Kerner’s parents, but Kerner is a separate variety. It often shares Riesling’s freshness while offering a broader, fruitier profile.

What colour is Kerner wine?

Kerner produces white wine. Its aromatic profile can range from crisp and citrusy to fuller and stone-fruited.

What does Kerner mean on a wine label?

It usually identifies the grape variety used to make the wine. It does not, by itself, specify a region or sweetness level.

Is Kernling another name for Kerner?

No. Kernling is a mutation of Kerner, not simply a synonym for the original variety.

Should I try Kerner?

If you enjoy aromatic white wines and want something related to Riesling but potentially broader and fruitier, Kerner may be worth exploring. Check the label or ask about sweetness before buying, because the style can vary considerably.
