How non-alcoholic wine is made

Most dealcoholised wine starts as fermented wine. Learn how alcohol is removed, why aroma and texture can change, and what Australian labels may mean.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Most dealcoholised wine starts as conventionally fermented wine.
  • Alcohol is removed after fermentation, not simply left out of the process.
  • Vacuum distillation, spinning-cone columns and reverse osmosis are among the methods used.
  • Removing alcohol can also affect aroma, body, texture, acidity and sweetness perception.
  • Some aroma compounds may be collected and returned to the wine.
  • “Dealcoholised” does not automatically mean completely alcohol-free.
  • Australian labelling terms and alcohol thresholds do not necessarily mean the same thing in every country.

Quick answer

Non-alcoholic or dealcoholised wine is generally made by first producing wine in the usual way: grapes are processed, the juice ferments, and the resulting wine may be clarified, matured or blended.

The alcohol is then removed through a controlled separation process. Common approaches include vacuum distillation, spinning-cone column systems and reverse osmosis. These methods are designed to remove ethanol while retaining as much of the wine’s other character as possible, but alcohol removal can also take away aroma compounds and change the wine’s body, texture, acidity and balance.

Afterwards, the producer may recover or reintroduce selected aromas, blend the wine or make other adjustments before stabilising, filtering and bottling it. The exact sequence varies between products, and not every producer uses every method.

The process, step by step

1. Grapes become juice

The process usually begins much like conventional winemaking. Grapes are pressed or otherwise processed to obtain juice. Depending on the intended style, the juice may have varying amounts of contact with grape skins before fermentation.

This is the first useful distinction: a fermented dealcoholised wine is not simply grape juice with a wine-style label. It generally starts with wine.

2. The juice ferments

Yeast converts grape sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Fermentation also helps create the aromas, acidity, colour, phenolic structure and other features associated with wine.

That means alcohol is normally present during an important part of the process. Dealcoholisation happens later, after the producer has made a fermented wine.

3. The wine may be clarified, matured or blended

Before alcohol removal, the wine may be clarified, stabilised, matured or blended. The order depends on the producer and the style being made.

These steps are not universal. A fresh white, a red wine and a sparkling-style product may be handled differently before and after dealcoholisation.

4. Alcohol is separated from the wine

The producer then uses a separation method to reduce the alcohol level. The main approaches work differently.

Vacuum distillation

Under reduced pressure, volatile components can be separated at lower temperatures than they would be at ordinary atmospheric pressure. This can help limit heat exposure, but it does not guarantee that all aroma will remain. Some volatile compounds can leave with the alcohol.

Spinning-cone columns

A spinning-cone column uses rotating cones and controlled vapour movement to separate volatile components. Aroma fractions may be collected during the process and potentially returned later.

Australian wine-industry research identifies spinning-cone systems as one of the technologies used commercially, particularly when very low alcohol levels are required. That does not make it a universal method or a guarantee of a particular flavour.

Reverse osmosis

Reverse osmosis is a pressure-driven membrane process. The membrane allows some components to pass more readily than others, helping separate alcohol and water-related fractions from the wine.

It does not remove only alcohol. Water, acids and aroma compounds may also be affected, which is why the wine may need further adjustment.

Why the finished wine can taste different

Alcohol contributes to more than a wine’s strength. It influences how the wine feels across the palate and how aromas and flavours are perceived.

When alcohol is removed, the finished drink may have:

  • less body or palate weight;
  • different texture;
  • altered sweetness perception;
  • changes in acidity and balance;
  • lower or different aroma intensity;
  • changes to colour or other chemical characteristics.

The result depends on the original wine, its alcohol level, the chosen method and how far the alcohol is reduced. A dealcoholised wine should not be expected to taste exactly like the wine it came from.

Aroma recovery and rebalancing

Alcohol removal can carry away some volatile aroma compounds. Some systems allow producers to collect aroma fractions and reintroduce selected compounds later.

A producer may also blend the wine or adjust its balance after separation. These steps can help shape the final product, but they are not automatic features of every dealcoholised wine. The process is better understood as a series of choices than as one fixed recipe.

Is non-alcoholic wine just grape juice?

Not necessarily.

Some alcohol-free wine-style beverages may be made without fermentation and may be based largely on grape juice or other ingredients. But a fermented dealcoholised product begins as wine, with alcohol removed afterwards.

Reading the product description and alcohol statement is therefore more useful than assuming every bottle labelled with a non-alcoholic term was made in the same way.

Can non-alcoholic wine contain alcohol?

It can, depending on the product and the terminology being used.

In Australia, dealcoholised wine may retain a small amount of alcohol after processing. Wine Australia guidance discusses products labelled with statements such as less than 0.5% or less than 1.0% alcohol by volume. “Dealcoholised” should therefore not automatically be read as “absolute zero”.

Australian guidance also cautions against representing a product containing alcohol as non-alcoholic or alcohol-free in a way that misleads consumers. The words “non-alcoholic”, “alcohol-free” and “zero alcohol” should not be treated as interchangeable with “dealcoholised”, and terminology can differ between export markets.

A short Australian labelling guide

For Australian products, the relevant label information depends on the alcohol concentration and the claim being made.

Broadly:

  • beverages containing 0.5% alcohol by volume or more fall within alcohol-content labelling requirements;
  • products at or below 1.15% alcohol by volume use wording to the effect of “contains not more than X% alcohol by volume”;
  • products above 0.5% alcohol by volume generally require a standard-drinks statement;
  • “low alcohol” is associated with beverages below 1.15% alcohol by volume in the cited Australian guidance.

These are Australian requirements and guidance, not universal international definitions. When the exact alcohol content matters, check the label rather than relying on the product category alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is alcohol removed before or after fermentation?

Usually after fermentation. Most dealcoholised wine starts as fermented wine, then undergoes an alcohol-separation process.

Does every non-alcoholic wine use reverse osmosis?

No. Reverse osmosis is one relevant method. Vacuum distillation and spinning-cone systems are also used, and producers do not all follow the same process.

Does removing alcohol remove flavour?

It can affect flavour and aroma because some volatile compounds may be removed alongside alcohol. Producers may recover or reintroduce selected aromas, but the finished product can still differ from the original wine.

Is dealcoholised wine the same as alcohol-free wine?

Not necessarily. In Australia, a dealcoholised product may contain a small amount of residual alcohol. Terms such as alcohol-free and zero alcohol can carry different labelling implications, so the alcohol statement matters.

The simplest way to remember it

Think of dealcoholised wine as wine first, alcohol reduction second. The wine is fermented, alcohol is separated out, and the producer then works to rebalance the aroma, texture and flavour of the finished drink.
