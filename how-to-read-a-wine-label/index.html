How to Read a Wine Label

Learn how to find the producer, region, grape, vintage, alcohol and other useful clues on an Australian wine label.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • A wine label mixes product facts, origin information, warnings, branding and marketing language.
  • A grape variety is not the same thing as a wine region.
  • A vintage is the grape harvest year, not necessarily the bottling or release year.
  • Alcohol percentage, bottle volume and standard drinks are three different things.
  • A region name can tell you where the grapes came from, but it does not guarantee quality.
  • Words such as “reserve”, “estate”, “old vines” and “dry” need context.
  • The label is a useful starting point, not a complete report on how the wine will taste.

Quick answer: how to read a wine label

Read the label in this order:

  1. Identify the product. Is it wine, sparkling wine, fortified wine or a wine-based beverage?
  2. Look for the producer or brand. These names may not be the same business.
  3. Check the grape variety or blend. A wine may name one variety, several varieties or none.
  4. Check the place. A region such as Barossa Valley or Yarra Valley is different from a country-of-origin statement such as Australia.
  5. Check the vintage. The year usually refers to when the grapes were harvested.
  6. Read the alcohol, volume and standard-drink information. These tell you different things.
  7. Turn the bottle around. Look for the supplier, origin statement, lot code, allergen or preservative information and pregnancy warning where applicable.
  8. Treat tasting notes and impressive-sounding words as guidance, not proof.

That quick process helps you understand what is in the bottle and where it came from. It cannot, by itself, tell you whether the wine is high quality, exactly how sweet it will taste or whether you will enjoy it.

Start with the front label—but check the whole bottle

The front label is usually designed to identify the wine quickly. It may show a brand name, producer, grape variety, region, vintage and style description. It is also the label’s main branding and marketing surface.

The back label often adds practical information, including alcohol percentage, standard drinks, bottle volume, supplier details, tasting notes and serving suggestions. Mandatory information is not always grouped on one side, however, so check the bottle, capsule and both labels rather than assuming the front or back tells the whole story.

A prominent name is not necessarily the formal product designation. A fanciful brand or range name may be large on the front, while the description explaining what the product actually is appears elsewhere.

What do the names mean?

Producer, brand and vineyard

A producer or winery is the business associated with making or preparing the wine. A brand is the commercial name used to identify a wine or range. A vineyard is a named grape-growing property or site.

These can be different. A brand name does not automatically identify the legal producer, and a vineyard name does not automatically mean that the named property made the wine.

You may also see a supplier, vendor, packer or importer. For wine sold in Australia, supplier details help identify the business responsible for supplying the packaged product in this market.

Grape variety or blend

A variety name tells you about the grape used—for example, Shiraz, Riesling or Chardonnay. It does not tell you the wine’s exact flavour, body, sweetness or quality.

Some wines are blends and may name more than one variety. Others may use a broader product description or leave the varieties unstated. A variety claim is not required on every wine label, but claims that are made must be truthful and supported under the applicable Australian requirements.

For a primer on the wider category, see What is wine?. Individual variety pages, such as What is Riesling?, can help when a label names a grape you do not recognise.

Variety versus region: two different clues

A grape variety is a type of grape. A region is a place.

“Chardonnay” and “Riesling” are variety names. “Yarra Valley”, “Adelaide Hills” and “Barossa Valley” are place names that may identify a geographical indication, or GI.

An Australian GI is a protected geographical description for a defined wine-growing area. It can be a zone, region or sub-region. A GI can tell you something about the claimed origin of the wine grapes, but it is not a quality score. A famous region does not guarantee that every bottle will suit you.

A country-of-origin statement is broader. “Australia” identifies the country associated with the product’s origin statement; it does not provide the same level of place information as a named regional GI. Conversely, a winery’s street address does not automatically prove that the grapes came from that location.

What does the vintage year mean?

The vintage is generally the relevant year in which the grapes were harvested. It is not automatically the year the wine was bottled, released or purchased.

For example, a wine labelled 2024 may have been made, matured and bottled after the 2024 harvest. A lot code or bottling date is a separate piece of information.

Vintage can be useful context, but it does not tell you on its own whether a wine is ready to drink or better than another year. Those questions require more information about the wine, its storage and its style.

Alcohol, volume and standard drinks

These three label details are easy to confuse:

  • Alcohol by volume (ABV): the concentration of alcohol in the drink, such as 13.5% alc/vol.
  • Volume: how much liquid is in the container, such as 750 mL.
  • Standard drinks: an estimate of the total ethanol in the package.

In Australia, one standard drink contains 10 grams of ethanol. A 750 mL bottle at 14% ABV contains approximately 8.3 Australian standard drinks.

The standard-drink figure usually refers to the whole package, not a recommended serving. It also does not tell you how many glasses a particular person should drink.

A higher ABV does not automatically mean a wine is fuller-bodied, and a smaller bottle does not necessarily contain fewer standard drinks if its alcohol concentration is higher.

Check the practical information

Country of origin and supplier

Wine sold in Australia must carry a text country-of-origin statement. Imported wine must also meet Australian requirements when sold here, although labels made for export markets may use different conventions.

The supplier’s name and Australian business address can help you identify whom to contact about the product. This address may be different from the vineyard or region where the grapes were grown.

Lot or batch code

A lot or batch code supports traceability. It can help a retailer, producer or regulator identify a production run if there is a recall or product issue.

Do not assume a lot code is a bottling date or vintage. It is primarily an identification code.

Allergens, preservatives and pregnancy warnings

Wine may contain or require declarations for substances such as sulphites, depending on the ingredient, concentration and applicable exemptions. Added sulphites must be declared when present at or above the applicable threshold of 10 mg/kg. Relevant allergen information may also appear in the required plain-English format.

Packaged alcoholic beverages above 1.15% ABV sold at retail in Australia and New Zealand must display the prescribed pregnancy warning, subject to limited exceptions. This warning is regulatory information, not a style or quality cue.

Labelling rules can change, and imported products may have additional requirements. If you have an allergy or need precise product information, use the label as a starting point and contact the producer or supplier.

Which words are facts—and which are marketing?

A label can contain several kinds of language.

Product facts include the stated variety, vintage, GI, alcohol percentage, volume, standard drinks, origin and lot information. These claims still need to comply with applicable requirements, but they are different from advertising copy.

Tasting notes such as “plum”, “citrus”, “floral”, “fresh” or “silky” describe expected sensory character. They are not guarantees that every drinker will perceive the same flavours.

Marketing terms such as “reserve”, “premium”, “estate”, “old vines”, “limited release” and “special selection” may have specific meanings in some contexts but are not universal guarantees of quality. “Dry” should also be treated carefully: it is not simply a promise that the wine will taste flavourless, and its meaning can depend on the wine and the relevant rules.

An elegant label, prestigious-looking crest or expensive paper does not prove that the wine is better. Nor does an unfamiliar label suggest that the wine is poor.

What a wine label cannot tell you

A label cannot reliably establish:

  • the wine’s exact sweetness from the design or a single adjective;
  • whether it is excellent or good value;
  • whether it is ready to drink;
  • how it was farmed, unless a credible and relevant claim or certification supports that information;
  • whether it will suit your personal taste;
  • whether a regional style description applies perfectly to this particular bottle.

When the label leaves questions unanswered, look for reliable information from the producer, the relevant region or a trustworthy explanation of the grape variety. The label gives you a map; it is not the whole journey.

Frequently asked questions

Does every wine label show the grape variety?

No. Variety is useful when stated, but not every wine label names a grape variety. Some wines are blends or use another product designation.

Is the vintage the bottling year?

No. Vintage generally refers to the relevant grape harvest year. Bottling, release and lot information are separate.

Does a region name guarantee quality?

No. A regional name or GI is an origin claim, not a quality rating.

Does a higher alcohol percentage mean a fuller wine?

Not necessarily. Alcohol is one part of a wine’s structure and does not independently determine body.

Are tasting notes factual?

They are descriptions of expected aroma or flavour, not guarantees of what every person will taste.

Why do some labels look different from others?

Labelling conventions vary by wine style, producer, jurisdiction and export market. Australian domestic requirements should not be assumed to apply everywhere.

The simple takeaway

Use a wine label to answer three questions: What is this product? Where is it from? What practical information do I need to know? Then separate those facts from branding and tasting language.

That is enough to make an unfamiliar bottle feel much less mysterious.
