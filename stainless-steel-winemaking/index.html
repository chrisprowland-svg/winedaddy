What Is Stainless-Steel Winemaking? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what stainless-steel winemaking means, why tanks are used for fermentation and storage, how steel affects oxygen and temperature management, and why “unoaked” does not always mean stainless steel.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Stainless-steel winemaking means using stainless-steel tanks or vessels during one or more stages of production.
  • Tanks may be used for fermentation, settling, storage, maturation, blending and transfers.
  • Stainless steel is relatively neutral: it does not add oak-derived flavour or tannin.
  • Stainless steel can support temperature control and careful oxygen management, but neither is automatic.
  • “Unoaked” and “stainless-steel fermented” do not mean exactly the same thing.
  • Stainless steel is used for Australian white, red, rosé and sparkling wines—not only crisp white wines.
  • The vessel influences a wine, but grape variety, yeast, temperature, lees, oxygen, oak and blending matter too.

Quick answer: what does stainless steel do in winemaking?

Stainless-steel winemaking is the use of stainless-steel tanks for one or more parts of making wine. A tank might hold grape juice during fermentation, allow solids to settle, store finished wine, support maturation on lees, or be used for blending and transfers.

The attraction is practical as well as stylistic. Stainless steel is durable, relatively easy to clean and generally does not contribute oak-derived flavour or tannin. Many tanks can also be fitted with temperature-control systems, while closed designs can help winemakers manage oxygen exposure.

But stainless steel is a vessel choice, not a complete wine style. A wine made in stainless steel is not automatically fresh, crisp, unoaked or reductive. The result also depends on the grapes, fermentation, yeast, temperature, lees contact, oxygen management, malolactic fermentation, time in vessel and blending.

How are stainless-steel tanks used?

Stainless-steel tanks can be involved at several points in the winemaking process:

  • Fermentation: grape juice or fermenting wine is held in the tank while yeast converts sugar into alcohol.
  • Settling and clarification: solids can settle before the clearer wine is moved or processed.
  • Storage: wine may be held in tank between production stages or before bottling.
  • Maturation: wine can spend time in stainless steel, including on its lees—the fine sediment left after fermentation.
  • Blending and transfers: tanks can be used to combine wines or move them through the cellar.

That does not mean every stainless-steel tank is used for all these jobs. “Fermented in stainless steel” tells you about fermentation; it does not necessarily describe where the wine was later stored or matured.

Some tanks are open, while others are closed. An open tank gives the winemaker easier access for certain cellar work. A closed tank provides more controlled headspace and gas-management options. Neither is automatically better, and a closed tank is not automatically oxygen-free.

Why do winemakers choose stainless steel?

Cleanability and durability

Stainless steel is a robust cellar material with smooth internal surfaces that can be cleaned after fermentation, settling, fining and stabilisation. Cleanability matters because a tank is not hygienic simply because it is made from stainless steel: it still needs appropriate cellar cleaning and maintenance.

Temperature-control compatibility

Fermentation creates heat. Some stainless-steel tanks have cooling or heating jackets, allowing winemakers to manage temperature as fermentation or storage conditions change. This can be especially useful when a particular fermentation temperature is part of the intended process.

However, stainless steel alone does not provide precise temperature control. The tank’s equipment, insulation, size, cellar conditions and operating decisions all matter.

A relatively neutral material

Unlike oak, stainless steel does not supply wood-derived flavour, aroma or tannin. This gives winemakers a way to work with fruit, acidity, yeast and lees without adding oak character from the vessel itself.

“Relatively neutral” is more accurate than “completely inert”. Stainless steel still affects the production environment through its shape, closure, temperature-control options, oxygen-management possibilities and lees-handling conditions.

Does stainless steel make wine taste fresh?

It can support a fresh, fruit-focused style, but it does not guarantee one.

A closed, well-managed tank may help limit unnecessary oxygen exposure. Temperature control may help protect particular fermentation goals. Avoiding oak also means the wine will not receive oak-derived character from that vessel.

But oxygen can enter through headspace, sampling, pumps, transfers, stirring and other cellar actions. A stainless-steel tank can therefore be used in a low-oxygen process—or in a process where oxygen is deliberately introduced.

The same principle applies to flavour and texture. Wine matured in stainless steel can still have considerable texture or complexity from grape ripeness, fermentation, lees contact, malolactic fermentation, skin contact and blending. The tank does not make all those decisions on its own.

Stainless steel, oak, concrete and clay

At a beginner level, the main distinction is the material and the production environment it provides:

  • Stainless steel: generally relatively neutral, durable and compatible with temperature control and closed handling. It does not add oak-derived flavour or tannin.
  • Oak: can contribute wood-derived aroma, flavour and tannin, as well as providing a different maturation environment.
  • Concrete: behaves differently depending on its construction, lining, shape and temperature-control design. It is not automatically porous or flavour-producing.
  • Clay or amphora: covers several kinds of ceramic and clay vessels. Their effect depends on the material, firing, shape, sealing and winemaking choices.

None of these vessels is inherently superior. A winemaker may also use more than one type for the same wine—for example, stainless steel for one stage and oak or concrete for another.

What is the difference between “unoaked” and “stainless-steel”?

These terms describe different things.

  • Unoaked generally means the wine has not received oak influence.
  • Stainless-steel fermented means fermentation took place in stainless steel.
  • Stainless-steel matured means the wine was held in stainless steel after fermentation.

A wine can be fermented in stainless steel and later matured in oak. Conversely, an unoaked wine might be made or held in stainless steel, concrete, clay, glass or another vessel that does not provide oak influence.

This distinction is particularly useful when reading Australian wine descriptions such as “unoaked Chardonnay”. The phrase tells you about oak influence, not necessarily the wine’s complete vessel history.

Is stainless steel used for all kinds of wine?

No. Australian research and wine education sources document stainless-steel use in several contexts, including cold-fermented Semillon and Sauvignon Blanc, red-wine fermentation, rosé-style small-lot production and tank fermentation for some sparkling wines.

That variety is a useful reminder: stainless steel is not shorthand for one grape variety or one flavour profile. It is a flexible production tool used alongside decisions about fruit, fermentation, oxygen, lees, oak and blending.

What can you tell from a label?

A reference to stainless steel usually tells you that the vessel was relevant at a particular stage. It may not tell you whether the wine was fermented, stored, matured, blended or transferred exclusively in stainless steel.

If the production method matters to you, look for the precise wording:

  • “fermented in stainless steel” describes fermentation;
  • “matured in stainless steel” describes time after fermentation;
  • “unoaked” describes the absence of oak influence.

The most useful takeaway is simple: stainless steel tells you about the winemaking environment, not the entire personality of the wine.

Frequently asked questions

Is stainless-steel wine always unoaked?

No. Stainless steel does not add oak-derived flavour, but a wine fermented in stainless steel may later be matured in oak.

Is stainless steel better than oak?

Neither is automatically better. They provide different production environments and different opportunities for the winemaker.

Does stainless steel prevent oxidation?

No. A closed, well-managed tank can help limit unnecessary oxygen exposure, but transfers, headspace, pumps and other cellar actions still matter.

Is stainless steel only used for white wine?

No. It is also used for red, rosé and sparkling-wine production.
