Greco di Tufo: a beginner’s guide to the Italian wine

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Greco di Tufo is a protected Italian wine denomination from Irpinia in Campania. It is best known for dry white wines made mainly from the Greco grape, but the denomination also includes traditional-method sparkling wines.

The important distinction is this: Greco is a grape; Greco di Tufo is a protected place-based denomination. A wine labelled simply “Greco” is not automatically from Greco di Tufo.

Highlights

  • Greco di Tufo is an Italian DOCG and European DOP/PDO.
  • It comes from eight municipalities in the Province of Avellino, in Campania.
  • The name comes from Tufo, the best-known town in the denomination.
  • Wines must contain at least 85% Greco and may contain up to 15% Coda di Volpe.
  • The denomination covers still white wines and traditional-method sparkling wines.
  • Still examples are legally dry, fresh and harmonious.
  • In the glass, Greco di Tufo is often lively, textural and savoury, with citrus, orchard-fruit or herbal notes.
  • DOCG tells you the wine has a regulated origin and production framework—not that every bottle tastes the same.

Where is Greco di Tufo?

Greco di Tufo sits in southern Italy, within this broad hierarchy:

Italy → Campania → Province of Avellino → Irpinia → Greco di Tufo production area

The legal production area covers the entire administrative territories of eight municipalities:

  • Tufo
  • Altavilla Irpina
  • Chianche
  • Montefusco
  • Prata di Principato Ultra
  • Petruro Irpino
  • Santa Paolina
  • Torrioni

Tufo is the most recognisable orientation point, but it does not represent the whole denomination. Avellino is the nearest useful regional centre. From Naples, the area is roughly an hour’s drive north-east, depending on the route, traffic and exact destination. That is a broad travel orientation rather than a guaranteed journey time.

The vineyards are hilly and inland, around the Sabato River and near the boundary of the Province of Benevento. The area includes clayey-calcareous soils as well as volcanic and other mineral materials. Elevation and exposure vary across the communes, so wines from different sites can differ in weight, texture and freshness.

What does DOCG mean?

DOCG stands for Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita, or “Controlled and Guaranteed Designation of Origin”. In plain English, it is a regulated Italian designation that sets rules for where a wine comes from and how it is made.

For an Australian wine drinker, DOCG is best understood as an origin and production framework. It is not a universal ranking system, and it does not guarantee that every bottle will have the same flavour or quality.

Greco di Tufo is also recognised at European level as a DOP or PDO—terms referring to protected designation of origin.

For more background, see our guide to Italian DOC and DOCG.

What grapes are used?

Greco di Tufo must contain at least 85% Greco. Up to 15% Coda di Volpe is permitted.

That rule explains why it is inaccurate to describe every bottle as 100% Greco. It also reinforces the difference between the grape and the denomination:

  • Greco is a grape variety.
  • Greco di Tufo is a protected denomination linked to a defined area.
  • A wine made from Greco somewhere else is not automatically Greco di Tufo.
  • A Greco di Tufo wine may legally include some Coda di Volpe.

The practical lesson is simple: the complete denomination name on the label tells you more than the isolated word “Greco”.

What styles does the denomination include?

Greco di Tufo covers four recognised categories:

  • Greco di Tufo
  • Greco di Tufo Riserva
  • Greco di Tufo Spumante
  • Greco di Tufo Spumante Riserva

The first two are still white wines. Under the specification, the standard still wine has a minimum total acidity of 5.0 grams per litre and a minimum alcohol level of 11.5% by volume. Riserva requires at least 12 months’ ageing, calculated from 1 November of the harvest year.

The Spumante categories are sparkling wines made by the traditional method, with a second fermentation in the bottle. Standard Spumante requires at least 18 months on lees, while Spumante Riserva requires at least 36 months. “Lees” are the sediment left after fermentation; contact with them can contribute texture and savoury complexity.

The sparkling wines can range from extra brut to extra dry. Those terms describe sweetness categories for sparkling wine and should not be confused with the general meaning of “dry” in still wine.

What does Greco di Tufo taste like?

The legal specification describes still Greco di Tufo as dry, fresh and harmonious, with an intense and characteristic aroma.

Beyond those legal descriptions, wine-industry sources commonly describe it as a medium- to full-bodied white with lively acidity, firm or phenolic texture and a savoury or saline impression. It may show citrus, orchard fruit, stone fruit, floral, herbal, smoky, mineral or nutty notes.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. Site, vintage and winemaking can shift the style. Some examples are direct and refreshing, while others feel broader, more viscous and more textured. A wine’s fruitiness also does not mean it is sweet: a dry wine can still smell and taste of ripe fruit.

Because the wines combine acidity and structure, some examples can develop in bottle. That does not mean every bottle needs ageing or that there is one universal drinking window.

Greco di Tufo versus Fiano di Avellino

Fiano di Avellino is a separate DOCG in the same broad Irpinian and Avellino context. It is not another name for Greco di Tufo, and Greco di Tufo is not a sub-category of Fiano di Avellino.

The two denominations are useful companions when learning about Campanian white wine, but each has its own permitted grapes and geographic rules. You can explore the grape Fiano separately.

How to recognise it on a label

Look for wording such as:

  • Greco di Tufo
  • Greco di Tufo DOCG
  • Greco di Tufo Riserva
  • Greco di Tufo Spumante
  • Greco di Tufo Spumante Riserva

Still-category labels must show the vintage under the specification, while the Spumante categories are exempt from that specific vintage requirement.

A bottle marked only “Greco” may come from another denomination or geographic origin. It should not be assumed to come from the eight-municipality Greco di Tufo area.

How should you serve it?

For still Greco di Tufo, start at around 8–12°C. Serve fresher examples towards the cooler end of that range, while allowing fuller or older wines to warm slightly in the glass.

Its combination of dryness, acidity and texture makes it a useful match for food. Try it with:

  • grilled prawns or fish;
  • lemon-and-herb chicken;
  • creamy seafood or mushroom pasta;
  • roast vegetables with salty cheese;
  • ricotta, feta or other moderately salty cheeses.

These are starting points, not rules. The best match depends on the intensity, seasoning and preparation of the dish.

Frequently asked questions

Is Greco di Tufo always made from 100% Greco?

No. It must contain at least 85% Greco and may contain up to 15% Coda di Volpe.

Is Greco di Tufo a grape or a region?

It is a protected wine denomination. “Greco” is the grape name; “Greco di Tufo” combines grape identity with a legally defined place and production framework.

Is Greco di Tufo always a still white wine?

No. The denomination also includes traditional-method sparkling wines labelled Spumante and Spumante Riserva.

Is DOCG a guarantee that the wine will be excellent?

No. DOCG confirms regulated origin and production requirements. It does not make every bottle identical or guarantee that every drinker will prefer it.

Is Greco di Tufo the same as Fiano di Avellino?

No. They are separate DOCGs from the broader Irpinia and Avellino area, with different grape and geographic rules.
