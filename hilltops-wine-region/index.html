Hilltops wine region: location, grapes and wine styles

Understand the Hilltops wine region in southern New South Wales, including its GI status, location, climate, key grape varieties and what “Hilltops” means on an Australian wine label.

Quick answer: what is the Hilltops wine region?

Hilltops is a recognised Australian wine region and protected geographical indication in southern New South Wales. It sits within the Southern New South Wales zone, on the elevated south-western slopes of the Great Dividing Range.

For everyday orientation, think of the country around Young, Harden–Murrumburrah and Boorowa. Young is the most useful regional centre for many visitors and wine drinkers. Hilltops lies roughly two hours by road north-west of Canberra, although the actual time depends on your route and which part of the region you visit.

The region’s established wine identity is based on Cabernet Sauvignon and Shiraz. Its cooler, elevated setting also suits a broader group of varieties, including Tempranillo, Nebbiolo, Prosecco/Glera, Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris and Sangiovese.

In short, Hilltops is best understood as an elevated, diverse southern New South Wales wine region: traditionally associated with structured reds, but increasingly interesting for alternative varieties and fresher white and sparkling styles too.

Where is Hilltops?

Wine Australia places Hilltops in the Southern New South Wales zone of New South Wales. The GI is a formal geographic term used for wine labelling, rather than simply a description of a town or local government area.

Young, Harden–Murrumburrah and Boorowa are useful places to use when picturing the region. They are orientation points, not a replacement for the GI’s legal boundary. The Hilltops Council area is an administrative area that overlaps the practical wine landscape, but it should not automatically be treated as identical to the wine GI.

Hilltops is also distinct from nearby wine regions. Cowra, Orange, Gundagai, Canberra District and Tumbarumba are separate GIs, even when they appear alongside Hilltops in broader discussions of southern or central New South Wales wine.

What are Hilltops’ landscape and climate like?

Hilltops is an elevated and undulating wine-growing area. Wine Australia’s 2024–25 regional snapshot records vineyards across approximately 249 to 716 metres in elevation. It also records 591 hectares of vineyard area and long-term annual rainfall of 658 millimetres.

The regional climate is described as cool continental, with chilly winters, warm summers, cool nights during ripening and generally settled autumn conditions. Dry summer and autumn conditions are also an important part of the regional description, with irrigation considered important for viticulture.

The soils are described by Wine Australia as deep, rich soils that commonly include dark red granite clays with basalt influence. They can retain water at depth while remaining free-draining, particularly across ridge tops and hillsides.

These details help explain why Hilltops wines can combine ripe flavour with freshness and structure. They do not dictate the character of every bottle. Variety, vineyard position, season, crop levels and winemaking choices all influence the finished wine.

Which grape varieties is Hilltops known for?

Cabernet Sauvignon and Shiraz

Cabernet Sauvignon and Shiraz established Hilltops’ traditional red-wine identity.

Hilltops Cabernet Sauvignon is often described through savoury and briar-like characters, intense flavour and a full, mellow palate. Hilltops Shiraz is associated with red-berry fruit, pepper and spice, full flavour and mid-tannin structure.

That does not mean every Hilltops Cabernet or Shiraz tastes the same. A lighter, earlier-picked wine can feel very different from a fuller wine matured with oak.

Tempranillo and Nebbiolo

Tempranillo is an early-ripening variety associated with mellow, mid-weight red wines in Hilltops.

Nebbiolo is one of the region’s more distinctive emerging varieties. Regional examples are often presented as structured and intense, making it a useful variety to look for if you want to explore beyond the region’s traditional Cabernet and Shiraz.

Prosecco, Pinot Grigio and other varieties

Prosecco, made from Glera, is another increasingly visible Hilltops variety. It can be used for zesty, lemon-fresh sparkling wines, although the exact style should always be checked on the bottle.

Pinot Grigio and Pinot Gris can offer different expressions depending on harvest timing and winemaking. Earlier-picked examples may be crisp and fresh, while fuller Pinot Gris styles can be more rounded and fruit-driven.

Sangiovese, Fiano, Corvina and Rondinella are among the alternative varieties associated with plantings in the region. They broaden Hilltops beyond a simple “Cabernet and Shiraz region” label.

What does Hilltops wine usually taste like?

There is no single Hilltops flavour profile, but the region’s elevation, cool nights and dry ripening conditions help explain a common combination of:

  • Freshness and natural-looking lift.
  • Concentrated fruit flavour.
  • Savoury character in some reds.
  • Pepper and spice, particularly in Shiraz.
  • Tannin and structure in Cabernet Sauvignon, Shiraz and Nebbiolo.
  • Crispness in some white and sparkling wines.

Treat these as regional tendencies, not promises. A wine labelled Hilltops does not automatically have a particular body, sweetness level, oak character or flavour profile.

What does “Hilltops” mean on a wine label?

“ Hilltops” on an Australian wine label is a claim about geographic origin. It is not a grape variety, a sweetness category or a guarantee of quality.

For a wine making a single-GI claim, Wine Australia states that at least 85 per cent of the fruit must come from within the named GI, subject to the applicable labelling rules. The regional indication therefore gives you a meaningful clue about where the grapes came from, but it does not tell the whole story.

When choosing between Hilltops wines, also check:

  • The grape variety.
  • The vintage.
  • The producer.
  • Whether the wine is dry or sweet.
  • The likely body and acidity.
  • Any stated oak treatment.
  • The serving occasion or food you have in mind.

A Hilltops Cabernet Sauvignon and a Hilltops sparkling wine may share a regional origin while offering completely different experiences.

How should you compare Hilltops wines?

Start with variety. Choose Cabernet Sauvignon or Shiraz for a structured red starting point, or try Tempranillo, Nebbiolo or Sangiovese for something different. Prosecco/Glera and Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris provide a way to explore the region’s lighter and fresher side.

Then consider vintage. Different seasons can influence ripeness, acidity and tannin. Finally, remember that producer and winemaking choices matter: oak, harvest timing and extraction can change the shape of a wine even when the region and variety are the same.

The most useful question is not “What does every Hilltops wine taste like?” It is “What does this producer, variety and vintage suggest about this particular bottle?”

In summary

Hilltops is a protected wine GI in the Southern New South Wales zone of New South Wales, practically centred around Young, Harden–Murrumburrah and Boorowa. Its elevated, cool continental setting helps explain wines with freshness, flavour and structure.

Cabernet Sauvignon and Shiraz are the established regional reference points, while Tempranillo, Nebbiolo, Prosecco/Glera, Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris and Sangiovese show a broader and developing identity. On a label, “Hilltops” tells you where the wine comes from—not exactly how it will taste. Use the region as your starting clue, then look to the variety, vintage, producer and winemaking details.

Frequently asked questions

Is Hilltops a town?

No. Hilltops is a wine region and protected geographical indication. Young, Harden–Murrumburrah and Boorowa are important orientation points, but the formal GI should not be reduced to a town list.

Is Hilltops part of Canberra District?

No. Hilltops and Canberra District are separate wine GIs, although both are in southern New South Wales and relatively close geographically.

What is Hilltops best known for?

Hilltops is traditionally associated with Cabernet Sauvignon and Shiraz. It is also developing a broader reputation for varieties such as Tempranillo, Nebbiolo, Prosecco/Glera, Pinot Grigio/Pinot Gris and Sangiovese.

Does Hilltops wine have to be red?

No. The region is strongly associated with red varieties, but Hilltops wines can also include white and sparkling styles. The label’s variety and style information will tell you more than the regional name alone.
