How to plan a wine tasting at home

Plan a relaxed home wine tasting with practical advice on choosing wines, serving order, tasting notes, equipment and responsible hosting in Australia.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

A home wine tasting can be as simple as comparing three bottles around a table. You do not need specialist equipment, expert knowledge or a room full of serious faces. Choose a useful theme, serve modest pours in a sensible order, and give everyone time to notice what they notice.

The goal is curiosity—not guessing the “right” answer.

Highlights

  • Compare wines around one clear idea, such as the same grape from different regions.
  • Three to six wines is plenty for a relaxed beginner tasting.
  • Use small pours, water, simple food and notes rather than expensive equipment.
  • Serve lighter, more delicate wines before fuller, sweeter or more intense ones.
  • Tasting notes are personal observations, not an exam.
  • Blind tasting is optional. It can be fun, but it does not prove expertise.
  • Good hosting includes non-alcoholic choices, safe transport and no pressure to drink.

Quick answer: what do you need for a home wine tasting?

Start by deciding what you want guests to learn or compare. You might explore one grape variety from different regions, one region made with different grapes, or different production methods.

For a manageable tasting, invite roughly four to eight people and choose about three to six wines. These are practical starting points, not rules. A smaller group may suit a more detailed discussion, while a larger group may work better as a social activity.

Prepare clean glasses, water, plain crackers or bread, paper and pens, suitable lighting, and a container for wine guests do not wish to swallow. Keep the setting free from strong scents such as candles or flowers, as smell is part of how we experience wine.

Pour a small amount of each wine. Taste from lighter and more delicate wines towards fuller-bodied, sweeter or more intense examples. Ask guests to consider the wine’s appearance, aroma, palate, structure, finish and overall impression.

Most importantly, let people disagree. “I smell lemon” and “I prefer the second wine” are both valid contributions, even if someone else notices apple or dislikes the wine.

Choose a purpose and comparison theme

A tasting becomes easier to plan when it has one main purpose. Is it a social evening with a little learning, an introduction to tasting vocabulary, a comparison of regions, or a blind-tasting game?

Then choose a theme that gives the wines something meaningful in common. Useful options include:

  • the same grape variety from different regions;
  • different grape varieties from one region;
  • the same broad style made in different ways;
  • sparkling wines made using different production methods;
  • dry and sweet wines, if the difference is clear.

A good comparison changes one or two main variables. If every wine is completely unrelated, the event may still be enjoyable, but guests will have less basis for comparison. You do not need to make the wines identical; you simply want a question that the line-up can help answer.

Decide how many wines and how much to pour

More wines do not necessarily create more learning. Three well-chosen examples are often easier to compare than ten wines served quickly.

Small tasting pours of approximately 30–60 millilitres are a practical planning suggestion. They allow guests to compare several wines without filling a glass or encouraging rapid drinking. Keep the pace relaxed and top up only when appropriate.

Check the label when planning quantities. In Australia, one standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol, and packaged alcohol shows standard-drink information. The number of standard drinks in a pour depends on both its size and the wine’s alcohol concentration.

These figures are useful for planning, not a target or a guarantee of safe consumption.

Prepare the room and equipment

You can run a good tasting with ordinary household equipment.

Have ready:

  • one clean glass per guest, or more glasses if you want wines compared side by side;
  • water for drinking and, if needed, rinsing;
  • plain crackers, bread or another neutral option;
  • paper tasting sheets and pens;
  • a container for unwanted wine;
  • good, neutral lighting;
  • non-alcoholic drinks;
  • labels, paper or reusable covers if tasting blind.

A general-purpose wine glass is enough. Different glass shapes can change how aromas and flavours are experienced, but no particular glass is compulsory. If you are interested in the trade-off between glass shapes, see the registered visual VIS-012: Wine glass anatomy and shape comparison.

Avoid strong room scents, smoke and heavily perfumed products. Noise, lighting, room temperature, wine temperature and recently eaten food can all influence perception, so aim for reasonably consistent conditions rather than laboratory perfection.

Serve the wines in a sensible order

A useful broad sequence is:

  1. sparkling wines;
  2. lighter or more delicate whites;
  3. fuller-bodied whites;
  4. rosé;
  5. lighter reds;
  6. fuller-bodied reds;
  7. sweet wines;
  8. fortified wines.

This is a flexible convention, not a universal law. The wines you have chosen may justify a different order. The main idea is to avoid allowing a very sweet, tannic, strongly flavoured or high-alcohol wine to overwhelm a more delicate one.

Warm Australian weather can make chilled wines lose their freshness quickly, so keep them cool and bring them out progressively. Reds should not automatically be served warm: an overheated room can make them seem heavy. Aim for a suitable, reasonably controlled temperature rather than following a rigid number for every bottle.

Open the bottles shortly before the tasting. Advanced decanting is not necessary for a simple home comparison.

Use a simple tasting framework

Give guests a few quiet minutes with each wine. They can taste, spit or discard it; swallowing every sample is not required.

Appearance

Ask:

  • Is the wine still or sparkling?
  • Is it clear or hazy?
  • What colour and depth does it have?

Aroma

Ask:

  • Is the aroma light, moderate or pronounced?
  • Do you notice fruit, flowers, herbs, spice, earth, oak or something else?
  • Does it seem fresh, savoury, sweet, developed or unfamiliar?

There is no need to identify a precise fruit or aroma. Broad observations are a useful starting point.

Palate and structure

Consider:

  • Is the wine dry, off-dry or sweet?
  • Does the acidity feel sharp, soft or somewhere between?
  • Is the body light, medium or full?
  • In red wine, are the tannins barely noticeable or more gripping?
  • Does the texture feel smooth, creamy, chalky or otherwise distinctive?

“Structure” simply means the parts that give a wine shape and weight, including acidity, body, tannin and texture.

Finish and overall impression

What remains after tasting? Does the flavour disappear quickly or continue? What stood out, and how was this wine different from the previous one?

A tasting note is a record of your observations—not a verdict that must match a critic, winemaker or another guest.

Make the conversation welcoming

As host, ask open questions:

  • “What did you notice first?”
  • “Did anyone find a different aroma?”
  • “Which wine felt fresher or fuller?”
  • “What changed when you tasted them side by side?”

Try to separate observation from preference. “I smell lemon” is an observation. “I prefer the second wine” is a preference. “The wine seems high in acidity” is a technical description. None of these needs to be corrected simply because another person experienced the wine differently.

Avoid asking guests to prove themselves. The best tasting may be the one where people become more comfortable describing what they enjoy.

Add blind tasting only if it suits the group

Blind tasting is easy to arrange: cover the labels, number the bottles and reveal them after everyone has discussed the wines.

Guests might guess the grape variety, region, production style or their order of preference. Treat it as a playful learning tool. Hiding the label can reduce preconceptions, but it can also make the wines harder to understand. It is not more intelligent or authentic than an open tasting, and nobody needs to identify a wine correctly for the activity to succeed.

Host responsibly and include everyone

Plan transport before serving. Guests may arrange a sober driver, public transport, a taxi or rideshare, or an overnight stay. Do not assume that counting glasses can tell someone whether they are fit to drive. Australian drink-driving rules include a BAC limit of 0.05 for many drivers, with zero-BAC rules applying to some learner and provisional drivers.

Offer water throughout, keep pours small and never pressure anyone to taste, finish or explain a wine. Food can make the event more comfortable, but it does not remove alcohol risk.

People under 18 should not drink alcohol. Provide appealing non-alcoholic options for guests who are underage, driving, abstaining or simply do not want alcohol. Make participation genuinely optional.

Australian alcohol laws vary between states and territories, so do not treat a private tasting as covered by one simple nationwide service rule. For a specific legal question, check current government guidance for your state or territory.

A simple host checklist

Before guests arrive

  • Choose the purpose and comparison theme.
  • Select a manageable number of wines.
  • Check labels for alcohol content and standard drinks.
  • Prepare wines for suitable serving temperatures.
  • Arrange glasses, water, neutral food and notes.
  • Set out a container for unwanted wine.
  • Provide non-alcoholic drinks.
  • Confirm transport plans.
  • Cover labels only if using blind tasting.

During the tasting

  • Explain that there are no required answers.
  • Pour small amounts.
  • Move from lighter towards fuller or more intense wines.
  • Give guests time to observe and smell.
  • Ask open questions.
  • Allow spitting or discarding.
  • Offer water regularly.
  • Keep the pace relaxed.

Afterwards

  • Reveal blind-tasting identities, if relevant.
  • Discuss differences and preferences.
  • Stop when the learning activity is complete.
  • Help guests follow their transport plans.
  • Store remaining wine safely and keep alcohol away from children.

A successful home tasting is not the one with the most bottles or the most impressive vocabulary. It is the one that helps people pay attention, compare confidently and enjoy the conversation.

Suggested FAQs

Do I need special wine glasses?

No. Clean, suitable household wine glasses are enough. Different shapes may change the tasting experience, but they are not essential.

How many wines should a beginner tasting include?

Around three to six wines is a practical starting point. Choose fewer if the group wants a detailed discussion.

Should every tasting be blind?

No. Open tastings are often easier for beginners because guests can connect the wine with its grape, region or style. Blind tasting is an optional game.

Do I need to spit the wine?

No, but you may. Spitting or discarding samples is a normal way to taste several wines and is never something a host should discourage.

What food should I serve?

Keep it simple. Plain crackers, bread or another neutral option can refresh the palate without turning the event into a detailed food-pairing exercise.
