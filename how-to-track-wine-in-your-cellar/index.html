How to track wine in your cellar

Learn a simple way to track wine in your cellar, including essential inventory fields, location and status updates, tool choices, backups and drinking-window notes.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • A useful cellar record answers four questions: what is the wine, how many bottles do you have, where is it, and what is its status?
  • A spreadsheet, notes app, paper list or dedicated cellar application can all work.
  • Start with the essential details. Extra tasting notes and photos can come later.
  • Update your record whenever a bottle arrives, moves, is opened, gifted, consumed or discarded.
  • A drinking-window note is an estimate, not a promise.
  • The best system is the one you will keep current.

Quick answer

To track wine in your cellar, create one consistent record for each wine or group of identical bottles. Record the producer, wine name, vintage where applicable, bottle size, quantity, location and status.

Your “cellar” might be an underground room, a wine cabinet, a fridge, a cupboard or another organised storage area. You do not need specialist software or a large collection to benefit from an inventory.

Choose the simplest tool you are likely to maintain. A spreadsheet is flexible, phone notes are quick, paper can be perfectly adequate for a small collection, and a dedicated application may offer more structured features. None is automatically the right choice.

The important habit is keeping the record aligned with the bottles. If a bottle moves from a cabinet to the kitchen, is opened for dinner or is given to a friend, update the record.

What your record needs to tell you

A cellar inventory is useful when it helps you find a bottle and make a decision without searching through every shelf. Begin with these essential fields:

Field What to record
Producer The producer or winery name as shown on the bottle
Wine name The full wine or cuvée name
Vintage The year shown on the bottle, where applicable
Variety or style For example, Shiraz, Riesling, rosé or sparkling wine
Bottle size Such as 375 mL, 750 mL or 1.5 L
Quantity The number of bottles currently available
Location A repeatable description of where the bottles are
Status Available, reserved, opened, consumed, gifted, damaged, moved or discarded

The producer and wine name identify the bottle. Variety or style helps you search and choose, but should not replace the full name. Vintage means the year shown on a vintage-dated wine; not every wine has one, so use a consistent notation such as “NV” for non-vintage wine.

Bottle size matters when your collection includes half bottles, standard bottles or magnums. Also be clear about what your quantity means: individual bottles or unopened cases.

Make locations easy to understand

Avoid locations such as “at the back” or “near the red wines”. They may make sense today but become surprisingly unhelpful later.

Instead, use a simple hierarchy:

Cabinet 1 → Shelf B → Position 4

Or:

Wine fridge → Upper shelf → Left

You do not need to reorganise the whole cellar around a particular layout. Just name the spaces consistently. If bottles are stored in several places—a cupboard, a fridge and a box in the garage—include the room or storage area in the location.

A location record is especially useful when someone else in the household needs to find a bottle, or when the collection has grown beyond what you can remember.

Choose a tracking method

Spreadsheet

A spreadsheet is a strong starting point because you can add columns, sort by vintage or style, filter by location and export a copy. Its main weakness is that it depends on you entering information consistently. Different spellings, duplicate rows and forgotten updates can make it unreliable.

Use standard terms for fields such as status and location. For example, choose “consumed” rather than alternating between “drunk”, “gone” and “finished”.

Phone notes

Notes are quick and convenient, particularly for a small collection. A repeatable line might look like this:

Producer — Wine — Vintage — Size — Quantity — Location — Status

Free-form notes become harder to search as the collection grows, so keep the inventory format separate from longer tasting comments.

Dedicated cellar application

A dedicated application may offer structured fields, photographs, searching, scanning or multiple locations. Features vary, however, and may change over time. Consider whether the service requires an account, works on your devices and lets you export your information.

A scan or automated label entry is a convenience, not a guarantee. Check the result against the physical bottle, especially the producer, vintage and bottle size.

Paper list

Paper is accessible and can work well for a small, stable collection. Date the list and use a consistent format. It is also sensible to keep a photograph or digital copy if losing the original would be inconvenient.

The best method is not the most sophisticated one. It is the method you will actually update.

Track status, not just bottles

A quantity count can be misleading if every bottle remains marked as available. Add a status when a bottle is:

  • reserved for a dinner or celebration;
  • opened but not finished;
  • consumed;
  • gifted or returned;
  • damaged or leaking;
  • moved to another location;
  • discarded.

For a small collection, one row with a quantity works when all bottles share the same location and status. Use separate rows when bottles are in different places, have different conditions or are being held for different plans.

Optional details worth adding

Once the essentials are working, add a field only when it helps you find, identify, decide about or remember a bottle. Useful options include:

  • purchase date and retailer;
  • price paid, if useful for household records;
  • provenance or who gave you the bottle;
  • a serving plan;
  • food or occasion notes;
  • personal tasting notes or ratings;
  • a photograph of the label;
  • an estimated drinking window.

A receipt, retailer account or app record can support your information, but it does not replace your own current inventory.

Treat drinking-window notes carefully. Record whether a note comes from the producer, an external reference, your own experience or simply your plan to open the wine. “Review from 2028” communicates uncertainty more honestly than “will be perfect in 2028”. An inventory cannot predict exactly how an individual wine will develop.

A simple setup workflow

If your cellar is new—or your existing list is out of date—use this process:

  1. Decide what you want the record to do: locate bottles, count stock, plan occasions, remember tasting experiences, or a combination.
  2. Choose one tool and start with the least complicated option you will maintain.
  3. Set a consistent order for each record.
  4. Name your rooms, cabinets, shelves, racks, bins or boxes.
  5. Work through one defined area at a time.
  6. Read the bottle label rather than guessing the producer, wine name, vintage or volume.
  7. Record quantity, location and status immediately.
  8. Add optional fields only after the essentials are complete.
  9. Compare the record with the physical bottles and correct discrepancies.
  10. Save a backup or exportable copy.

For an existing collection, you do not have to catalogue everything perfectly in one sitting. Start with the area you use most, then expand. Mark uncertain entries as “needs checking” rather than inventing missing details.

Keep it current

Update the record whenever:

  • new wine arrives;
  • a bottle changes location;
  • a case is split;
  • a bottle is reserved;
  • a bottle is opened or finished;
  • wine is gifted, returned, damaged or discarded;
  • you discover that a label detail or quantity is wrong.

Updating at the moment of change is easier than trying to reconstruct several months of activity later. If you have hosted a dinner or moved several bottles, do a quick physical check. Review the whole record periodically, with the timing based on how often your collection changes.

A short, accurate list is more useful than a detailed list that has quietly become stale.

Keep digital records sensible

If your inventory is digital, avoid sharing more information than necessary. Think carefully before recording your home address, access details or unusually valuable holdings. Review what an application collects, protect your account and keep a backup or exportable copy.

A backup is most useful when you know it can be opened. Periodically check the copy rather than assuming it will work when you need it.

Australian homes and storage spaces vary considerably by region and season. You can add a simple condition note if you observe a problem, but this article is about tracking bottles—not detailed advice on temperature, humidity, bottle orientation or cellar construction.

FAQs

Do I need a cellar app?

No. A spreadsheet, notes system or paper list may be a better fit for a small collection. Choose based on what you will maintain, not on how serious your collection looks.

Should I record every bottle separately?

Not always. Group identical bottles when they share a location and status. Use separate records when their locations, condition or plans differ.

What does “vintage” mean in an inventory?

It is the year shown on a vintage-dated wine. Non-vintage wines do not have one; use a consistent entry such as “NV” instead of leaving the field unclear.

Should I record how long a wine will last?

You can record an estimated drinking window as a planning aid, but it is not a promise. Identify the source of the estimate and keep your own experience separate from producer or external guidance.
