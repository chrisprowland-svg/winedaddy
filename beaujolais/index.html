Beaujolais: A Beginner’s Guide to Gamay, Crus and Nouveau

Understand Beaujolais, its Gamay-led wines, appellation hierarchy, ten crus, Beaujolais Nouveau and carbonic maceration.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Beaujolais is a French wine region best known for bright, fragrant red wines made from Gamay. It sits south of the Mâconnais and north of Lyon, close to Burgundy but with its own appellations, grape identity and range of styles.

The easiest way to understand Beaujolais is to separate three ideas:

  • Beaujolais is the broad regional appellation.
  • Beaujolais-Villages is a more specific appellation covering qualifying villages.
  • The ten Beaujolais crus are named communal appellations, such as Fleurie, Morgon and Moulin-à-Vent.

Beaujolais Nouveau is one young-release style from this region—not another name for Beaujolais as a whole.

Highlights

  • Beaujolais is in France, broadly between Mâcon and Lyon.
  • It is geographically adjacent to Burgundy but is best understood as a distinct wine region.
  • Gamay noir à jus blanc, usually shortened to Gamay, is the defining red grape.
  • Wines range from fresh and light-bodied to more structured and ageworthy.
  • Beaujolais is associated with carbonic and semi-carbonic maceration, but neither technique describes every wine.
  • Beaujolais Nouveau is released young, traditionally on the third Thursday of November.
  • A label’s appellation—Beaujolais, Beaujolais-Villages or a cru—gives useful clues about origin and likely style.

Where is Beaujolais?

Beaujolais lies in France, south of the Mâconnais and north of Lyon, on the western side of the Saône valley. Mâcon is a useful orientation point for the northern end of the region, while Villefranche-sur-Saône is a practical regional centre. Lyon is the nearest major city for broad travel orientation.

From Lyon, some parts of Beaujolais can be reached in roughly 45–75 minutes by road, although the actual time depends on the destination, traffic and route. Treat that as a sense of direction rather than a precise travel promise.

Beaujolais is often discussed alongside Burgundy because of its location, history and proximity. For a wine learner, however, it is more useful to treat Beaujolais as a distinct region with its own appellation structure and dominant grape. The Mâconnais is immediately north and is a separate wine area within Burgundy’s broader wine geography.

The Beaujolais appellation hierarchy

An appellation is a regulated geographic name with production rules. On a bottle, it tells you more than just where the grapes were grown: it can also indicate the level of geographic specificity and the kind of style you might expect.

Beaujolais

Beaujolais is the broadest regional appellation. It is the least specific of the three main label categories, although individual wines can still show plenty of character.

The regional appellation also covers some white and rosé production. Beaujolais is overwhelmingly associated with red wine, but it is not accurate to assume that every bottle from the appellation is red or made from Gamay.

Beaujolais-Villages

Beaujolais-Villages covers qualifying villages, mainly in the northern part of the vineyard. It sits between the broad Beaujolais appellation and the named crus in the usual learner-friendly hierarchy.

The name does not mean that the wine comes from one village called “Beaujolais-Villages”. It is an appellation covering a group of qualifying communes. Wines can be fresh and fruit-led, or show more concentration and structure, depending on site, vintage and winemaking.

The ten crus

The ten crus are named communal appellations:

  • Brouilly
  • Chénas
  • Chiroubles
  • Côte de Brouilly
  • Fleurie
  • Juliénas
  • Morgon
  • Moulin-à-Vent
  • Régnié
  • Saint-Amour

A cru name on the front label—such as Morgon or Fleurie—is an appellation, not simply a producer’s blend name. Cru wines are not automatically better, fuller or more expensive, but the name gives a more specific sense of place than a broad regional label.

Some cru wines are fragrant and fine-boned; others can be darker, firmer and more structured. Morgon and Moulin-à-Vent are often discussed as having ageing potential, but the result still depends on the individual wine, vintage and producer.

What grape is Beaujolais made from?

Gamay noir à jus blanc is the defining red grape of Beaujolais. In everyday wine conversation, it is usually called Gamay.

Gamay-based Beaujolais often shows red-fruit and floral aromas, fresh acidity and relatively supple tannins. That description is a tendency, not a guarantee. Ripeness, site, vintage and winemaking can all change the balance of fruit, freshness, body and tannin.

The regional Beaujolais appellation also has a white-wine context, with Chardonnay relevant to Beaujolais blanc. That should not be confused with the red-wine identity of the ten crus.

Why do Beaujolais wines taste different?

Beaujolais is not one uniform landscape. Its soils and sites vary, including granite-derived, sandy, clay-limestone and other geological materials. The northern vineyards include sloping sites, with some vineyards located at approximately 200–500 metres above sea level.

These factors can influence how a wine develops, alongside climate, exposure, drainage, vine age, vintage and winemaking. They do not dictate a single flavour.

A useful beginner’s expectation is a range:

  • Regional Beaujolais may be fresh, fruit-led and relatively light to medium-bodied.
  • Beaujolais-Villages may show additional concentration or structure while remaining energetic and approachable.
  • Cru Beaujolais can range from fragrant and delicate to firm, savoury and more powerful.

It is also worth remembering that fruity does not mean sweet. A dry wine can smell and taste strongly of berries. Freshness usually relates to acidity, while body describes the wine’s weight or fullness in the mouth. These are different parts of the experience.

What is Beaujolais Nouveau?

Beaujolais Nouveau is a young-release, or primeur, category. It is not the whole Beaujolais region.

Nouveau wines are made under the Beaujolais or Beaujolais-Villages appellations and are intended to be released soon after the vintage. The established traditional release convention is the third Thursday of November.

That timing is part of the wine’s identity: it gives drinkers an early impression of the new vintage. Nouveau is generally associated with youthful fruit and immediate drinking, but it should not be used as a shortcut for judging all Beaujolais wines. A cru wine from the same broad region may have a very different texture, concentration and ageing trajectory.

What are carbonic and semi-carbonic maceration?

Maceration is the period when grape juice remains in contact with grape solids such as skins and, sometimes, whole bunches. In red winemaking, this affects colour, aroma, flavour and tannin.

Carbonic maceration places intact grapes in a carbon-dioxide-rich environment. Fermentation begins inside individual berries before conventional fermentation becomes the main process.

Semi-carbonic maceration, strongly associated with Beaujolais, is a mixed process. Whole bunches are placed in a vat. Grapes at the bottom are crushed by their own weight, and the fermenting juice produces carbon dioxide. That atmosphere affects intact bunches higher in the vat. The process later combines with more conventional fermentation, pressing and extraction.

These techniques can contribute to lifted fruit, floral aromas and a softer impression of tannin. But the final wine also depends on temperature, maceration length, whole-bunch use, extraction, vessels, yeast and maturation.

So the useful statement is: Beaujolais is associated with carbonic and semi-carbonic techniques, but not every Beaujolais wine is made in exactly the same way. For more technical detail, see carbonic maceration and semi-carbonic maceration.

How should an Australian shopper read a Beaujolais label?

French labels often foreground place rather than grape variety. When choosing or discussing a bottle, look for:

  • Beaujolais — the broad regional appellation.
  • Beaujolais-Villages — a more specific appellation covering qualifying villages.
  • A cru name — such as Fleurie, Brouilly or Morgon.
  • Beaujolais Nouveau — a young-release designation under Beaujolais or Beaujolais-Villages.
  • The vintage — the year the grapes were harvested.
  • Any variety information — useful, but not always displayed as prominently as it would be on an Australian label.

French AOC/AOP rules and Australian labelling conventions do not operate identically. The safest approach is to read the appellation, vintage, producer information and any variety statement together. For background, see French AOC and AOP.

How should Beaujolais be served?

Many fresh Beaujolais wines can be enjoyable served slightly cool rather than warm. A young Nouveau may suit a cooler serving temperature, while a more structured cru can benefit from being served less chilled.

There is no single rigid temperature that suits every bottle. The practical approach is to start slightly cool and let the wine warm gradually in the glass.

Beaujolais’ freshness, fruit and moderate tannin can work well with foods such as roast poultry, charcuterie, sausages, mushrooms, grilled vegetables and lighter meat dishes. These are useful starting points rather than rules.

Frequently asked questions

Is Beaujolais part of Burgundy?

Beaujolais is commonly taught as a distinct wine region with its own appellations and Gamay-led identity. It is immediately adjacent to Burgundy and may be grouped with Burgundy in broader historical or institutional contexts, so the answer depends on the context. For reading a wine label, treating Beaujolais as distinct is the clearest approach.

Is Beaujolais always light-bodied?

No. Many regional examples are relatively light to medium-bodied, but Beaujolais-Villages and cru wines can show greater concentration, tannin and structure. Individual site, vintage and winemaking matter.

Is Beaujolais Nouveau the same as Beaujolais?

No. Beaujolais Nouveau is a young-release category made under the Beaujolais or Beaujolais-Villages appellations. It represents one style and release point within the wider region.

Is Beaujolais always made using carbonic maceration?

No. Carbonic and semi-carbonic maceration are strongly associated with Beaujolais, but winemaking varies between wines.

What should I remember first?

Remember the hierarchy: Beaujolais is the region and broad appellation; Beaujolais-Villages is more specific; the crus are named communal appellations; and Nouveau is a young-release style. Gamay is the grape that connects the region’s best-known red wines.
