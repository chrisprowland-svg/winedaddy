Wine Fundamentals

Describing wine without jargon

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

You do not need specialist vocabulary, a perfect palate or a formal score to describe wine well. Start with what you notice: what it smells like, what happens in your mouth, how long the sensations last and whether you enjoy them.

A clear description is not a test. It is a useful way to communicate your experience to yourself, a friend, a cellar-door host, a bottle-shop assistant or a restaurant team.

Highlights

1. Notice what is there

Look briefly if the wine’s appearance seems relevant, then smell it before taking a sip. Ask simple questions:

  • What is the first thing I notice?
  • Does it smell like fruit, herbs, flowers, spice, toast or earth?
  • What is the first flavour or sensation on my palate?
  • Does the wine feel light, weighty, fresh, drying or warm?

You do not have to identify a precise aroma. “Red fruit” is a useful observation even if you cannot decide between cherry, raspberry and strawberry.

For a fuller explanation of the tasting process, see How to Taste Wine.

2. Separate smell, flavour and texture

Aroma is what you perceive through smelling. This includes the smells you notice before drinking and the aromas that travel from your mouth to your nose while you drink.

Flavour is the broader experience while the wine is in your mouth and as you swallow. It includes taste, retronasal aroma and other sensations.

Texture, or mouthfeel, describes how the wine feels: silky, creamy, oily, lean, rough or grippy. These categories overlap in a real tasting, but separating them helps you become more specific. You can read more in What Is Wine Aroma?, Wine Flavour vs Wine Aroma and Wine Mouthfeel.

3. Describe the wine’s shape

After noticing aroma and flavour, consider the main sensations that give the wine its shape.

  • Sweetness: the level of sweetness you perceive.
  • Acidity: the quality that can make wine feel fresh, lively or mouth-watering.
  • Tannin: a drying, firm or gripping sensation, especially familiar in red wine.
  • Alcohol: warmth or heat in the mouth and throat.
  • Body: the wine’s apparent weight or fullness, from light-bodied to full-bodied.
  • Finish or length: what remains after the sip and how long identifiable flavours or sensations persist.

These are different observations. A wine can be dry but fruity, light-bodied but highly aromatic, or full-bodied without being sweet. For deeper explanations, explore Wine Structure, What Is Wine Body?, What Is Acidity in Wine?, What Are Tannins? and What Is Sweetness in Wine?.

4. Compare and qualify

Comparison makes differences easier to notice. If you have two wines, ask whether one is more or less:

  • acidic or mouth-watering;
  • tannic or drying;
  • full-bodied or light;
  • intense in aroma;
  • warm from alcohol;
  • persistent on the finish.

Use careful language when you are unsure. “It reminds me of…”, “I notice…”, “slightly drying” and “medium-bodied” are all useful. They are often more honest than pretending to be certain.

5. Summarise, then add your preference

A simple template is:

“It smells like ___. On the palate it is ___, with ___ acidity, ___ tannin and a ___ body. The finish is ___, and I ___ because ___.”

For example:

“It smells like lemon, green apple and fresh herbs. It is dry and light-bodied, with bright acidity and a clean, short finish. I prefer it with food because it feels fresh rather than heavy.”

The first part describes the wine. The last part describes your preference. Both are valuable, but they are not the same thing.

Common wine words, in plain English

Dry does not mean flavourless

In wine, dry describes perceived sweetness. It does not mean the wine lacks fruit, aroma or flavour.

A dry wine can smell strongly of berries, citrus or tropical fruit. This is why “fruity but dry” is perfectly sensible: the wine has fruit-like aromas or flavours but little noticeable sweetness. Dry wine meaning and Fruitiness vs Sweetness explain this distinction in more detail.

Fruity does not necessarily mean sweet

“Fruity” describes a smell or flavour that reminds you of fruit. “Sweet” describes a taste sensation. They often appear together in some wines, but one does not prove the other.

It is fine to say: “It has ripe peach aromas but finishes dry.”

Smooth needs an explanation

“Smooth” is common, but it can mean different things. You might mean that the tannins feel soft, the texture feels rounded, the acidity is gentle or that the wine is simply pleasant to drink.

Instead of writing “smooth”, try:

“The tannins feel soft and the texture is rounded.”

That gives the other person something more useful to understand. Smooth is not automatically a synonym for low alcohol or good quality.

Full-bodied is about weight

A full-bodied wine feels broad, weighty or mouth-filling. It is not automatically high in alcohol, sweet or better than a light-bodied wine. If the wine also feels warm, describe the alcohol separately.

Compare:

“It is full-bodied and warm, with moderate acidity.”

That is clearer than calling the wine “strong”.

A long finish is more than lingering heat

A long finish means that recognisable flavours or sensations continue after the sip. If only alcohol warmth remains, describe that specifically rather than calling the finish long.

For example:

“The citrus and savoury notes remain for several moments after the sip.”

Three more examples

A red wine

“It has aromas of red berries and dried herbs. It is dry, medium-bodied and fairly warm, with firm tannins that dry the gums. The berry flavour stays for a medium-length finish.”

A fuller style

“It smells of ripe black fruit, spice and toast. It is dry and full-bodied, with noticeable warmth, moderate acidity and a firm, textured finish. I find it powerful rather than soft.”

Turning vague reactions into useful descriptions

  • “Strong” → “The aroma is intense” or “The alcohol feels warm.”
  • “Nice” → “It is fresh, medium-bodied and easy to drink, with soft tannins.”
  • “Smooth” → “The tannins feel soft and the texture is rounded.”
  • “Fruity but dry” → “It has clear berry aromas and flavour but little noticeable sweetness.”

Use familiar comparisons without overclaiming

Citrus, berries, herbs, flowers, spice, toast and earth are useful reference points. They describe what a wine reminds you of; they do not mean those ingredients were added.

An aroma comparison is also not proof of a grape variety, region or winemaking method. A wine that reminds you of blackberries is not necessarily made from a particular grape. Tasting language records perception; it does not guarantee identification.

Describing wine in everyday Australian settings

At a cellar door, you can ask for help using preferences rather than technical names:

“I like fresh, dry whites.”

Or:

“I prefer reds with softer tannins.”

In a bottle shop, describing the style you want is usually more useful than asking for the “best” wine. At a restaurant, you can ask for something lighter or fuller-bodied, crisp or soft, dry or sweet, depending on the meal and your taste.

At home, compare two wines side by side and write one sentence about each. You may notice differences in aroma intensity, acidity, body or finish more easily than when tasting one wine on its own.

Your perception can also change with context, temperature, glassware, recent food, lighting and noise. If you cannot identify a named aroma, nothing has gone wrong. Say “fresh fruit”, “herbal”, “floral” or simply “I notice something savoury”. Personal vocabulary is welcome when it is clear and honest.

The point is communication, not performance

Wine descriptions are shared tools, not a rigid official language. “Reminds me of” is valid tasting language. So is “I’m not sure, but it feels drying”.

Most importantly, separate observation from judgement:

“The wine has firm tannins” describes a sensation.
“I prefer softer reds” describes your taste.

Neither statement is more sophisticated than the other. Together, they tell a fuller story.

Quick FAQs

Do I need to identify exact fruit aromas?

No. Broad descriptions such as “citrus”, “red fruit” or “herbal” are useful. Precision is helpful only when it genuinely reflects what you notice.

Is dry wine less fruity?

No. Dryness concerns sweetness. A dry wine can be highly fruity and strongly flavoured.

Does a long finish mean a wine is high quality?

Not necessarily. Length is an observation about persistence. Quality is a separate judgement.

Can tasting notes prove where a wine comes from?

No. They may suggest an impression, but descriptors do not prove a grape variety, region or production method.
