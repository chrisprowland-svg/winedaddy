What Is Lees Stirring? A Beginner’s Guide

Lees stirring, also called bâtonnage, is the deliberate resuspension of wine sediment. Learn how it differs from lees ageing and how it may affect texture, integration and oxygen exposure.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Lees are the sediment left behind during winemaking, made mainly from yeast cells plus other solids.
  • Lees stirring means deliberately mixing that sediment back into the wine.
  • The technique is often called bâtonnage or batonnage.
  • It is commonly associated with white wine and Chardonnay, but it is not exclusive to either.
  • Lees stirring may add texture, roundness and integration, but it does not automatically make a wine better.
  • Lees ageing means keeping wine in contact with lees; stirring is the physical action used to resuspend them.
  • The result depends on the lees, vessel, timing, frequency and oxygen exposure.

Quick answer: what is lees stirring?

Lees stirring is the deliberate resuspension of settled wine sediment during maturation. After fermentation, yeast cells and other winemaking solids sink to the bottom of a barrel, tank or another vessel. A winemaker may stir those deposits so they mix back through the wine and remain in contact with it for a period.

The sediment is called lees. It consists mainly of yeast cells, but can also include tartaric salts, bacteria, phenolic material and remaining grape solids. The finer, lighter portion is known as fine lees. Heavier, coarser deposits are called gross lees.

Lees stirring is often used during lees ageing, particularly in white-wine production. It may contribute to a fuller or creamier mouthfeel, greater palate weight, or better integration between fruit, yeast and oak characters. These are possible outcomes, not guarantees. Some wines are more successful when their lees are left undisturbed or removed.

How does lees stirring work?

Once sediment has settled, the winemaker gently resuspends it in the wine. This might involve stirring a barrel with a suitable tool, rolling the barrel, or using an appropriate mixing method in a tank.

The lees then circulate through the wine before settling again. The action may be repeated, stopped or combined with other lees-management decisions. There is no universal schedule that applies to every wine. The choice depends on the wine’s condition, the amount and type of lees, the vessel, the intended style and the winemaker’s management of oxygen.

This is why a tasting note or producer description that mentions “bâtonnage” tells you about a winemaking choice, not a guaranteed flavour profile.

Why stir lees?

To keep wine in contact with fine lees

Settled lees have little contact with the wine above them. Stirring distributes them through the vessel again, increasing contact between the wine and the yeast-derived material.

As yeast cells break down naturally, a process called autolysis, they can release compounds such as mannoproteins, polysaccharides, amino acids and lipids. These compounds may influence the wine’s texture, aroma, flavour and stability.

To influence texture

Lees contact may make a wine seem broader, rounder, creamier or fuller on the palate. Some wines develop more apparent weight or a softer, more integrated texture.

That does not mean lees stirring always creates creaminess. The result varies with the wine, the lees and the wider winemaking process. It is also possible to notice a change without preferring the stirred wine.

To integrate different characters

Lees contact may help bring fruit, oak and yeast-derived characters together. In a barrel-aged white wine, for example, a winemaker may use lees management as one part of shaping the balance between freshness, oak influence and palate texture.

Lees stirring does not create oak flavour by itself, and a wine can be aged on lees without being matured in oak.

To manage oxygen

Lees can consume dissolved oxygen, so suspended lees may sometimes help reduce oxygen in the wine. However, stirring can also introduce oxygen, especially when a vessel is opened or the movement is vigorous.

This makes oxygen management a balancing act. Excessive exposure may reduce sulphur dioxide and contribute to undesirable changes, including acetaldehyde or acetic acid formation. Lees stirring should therefore not be described as simply protective or simply oxidative.

Where is lees stirring used?

Lees stirring is often associated with white wines, especially Chardonnay and wines matured in contact with fine lees. It may also be used in stainless-steel tanks, concrete vessels and other suitable maturation vessels. The presence of one of these vessels does not prove that lees were stirred.

The technique is not limited to one grape variety, wine colour, vessel or Australian region. Australian winemakers may use different lees regimes according to their fruit, wine style, equipment and cellar decisions. The Australian Wine Research Institute discusses both the potential benefits and risks of lees contact and stirring, but does not support treating the practice as a regional signature or a universal recipe.

Lees stirring, lees ageing and bâtonnage: what is the difference?

These terms are related but not identical:

  • Lees ageing means keeping wine in contact with lees over time.
  • On lees and sur lie describe wine that has been held or matured in contact with lees.
  • Lees stirring is the physical intervention that resuspends settled lees.
  • Bâtonnage or batonnage is the common French-derived industry term for stirring or resuspending lees.

A wine can be aged on lees without being stirred. Stirring may be one part of a broader lees-management programme.

Lees stirring is also different from:

  • Racking, which transfers wine from one vessel to another and may leave selected sediment behind.
  • Fining, which uses a fining agent to remove or reduce selected compounds or particles.
  • Malolactic fermentation, a separate biological process that converts malic acid into lactic acid.

Understanding these distinctions helps when reading a label, tasting note or winery explanation. “Aged on lees” describes contact over time; “bâtonnage” indicates that the lees were stirred.

What might lees-stirred wine taste or feel like?

Depending on the winemaking choices, you may notice:

  • a broader or fuller mouthfeel;
  • creaminess or roundness;
  • greater palate weight;
  • bready or savoury impressions;
  • more integrated fruit, oak or yeast characters.

But lees stirring does not guarantee any of these. A wine’s variety, acidity, alcohol, oak, fermentation, oxygen exposure and maturation time all affect what you taste. The best approach is to treat “lees-stirred” as useful context rather than a promise of richness or quality.

What should a beginner remember?

Lees stirring is a technique, not a quality score. It tells you that a winemaker deliberately brought fine sediment back into contact with the wine. That choice may influence texture, complexity, integration and oxygen behaviour, but the outcome depends on how and why it was used.

If you see lees-aged, on lees, sur lie or bâtonnage in an Australian wine description, you now have a useful starting point: ask whether the wine was merely kept on its lees, actively stirred, or both. That distinction can explain some of its texture and style without suggesting that one approach is automatically better than another.

Suggested FAQs

Is lees stirring the same as lees ageing?

No. Lees ageing is the period during which wine remains in contact with lees. Lees stirring is the action of resuspending those lees.

Does lees stirring make wine creamy?

It can contribute to a rounder or creamier texture, but it does not always do so. The result depends on the wine and the wider cellar process.

Is bâtonnage the same as lees stirring?

Bâtonnage, also written batonnage, is the common French-derived term for stirring or resuspending lees. It is generally treated as an equivalent term for lees stirring.

Is lees stirring only used for Chardonnay?

No. Chardonnay is a familiar example, but the technique is not exclusive to Chardonnay, white wine or barrel maturation.
