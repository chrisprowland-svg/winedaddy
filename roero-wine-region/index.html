Highlights

  • Roero is a hilly wine-growing area in Piedmont, north-western Italy.
  • It sits on the left bank of the Tanaro River, north of Alba and close to Bra.
  • Roero is also the name of an Italian DOCG and European PDO wine denomination.

  • Red Roero is based on at least 95% Nebbiolo.

  • Roero is separate from Langhe, Barolo and Barbaresco, even though they are close neighbours within Piedmont.

Quick answer: what is Roero known for?

Roero is best understood as both a place and a protected wine name. Geographically, it is a hilly part of Piedmont on the left bank of the Tanaro River. In wine terms, Roero is an Italian DOCG and European PDO denomination with defined boundaries and production rules.

For an Australian wine learner, the key label distinction is simple: a bottle labelled Roero principally refers to the red wine type, which must contain at least 95% Nebbiolo. Roero Arneis is the white expression, based on at least 95% Arneis. Roero Arneis Spumante or Roero Spumante refers to a permitted sparkling style.

The place is close to Alba, Bra and the neighbouring Langhe hills, but these names are not interchangeable. Nor is Roero another name for Barolo or Barbaresco. Those are separate Piedmont DOCG denominations with their own territories and rules.

Where is Roero?

Roero is in Piedmont, in north-western Italy. The hierarchy is:

Italy → Piedmont → Roero

The regulated Roero area lies on the left bank of the Tanaro River and covers parts of 19 municipalities in Cuneo province. Canale is a useful central orientation point. Bra and Alba are also familiar nearby references, although neither town defines the whole denomination.

Alba is the nearest widely recognised regional centre for many visitors and lies immediately south-east of the Roero area. From Turin, the Piedmont capital, Canale is roughly 47–48 kilometres by road to the south-east. A drive may take around 30–45 minutes in uncongested conditions, but this is only a broad orientation guide: routes, traffic and starting points vary.

A regional map can make this relationship easier to see, provided it shows Roero as an educational location rather than implying an exact legal boundary.

What do DOCG and PDO mean?

DOCG stands for Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita. It is an Italian regulatory category used for wines made under defined rules covering matters such as geographic origin, grape varieties, production and presentation.

PDO, or Protected Designation of Origin, is the corresponding European protection used for a product whose qualities and identity are connected with a defined place and production system.

These terms are useful when reading an imported Italian label, but they are not Australian legal categories. In practical terms, a Roero DOCG label tells you that the wine is using a protected name and must meet the relevant Italian and European requirements. It does not promise that every bottle will taste identical.

For more background, see our guide to Italian DOC and DOCG.

Roero’s main grapes and wines

Roero Arneis

Arneis is the principal grape of white Roero. Under the denomination rules, Roero Arneis must contain at least 95% Arneis, with a small proportion of permitted non-aromatic white grapes allowed.

Roero Arneis is broadly described as a dry white wine that may show floral aromas, apple, peach and hazelnut. Depending on the site, vintage, ripeness and winemaking choices, it may feel lighter and fresh or broader and more textured. Those descriptors are useful starting points, not a tasting guarantee.

The standalone Arneis guide explains the grape in more detail. This page’s focus is why Arneis is so closely connected with Roero.

Red Roero

Red Roero is based on at least 95% Nebbiolo. Up to 5% of permitted non-aromatic red grapes suitable for cultivation in Piedmont may also be used, so it is not correct to assume that every bottle is legally 100% Nebbiolo.

Official descriptions present red Roero as dry, potentially full-bodied and tannic, with possible aromas and flavours including cherry, sour cherry, raspberry, blackberry, blackcurrant and spice. Wood-related notes may appear where maturation contributes them.

For a beginner, the useful expectation is a Nebbiolo-based red with dry structure and noticeable tannin, while remembering that site, vintage, vinification and maturation can all change the result. Our guide to Nebbiolo provides the broader grape context.

Roero Arneis Spumante is also permitted. Its sweetness can range from brut nature through to sweet, so “sparkling Roero” does not automatically mean one particular level of sweetness.

How do the hills and soils matter?

Roero is a hilly landscape with alternating layers of sand, clay and limestone. Its geology is associated with the Tanaro River’s changing course, a process described in the official product material as “Tanaro capture”.

The official description links sandy or sandstone-derived soils with fresh, elegant wines and subtle aromas, clay with colour and body, and limestone with fine aromas. It also describes exposure as relevant: south-facing slopes are associated with red-wine production, while cooler or north-facing exposures may suit some white-wine production.

These are tendencies, not rules. Soil does not independently dictate a wine’s flavour. Grape variety, slope, exposure, vintage, vineyard decisions and winemaking all contribute. This is a useful example of terroir: the interaction of place and human choices rather than a single soil type acting like a recipe.

Roero, Langhe, Barolo and Barbaresco

Piedmont is the larger Italian region; Roero is one wine area and denomination within it.

Langhe is a closely associated regional and wine-geographic term around Alba, but it is not a synonym for Roero. A label naming Langhe and one naming Roero are not necessarily describing the same denomination.

Barolo and Barbaresco are separate Piedmont DOCG denominations, also strongly associated with Nebbiolo. Sharing a principal grape does not make them interchangeable with Roero. Each has its own legally defined territory, rules, exposures, soils, ageing requirements and producer choices.

It is more useful to think of these as neighbouring expressions of Piedmont than as steps in a simple quality ranking. Roero is not automatically a lighter, lesser or cheaper version of either Barolo or Barbaresco.

What should you look for on a Roero label?

When choosing or discussing an imported bottle, look first at the protected place name:

  • Roero: principally the red, Nebbiolo-based type.
  • Roero Arneis: the dry white expression based mainly on Arneis.
  • Roero Arneis Spumante or Roero Spumante: a permitted sparkling expression.
  • Riserva or Vigna: additional regulated terms that apply under specified conditions; they should not be treated as universal quality rankings.

A place name gives you useful information about origin and regulated style. It does not tell you every detail of the wine’s flavour, sweetness, oak use or texture. For more help interpreting labels, read How to read a wine label.

Frequently asked questions

Is Roero part of Piedmont?

Yes. Roero is a wine-growing area and protected denomination within Piedmont, in north-western Italy.

Is Roero the same as Langhe?

No. They are closely related Piedmont wine geographies, but Langhe is not a synonym for Roero.

Is Roero made from Nebbiolo?

Red Roero must contain at least 95% Nebbiolo. White Roero, generally labelled Roero Arneis, must contain at least 95% Arneis.

Is Roero the same as Barolo?

No. Roero and Barolo are separate Piedmont DOCG denominations with different territories and production rules.

What style of wine is Roero Arneis?

Roero Arneis is generally a dry white wine that may show floral, apple, peach and hazelnut notes. Producer, site, vintage and winemaking can change its weight and texture.
