What is Counoise?

Counoise is a southern Rhône red-wine grape best known for adding freshness, fruit and spice to blends. Learn its style, uses and Australian context.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Counoise is a black-skinned red wine grape from southern France.
  • It is especially associated with the southern Rhône.
  • It is usually used as a blending grape rather than the main grape in a wine.
  • Counoise can contribute freshness, light colour, red-fruit, floral and peppery-spice character.
  • Its name may appear in discussions of Châteauneuf-du-Pape and other Rhône wines, but its presence is not guaranteed.
  • Counoise is also grown in Australia, although on a very small scale.

The quick answer

Counoise is a distinct red-wine grape variety, not a region, appellation or generic term for a Rhône blend. It comes from southern France and is best known as a minor component of southern Rhône wines.

In a blend, Counoise may add lively acidity, fruit, floral notes and spice while contributing relatively little colour or weight compared with more deeply coloured, structured Rhône varieties. The finished wine can still vary considerably according to where the grapes were grown, how ripe they were picked, how the wine was made and what other grapes were blended with them.

You may encounter Counoise in a description of a southern Rhône blend, in a list of Rhône grape varieties or occasionally as the name of a single-varietal wine.

Where does Counoise come from?

Counoise is historically associated with southern France, particularly the southern Rhône. It was known by the early seventeenth century and remains one of the traditional black grape varieties connected with the Rhône Valley.

It is permitted in several Rhône appellations, including Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Côtes du Rhône, Gigondas, Lirac, Rasteau, Vacqueyras and Vinsobres. That means the variety may be used under the rules of those appellations; it does not mean every wine from those places contains Counoise.

This distinction matters. Châteauneuf-du-Pape is a regional wine designation, while Counoise is one possible grape variety used within that regional tradition.

What does Counoise add to a blend?

Counoise is generally discussed as a supporting variety. It is not interchangeable with Grenache, Syrah or Mourvèdre, which each bring different combinations of colour, body, acidity, alcohol, tannin and aroma.

Counoise can help bring brightness and lift to a blend. Its reported contributions include:

  • lively or good acidity;
  • red-fruit character;
  • floral aromas;
  • peppery or other spice notes;
  • relatively light colour and body.

The word “can” is important. A small amount of Counoise will affect a blend differently from a larger proportion, and the result also depends on the other grapes and the winemaking choices.

A useful way to think about it is that Counoise may help a blend feel more energetic and aromatic, rather than simply making it darker or heavier.

What does Counoise wine taste like?

Wines made from Counoise often tend towards relatively pale colour, light body and fresh acidity. Descriptions may include raspberry, cherry, cranberry, flowers and peppery spice.

These are broad tendencies, not a fixed tasting checklist. A ripe, extracted red wine made mostly from Counoise will not necessarily taste like a lightly made, pale blend containing a small amount of the variety. Fermentation, skin contact, extraction, oak, serving temperature and blend composition all influence what ends up in the glass.

Counoise is not best understood as universally low-tannin, soft or low-alcohol. Its structure depends on growing conditions, ripeness and winemaking. Compared with deeply coloured and firmly structured Rhône grapes, however, it is generally associated with a lighter frame.

Is Counoise mainly used in blends?

Yes. Counoise is most commonly encountered as a blending grape, particularly in southern Rhône discussions. Some producers also make single-varietal Counoise wines, but these are not the variety’s most typical context.

On a wine list or label, Counoise might appear in a blend description or in a technical note listing the grape varieties used. It may not appear on the front label at all, especially when the wine is sold under a regional designation rather than a variety name.

If you see “Counoise” alongside Grenache, Syrah or Mourvèdre, it usually indicates that the winemaker is explaining the blend’s ingredients—not that Counoise is the dominant grape.

How is Counoise grown?

Counoise is described as late-budding or mid- to late-ripening. It can grow vigorously and is reported to perform well in hot, dry conditions. Sources differ in how they describe its yield: one describes it as low to moderate, while another calls it quite productive. Yield can vary with site and vineyard management, so it is safer not to treat either description as universal.

The grape can also be difficult to colour fully even when ripe. That helps explain why Counoise is associated with lighter-coloured wines and why it can be useful when a blend needs freshness and aroma without adding deep colour.

Is Counoise grown in Australia?

Yes, but only on a very small scale. The Australian Wine Research Institute reported in 2024 that Counoise was being grown by around five Australian wine producers, with examples from the Canberra District, Frankland River and McLaren Vale.

The same source describes Australian Counoise as generally being used alongside varieties such as Shiraz and Grenache. This suggests a modest but documented Australian interest in the grape, rather than broad national significance. The producer figure should be treated as a dated industry estimate, not a permanent count or a complete census.

How do you pronounce Counoise?

A useful English approximation is “koon-wahz”. Pronunciations can vary between languages and speakers, so this is a practical guide rather than a rule.

Is Counoise the same as Aubun?

No. Counoise has sometimes been confused with Aubun, but they are distinct grape varieties. They should not be treated as interchangeable names.

You may also encounter historical or regional forms such as Moustardier, Coneze, Connoise or Quenoise in technical references. “Counoise” is the clearest name to use for general wine discussion.

Frequently asked questions

Is Counoise a red or white grape?

Counoise is a black-skinned grape used primarily for red wine, although its relatively light colour can also make it relevant to some rosé blends.

Is Counoise in every Châteauneuf-du-Pape?

No. Counoise is a permitted variety in Châteauneuf-du-Pape, but individual wines may use different combinations of permitted grapes.

Is Counoise the same as Grenache?

No. Both can be associated with southern Rhône blends, but they are different varieties with different typical contributions to colour, body, acidity and aroma.

Can you buy a single-varietal Counoise?

Single-varietal examples exist, but Counoise is more commonly encountered as part of a blend. Availability varies by producer, region and market.

What should I expect from a wine containing Counoise?

Look for the possibility of lighter colour, fresh acidity, red fruit, floral notes and spice. Treat these as clues rather than guarantees: the full blend and winemaking matter just as much.
