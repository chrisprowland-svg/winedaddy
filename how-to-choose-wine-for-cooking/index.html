How to choose wine for cooking

Learn how to choose wine for cooking by matching sweetness, acidity, colour, body and flavour intensity to the dish, with practical Australian label guidance.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Choose a sound, reasonably pleasant wine that suits the dish—not necessarily an expensive one.
  • Dry wine is the most flexible starting point for many savoury recipes.
  • Match the wine’s colour, acidity, body and flavour intensity to the food.
  • Sweet wine can work beautifully in desserts and sweet reductions, but sweetness becomes more concentrated as it reduces.
  • “Cooking wine” on a label does not automatically mean good or bad. Check the ingredients, sweetness, saltiness and alcohol information.
  • Cooking reduces alcohol, but does not guarantee that all of it disappears.
  • Reseal and refrigerate opened wine if you plan to cook with it soon.

The quick answer

The best wine for cooking is usually a fresh, sound wine whose style fits the recipe. For a savoury dish, start with a dry wine unless the recipe is designed around sweetness. Then consider whether the dish is delicate or robust, light or dark, rich or fresh.

A dry white wine can suit seafood, chicken, creamy sauces, pan sauces and lighter vegetables. A dry red can work in tomato-based sauces, beef or lamb braises and robust stews. Sweet or fortified wine has a place in desserts, fruit sauces and reductions where sweetness is intentional.

You generally do not need an expensive bottle. A modestly priced wine can work very well if it is pleasant, fresh and appropriate for the dish. Avoid wine that smells stale, strongly oxidised or otherwise unpleasant: reducing it can concentrate flavours you would rather not have in the finished food.

Start with the dish, not the price

Wine contributes more than alcohol. It can add acidity, sweetness, colour, fruit flavour and body.

Think about the intensity of the dish. A delicate seafood sauce can be overwhelmed by a very full-bodied, heavily flavoured red. A rich braise may need a wine with enough flavour to remain noticeable after simmering.

This is a useful starting point rather than a strict rule:

  • Delicate dishes: choose a lighter-bodied wine with moderate flavour.
  • Rich or robust dishes: choose a wine with more body and flavour intensity.
  • Creamy or buttery sauces: a fresh, dry wine can help balance the richness.
  • Long reductions: remember that sweetness, saltiness, acidity and strong flavours become more concentrated.

Dry or sweet?

For most savoury cooking, dry wine is the easiest default. “Dry” means the wine has little perceptible residual sweetness; it does not mean flavourless.

Sweet wine is not automatically unsuitable. Use it when the recipe calls for it, particularly in desserts, fruit sauces, sweet-and-savoury reductions or dishes where the sweetness is part of the intended balance. Be cautious when reducing sweet wine, because the sweetness becomes more concentrated.

If a savoury recipe does not specify a sweet wine, a dry option is usually the safer choice.

White or red?

White wine

Dry white wine is a useful choice for:

  • prawns, mussels and other seafood
  • fish and lighter vegetable dishes
  • chicken
  • creamy or buttery sauces
  • deglazing a pan after cooking

Its lighter colour can be useful when you want to preserve the appearance of a pale sauce. Choose a style with enough freshness to balance richness, without allowing strong oak or intense flavour to dominate.

Red wine

Dry red wine can suit:

  • beef or lamb braises
  • robust stews
  • tomato-based sauces
  • darker pan sauces
  • dishes with strong herbs, stock or spices

Red wine can add colour, fruit flavour and savoury depth. It is not limited to red meat, however. The sauce and other ingredients matter more than the protein alone.

Other styles

A sweet or fortified wine may be appropriate for desserts, fruit sauces or a recipe that specifically names one. Use it carefully and in the quantity intended, because concentrated sweetness can quickly change the balance of a dish.

How acidity helps

Acidity is the fresh, tart quality in wine. It can add lift and help balance rich, creamy or fatty food.

That does not mean the most acidic wine is always the best choice. A delicate dish may need freshness without an aggressive flavour, while a rich sauce may benefit from more noticeable acidity. Treat acidity as one part of the decision alongside sweetness, body and flavour intensity.

What about “cooking wine”?

“Cooking wine” is a product description, not a guarantee of one consistent quality or composition. Products with that wording can differ in sweetness, saltiness, alcohol level, preservatives and flavourings.

A sound table wine is often the most flexible option because you can assess its style and, if you wish, serve it with the meal. But a product labelled for cooking is not automatically poor or unsuitable. Read the label and consider whether its ingredients and flavour suit the recipe.

For packaged products in Australia, check:

  • the alcohol information and standard-drinks information;
  • the ingredient list;
  • any declared salt, sugar or other ingredients that could affect seasoning;
  • whether the product tastes and smells suitable for the dish.

Australian food-labelling rules do not give the phrase “cooking wine” one simple meaning that tells you exactly how every product is made.

Does price matter?

Usually, not as much as suitability.

If a recipe uses a small amount of wine alongside stock, vegetables, herbs and other ingredients, an expensive bottle may not provide good value. A reasonably priced wine can be an excellent cooking ingredient when it is sound and matches the dish.

Spending more can make sense when the wine is also being served with the meal, or when the recipe uses enough wine for its character to remain central. Price alone, however, does not tell you whether a wine is right for cooking.

A quick selection checklist

Before adding wine to your trolley or recipe, ask:

  1. Is the wine sound? Avoid stale, faulty or strongly oxidised wine.
  2. Is the recipe savoury or sweet? Choose dry for most savoury dishes; use sweet wine when sweetness is intended.
  3. How delicate is the dish? Match lighter food with lighter flavour intensity.
  4. What colour and depth do you want? White generally keeps a sauce lighter; red adds colour and deeper flavour.
  5. Will the wine be reduced? Reduction concentrates flavour, sweetness, acidity, salt and alcohol aroma.
  6. Could the label affect the dish? Check ingredients, sweetness, saltiness and alcohol information.
  7. Will you drink the rest? If so, choose a wine you would be happy to serve as well as cook with.

Does cooking remove all the alcohol?

No. Cooking can reduce alcohol, but it does not guarantee that all alcohol is removed. The amount remaining depends on the cooking method, including factors such as cooking time, temperature, surface area and how much liquid remains.

Alcohol may also be more noticeable in a sauce that has been reduced. If a dish must contain no alcohol, do not assume that cooking has removed it completely; choose an appropriate substitute instead.

What should I do with opened wine?

If you plan to cook with opened wine soon, reseal it and keep it refrigerated. Before using it, smell and taste a small amount if appropriate. If it smells stale, vinegary or otherwise unpleasant, replace it rather than expecting cooking to fix it.

There is no single useful storage period for every still, sparkling or fortified wine. The type of wine, closure and storage conditions all make a difference.

Frequently asked questions

Can I cook with wine that is inexpensive?

Yes. Price is not the main test. Choose a sound, reasonably pleasant wine that suits the dish.

Should I always cook with wine I would drink?

Use that as a sensible quality check, not an absolute rule. An inexpensive but sound wine can cook well. Avoid wine that is faulty or unpleasant.

Is dry white wine the best all-purpose cooking wine?

It is a versatile starting point for many savoury dishes, particularly seafood, chicken, pan sauces and creamy sauces. It is not the right choice for every recipe: some dishes need red, sweet or fortified wine.

Can I use red wine with chicken or seafood?

You can, but it may add colour and stronger flavour. Consider the sauce and other ingredients rather than choosing solely by the type of meat or seafood.

Is cooking wine always salty?

No. Products sold as cooking wine vary. Check the ingredient list and taste the product before adding extra salt to the recipe.

Does boiling remove the alcohol?

Do not assume that it does. Cooking reduces alcohol by varying amounts, but does not guarantee complete removal.
