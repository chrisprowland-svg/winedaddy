What Is Flor Ageing? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what flor ageing is, how a living surface yeast film changes wine, which Sherry styles use flor, and how it differs from oxidation, lees and solera ageing.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Flor is a living film of specialised wine yeast that grows on the surface of wine.
  • Flor ageing is biological ageing: the yeast actively changes the wine as it matures beneath the film.
  • The yeast needs oxygen, so flor does not create a completely airtight seal. It consumes oxygen and changes how the wine interacts with it.
  • Flor ageing is especially associated with Fino and Manzanilla Sherry.
  • Amontillado begins ageing under flor and later continues with oxidative ageing. Oloroso follows an oxidative pathway without sustained flor.
  • Flor ageing is not the same as lees ageing, barrel ageing, bottle ageing, oxidation or solera ageing.
  • Australia has a documented history of flor-aged fortified wine, although the evidence does not establish flor as broadly representative of modern Australian wine production.

Quick answer: what is flor ageing?

Flor ageing is the maturation of wine beneath a living surface layer of yeast. This layer is called flor, velo de flor in Spanish, or velum in technical writing.

Flor forms where wine meets air. It is made mainly by specialised strains of Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the same species of yeast involved in alcoholic fermentation, although flor strains are adapted to survive and grow in high-alcohol, nutrient-limited wine.

Rather than simply sitting on the wine like a lid, flor is active. It uses oxygen and some of the wine’s constituents as it grows. This changes the wine’s chemistry and can contribute savoury, nutty, doughy, saline and aldehydic characters. The exact result depends on the yeast, wine, vessel, temperature, time and cellar management.

That is why “flor protects wine from oxygen” is too simple. Flor consumes oxygen and modifies the wine’s exposure to it, but it does not make the wine completely oxygen-free.

How does flor form?

Flor usually develops after alcoholic fermentation, once much of the fermentable sugar and some nutrients have been used. Under suitable conditions, specialised yeast cells gather at the wine’s surface and form a floating biofilm.

Several factors influence whether flor forms and remains active:

  • the wine’s alcohol level and composition;
  • available nutrients;
  • access to oxygen at the surface;
  • temperature and cellar conditions;
  • the vessel and the amount of headspace;
  • the particular flor yeast population;
  • how the wine is topped, transferred or otherwise managed.

There is no single universal flor recipe. In traditional Sherry production, wines intended for a flor-aged pathway are commonly fortified to around 15% alcohol by volume, while wines intended for an oxidative Oloroso pathway are commonly fortified to around 17%. These are useful Sherry examples, not universal alcohol limits for every flor-aged wine.

Flor can weaken or disappear if the conditions no longer suit it. When active flor is no longer maintained, the wine may experience more direct oxidative development if oxygen contact continues.

What does flor do to wine?

Flor yeast carries out oxidative metabolism at the wine’s surface. It consumes oxygen and metabolises ethanol and other non-fermentable compounds. This can alter compounds including acetaldehyde, glycerol and other volatile substances.

In the glass, flor-aged wines may show:

  • almond or bitter-almond aromas;
  • bruised-apple or aldehydic notes;
  • savoury, saline or lightly savoury characters;
  • dough, bakery or bread-like impressions;
  • nutty or hazelnut-like aromas.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. Flor does not produce one identical flavour profile in every wine. A Fino-style wine, for example, may seem light, dry and lifted, while another biologically aged wine may show a different balance of savoury, nutty or oxidative characters.

The best approach is to treat flor as a clue to a wine’s possible character, not a promise of a particular aroma.

Flor ageing and Sherry

Flor is most strongly associated with the fortified wines of Jerez in southern Spain. The main distinctions are:

  • Fino is traditionally aged exclusively under flor.
  • Manzanilla is biologically aged under flor in the Sanlúcar context.
  • Amontillado begins under flor and later continues through oxidative ageing.
  • Oloroso follows an oxidative pathway without sustained flor.

So, not every Sherry is flor-aged. The style and maturation pathway matter.

Flor ageing can also occur alongside fortification. Adding spirit changes the wine’s alcohol level and helps direct it towards a flor-compatible or oxidative pathway. You can learn more about fortification and Sherry separately.

Flor is not exclusive to Spain. Related surface-yeast ageing is documented in wines such as Vin Jaune from the Jura and Vernaccia di Oristano from Sardinia. The yeast populations, cellar practices and regional terminology are not necessarily identical to those used in Jerez.

Flor ageing versus solera ageing

Flor and solera are often mentioned together, especially in discussions of Sherry, but they describe different things.

Flor ageing describes what is happening biologically to the wine: it is maturing beneath a living yeast film.

Solera ageing describes how wine is transferred and blended: older wine is drawn from one level of vessels and replaced with younger wine from another level. This is a fractional blending and maturation system.

A solera may be used with flor ageing, but solera is not a synonym for flor. A wine can be solera-aged without flor, and flor ageing does not automatically require a classic solera system. Read more about solera ageing.

Flor ageing versus other wine processes

The easiest way to keep the terms apart is to ask what each one describes:

  • Flor ageing: an active surface yeast biofilm.
  • Oxidative ageing: maturation in which oxygen exposure and chemical oxidation play an important role.
  • Barrel ageing: maturation in a wooden vessel. A barrel can hold flor-aged or non-flor-aged wine.
  • Bottle ageing: changes that occur after wine is bottled.
  • Lees ageing: contact with yeast sediment and other solids settled at the bottom of a vessel.
  • Lees stirring: resuspending those settled solids.

Flor and lees both involve yeast, but they are not the same process. Flor grows at the surface; lees are sediment. Compare flor ageing with lees ageing, lees stirring, barrel ageing and bottle ageing.

Flor is also not simply another word for oxidation. Some wines move from flor ageing into oxidative ageing, as Amontillado does, but the two processes should remain distinct. See oxidation in wine for the broader chemical process.

Australia’s flor ageing history

Flor has a documented historical role in Australian fortified-wine production. Australian oral histories record trials and production of flor Sherry-style wines from the 1920s onwards, including technical work in South Australian wineries.

Australian practice did not necessarily copy the traditional Spanish solera system. Historical technical accounts describe flor wines being matured in different vessels, including barrels and cement tanks, and indicate that Australian examples were not generally fractionally blended in the same way as Spanish solera wines.

This is useful context for Australian learners: flor is not only a Spanish idea. However, the available evidence supports a historical Australian role rather than a claim that flor ageing is widespread in current Australian wine production.

How might you recognise flor on a label or wine list?

Look for references such as:

  • “flor-aged”;
  • “biological ageing”;
  • “aged under flor”;
  • “velo de flor”;
  • “Fino” or “Manzanilla” in a Sherry context;
  • tasting notes mentioning almond, saline, savoury, doughy or aldehydic characters.

A reference to “solera” tells you about the maturation and blending system, not necessarily whether flor was present. Likewise, “barrel-aged” tells you about the vessel, not the biological process.

If you are tasting a wine described as flor-aged, focus on whether it seems savoury, nutty, saline, lifted or aldehydic rather than expecting one fixed flavour. Wine language describes tendencies, and the wine in your glass may not match every textbook example.

Frequently asked questions

Is flor a type of lees?

No. Flor is a living yeast film at the wine’s surface. Lees are yeast sediment and other solids that settle at the bottom of a vessel. They can both influence wine, but they are different materials and processes.

Does flor stop oxidation?

No. Flor needs oxygen and consumes it. The film can change and moderate the wine’s oxygen environment, but it is not an airtight barrier and does not completely stop oxidation.

Is all Sherry aged under flor?

No. Fino and Manzanilla are the clearest flor-aged styles. Amontillado begins under flor and later becomes oxidative, while Oloroso follows an oxidative pathway without sustained flor.

Is flor ageing the same as solera ageing?

No. Flor is a biological surface-yeast process. A solera is a system for transferring and blending wine through different age levels. The two may be used together.

Can Australian wine be flor-aged?

Australian flor-aged fortified wine has a documented historical precedent. The available evidence does not establish broad current prevalence, so it is best understood as a specialised technique with an Australian history.
