What Is Roussanne? A Beginner’s Guide

Roussanne is a Rhône white grape that can make floral, textured and complex wines. Learn how it differs from Marsanne and where Australians may encounter it.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Roussanne is a white wine-grape variety, not a wine region or wine style.
  • It comes from France and is strongly associated with the Rhône Valley.
  • Roussanne and Marsanne are different grapes, although they are often blended.
  • Wines made from Roussanne are commonly dry, aromatic, rounded and textured.
  • Typical descriptions include honeysuckle, flowers, peach, orchard fruit, dried fruit and honeyed characters.
  • Roussanne is grown in Australia, including the Goulburn Valley in Victoria.
  • A wine that smells fruity or honeyed is not necessarily sweet.

What is Roussanne?

Roussanne is a white grape variety of Vitis vinifera, the species used for most fine wine. It originated in France and is traditionally associated with the Rhône Valley, where it is used in both varietal wines and blends.

For an Australian wine learner, the simplest way to think about it is this: Roussanne is a Rhône white grape that can make richly textured, floral and complex wines, either on its own or alongside Marsanne.

The finished wine can vary considerably. Climate, vineyard site, ripeness, winemaking and bottle age all influence how Roussanne tastes.

How do you pronounce and spell Roussanne?

The usual spelling is Roussanne. An approximate English pronunciation is roo-SAHN or roo-SANN, with the final sound lightly nasalised in the French manner. English speakers may render it slightly differently, so there is no need to feel anxious about saying it perfectly.

In Savoie, a French wine region, Roussanne is known regionally as Bergeron. This is useful to recognise, but Bergeron should not be treated as a name used universally on every Roussanne label.

Where does Roussanne come from?

Roussanne is a French grape with a strong Rhône identity. It is particularly associated with the northern Rhône, but it is also used in parts of the southern Rhône.

It is often discussed alongside Marsanne in Rhône white wines. The two varieties are distinct, however: Roussanne is not another name for Marsanne, and Marsanne is not another name for Roussanne.

The varieties can complement one another in a blend, but it is too simplistic to say that one always provides a particular quality and the other always provides another. Their expression depends on the vineyard, vintage and winemaking choices.

What does Roussanne wine taste like?

Roussanne commonly makes dry white wines with a rounded, sometimes full-bodied texture. They may feel broader and more layered than a very light, sharply refreshing white wine.

Common aroma and flavour descriptions include:

  • honeysuckle and other floral notes;
  • peach and orchard fruit;
  • dried fruit;
  • honeyed characters; and
  • nutty or savoury notes, particularly as some wines develop.

These are tendencies, not a checklist. One Roussanne may taste fresh and fruit-driven, while another may be richer, more floral, savoury or honeyed.

Roussanne can also have useful structure and ageing potential in suitable examples. That does not mean every bottle is designed for long-term cellaring. Freshness, concentration, alcohol, winemaking and storage all affect how a wine develops.

Is Roussanne a sweet wine?

Not necessarily. Roussanne is a grape variety, and it can be used to make different wine styles. Many Roussanne wines are dry, meaning they contain little residual sugar, even when they smell of ripe fruit, dried fruit or honey.

This is an important distinction:

  • Fruitiness describes fruit-like aromas and flavours.
  • Sweetness describes the palate impression associated mainly with residual sugar.

So a dry Roussanne can smell like peach or honey without tasting sweet. Some sweet expressions also exist, including traditional straw-wine styles, but sweetness is not an automatic feature of the variety.

How is Roussanne used with Marsanne?

Roussanne may be made as a varietal wine or blended with other white grapes. Its most famous blending relationship is with Marsanne.

Marsanne and Roussanne are both traditional Rhône varieties, but they remain separate grapes. A label or description mentioning a Rhône-style white blend may therefore refer to a wine containing one or both varieties.

In Australia, Roussanne may appear as a varietal wine or as part of a Rhône-inspired blend. The variety may not always be prominent on the front label, so checking the back label or technical information can help if you want to know the blend composition.

Where is Roussanne grown in Australia?

Roussanne is recognised in Australia as an alternative grape variety rather than one of the country’s most widely encountered white grapes.

Wine Australia’s 2025 vintage data records Roussanne production in the Goulburn Valley, Victoria, including 44 tonnes crushed in that region. This confirms current Australian production, but it does not make Goulburn Valley a national leader or suggest that Roussanne is a major Australian variety.

For Australian drinkers, the practical expectation is that Roussanne may be available less often than familiar varieties such as Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Gris or Riesling. When you see it, it may be a varietal wine or part of a blend.

How should you serve Roussanne?

A dry Roussanne generally suits the same broad occasions as other textured white wines. It can work with food that has enough flavour and richness to meet its rounded body, rather than being limited to very delicate dishes.

Think of it as a useful starting point for dishes such as roast poultry, richer seafood or lightly spiced food, then adjust for the particular wine. The wine’s acidity, sweetness, oak influence and weight matter more than the grape name alone.

If you are tasting Roussanne, notice three things:

  1. Aroma: Is it floral, fruity, honeyed or savoury?
  2. Texture: Does it feel light and crisp, or rounded and broad?
  3. Sweetness: Does it actually taste sweet, or does it simply smell ripe?

Frequently asked questions

Is Roussanne the same as Marsanne?

No. They are different white grape varieties that are often blended, particularly in Rhône-style wines.

Is Roussanne always full-bodied?

No. Roussanne is often associated with rounded, textured wines, but the final style depends on site, ripeness and winemaking.

Is Roussanne always low in acidity?

No. Its freshness can vary. It is better to describe individual wines than to treat low acidity as a universal feature.

Can Roussanne age?

Some concentrated, well-made examples can develop in bottle, often gaining dried-fruit, honeyed or nutty characters. Ageing potential varies considerably between wines.

What is the main thing to remember about Roussanne?

Roussanne is a French Rhône white grape that can produce dry, aromatic and textured wines. It is distinct from Marsanne, but the two are frequently used together.
