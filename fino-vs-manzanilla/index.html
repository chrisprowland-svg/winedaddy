Fino vs Manzanilla: What’s the Difference?

Fino and Manzanilla are not grape varieties. They are closely related, dry Sherry styles made by ageing a dry, fortified wine under a layer of flor yeast.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Fino and Manzanilla are not grape varieties. They are closely related, dry Sherry styles made by ageing a dry, fortified wine under a layer of flor yeast.

The key difference is geographic and regulatory: Fino belongs to the protected denomination Jerez-Xérès-Sherry, while Manzanilla belongs to the separate protected denomination Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda and must be aged in Sanlúcar de Barrameda.

Both wines are usually pale, very dry, fresh and savoury. Manzanilla can seem lighter, more floral or saline, while Fino can seem more almondy, bready or structured. These are tendencies, not rules: the producer, solera, filtration, bottling and storage all influence the bottle in front of you.

What do Fino and Manzanilla have in common?

Both begin as a fully fermented, dry wine, usually made primarily from Palomino grapes. The wine is then fortified to around 15% alcohol, a level that supports the growth of flor.

Flor is a living film of yeast that forms on the surface of the wine in cask. It helps protect the wine from direct oxygen exposure while contributing aromas and flavours associated with fresh bread, almonds, herbs and savoury notes.

Both styles can also mature through the Sherry solera system, in which wines from different stages of maturation are blended progressively over time. For more detail, see our guides to flor ageing, solera ageing and fortification.

The result is a wine that is pale in colour, dry in taste and often much more savoury than a beginner might expect from a white wine.

The important difference: where they are aged

The wider production area is known as the Marco de Jerez. A useful orientation is the Sherry Triangle, formed by Jerez de la Frontera, Sanlúcar de Barrameda and El Puerto de Santa María. The broader authorised area also includes surrounding municipalities.

That geography matters because the two names do not mean exactly the same thing.

Fino is a style within the protected Jerez-Xérès-Sherry denomination. It is associated with the wider Jerez-Sherry production and ageing framework.

Manzanilla has its own protected denomination: Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda. Its ageing stage must take place in Sanlúcar de Barrameda, although the base wine may come from the wider authorised Marco de Jerez area.

So Manzanilla is not simply “coastal Fino”. It is a separately protected name linked to a particular place and production requirement. On an imported bottle, “Manzanilla” or “Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda” is a regulated geographic and production signal, not just a description of a salty flavour.

How do they taste different?

Sanlúcar’s coastal conditions can influence the flor and the resulting wine. Manzanilla is therefore often described as:

  • floral;
  • light or delicate in structure;
  • saline or briny;
  • fresh and savoury;
  • gently bitter on the finish.

Fino is often described as:

  • crisp;
  • almondy;
  • bready;
  • fresh;
  • savoury and sometimes slightly more structured.

These descriptions are useful starting points, not strict definitions. A powerful Manzanilla can be more intense than a delicate Fino, and a Fino can also show saline or coastal character.

The word “salty” deserves care. Both wines can smell and taste savoury, briny or saline. Manzanilla is often associated with those impressions, but it is not automatically saltier than every Fino.

Is Manzanilla drier than Fino?

No. Neither style is inherently drier than the other.

Both are generally classified as dry generoso wines. A wine can feel especially dry because of its acidity, alcohol, bitterness, freshness and lack of obvious fruit, but perceived dryness is not determined by the name alone. For a simple explanation of the term, see what “dry” means in wine.

The same applies to the question of which is lighter or fresher. Manzanilla is commonly described as lighter and more floral, while Fino is commonly described as crisp and structured. But individual bottles can blur those boundaries.

Which should you try?

If you enjoy savoury, coastal or floral flavours, Manzanilla may be an appealing place to start. If you prefer almond, fresh-bread and crisp savoury notes, Fino may be more familiar.

The best way to understand the difference is to taste both side by side. Use similar glasses, serve them at the same temperature and compare:

  1. the aroma;
  2. the texture;
  3. the savoury or saline impression;
  4. the length of the finish.

Do not treat one as universally superior. The comparison is about preference and expression, not a ranking.

How to buy, serve and store them in Australia

In an Australian bottle shop, restaurant or online retailer, look for Fino, Manzanilla or Manzanilla-Sanlúcar de Barrameda as the style or protected-name signal. These names should not be treated as grape names or interchangeable descriptions.

Imported Spanish bottles may also differ according to their solera, filtration, bottling style and time in storage. Less-filtered presentations can retain more cask character, while filtration can make a wine clearer and more stable.

Serve Fino and Manzanilla well chilled, at approximately 5–7°C. A reasonably sized white-wine glass gives the aromas more room than a very small glass. Pour modest servings and return the bottle to the refrigerator so it stays cold.

Store an unopened bottle upright in a cool, dark and stable place. Once opened, close it firmly and refrigerate it. A useful quality guideline is to drink it within about a week. That is a freshness recommendation, not a food-safety expiry date.

Australian-made fortified wine should not be presented as eligible to use protected Spanish names.

What food goes with Fino or Manzanilla?

Their dry, fresh and savoury character makes both styles useful with foods such as:

  • oysters, prawns and other seafood;
  • sushi and sashimi;
  • olives and almonds;
  • mushrooms;
  • cured ham;
  • salty snacks;
  • tapas-style dishes.

Manzanilla’s saline character can be particularly appealing with seafood, while Fino’s almondy and bready notes can work well with savoury, umami-rich foods. These are starting points rather than rules. Explore the pairing that suits your palate.

Frequently asked questions

Are Fino and Manzanilla the same wine?

They share a production foundation and are both dry, flor-aged Sherry styles, but they have different protected geographic identities. Manzanilla must be aged in Sanlúcar de Barrameda.

Are Fino and Manzanilla sweet?

They are generally very dry. Dry does not mean flavourless: both can be aromatic, textured and intensely savoury.

Are Fino and Manzanilla made from the same grape?

Most examples are made primarily from Palomino-based dry wine. Fino and Manzanilla are style and origin names, not grape varieties.

Does Manzanilla always taste saltier?

No. It is often described as saline or briny, but both styles can show savoury and saline character. Bottle variation matters.

Can Australian wine be called Manzanilla?

The article’s evidence supports treating Manzanilla as a protected Spanish name linked to regulated origin and production. Australian-made fortified wine should not be presented as using that protected name.
