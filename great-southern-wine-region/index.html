Great Southern Wine Region: What to Expect from Its Wines

Great Southern is a diverse Western Australian wine region known for Shiraz, Riesling, Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc. Learn how its coastal and inland sites shape its wines.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Great Southern is a large wine geographical indication in southern Western Australia.
  • It sits within the South West Australia zone.
  • The region includes coastal and inland growing areas, so its wines can vary considerably.
  • Shiraz, Riesling, Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc are important regional varieties.
  • Great Southern is better understood as a collection of different sites and styles than as one uniform wine region.
  • A Great Southern label tells you about geographic origin, not a guaranteed grape, flavour or quality level.

Quick answer: what is Great Southern known for?

Great Southern is known for its diversity. It is a registered Australian wine geographical indication in Western Australia, within the South West Australia zone. The region stretches across coastal and inland areas, creating differences in maritime influence, rainfall, temperature, elevation and vineyard conditions.

Its wines are commonly associated with Shiraz, Riesling, Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc. Great Southern Shiraz is often described through savoury spice, pepper and dark-fruit flavours. Riesling is commonly crisp and citrus-driven, while Cabernet Sauvignon can produce deeply coloured, structured wines. Chardonnay is made in several styles across the region.

Those descriptions are useful starting points, not promises. A wine from Albany may not resemble one from Frankland River, and the variety, vintage, producer and winemaking choices also matter.

In simple terms, expect Great Southern to offer a broad set of Western Australian wine styles rather than one fixed regional taste.

Where is the Great Southern wine region?

Great Southern is a large wine region in southern Western Australia. In Australia’s geographical-indication hierarchy, it sits here:

Australia → Western Australia → South West Australia zone → Great Southern region

The region is commonly discussed through five subregions:

  • Albany
  • Denmark
  • Mount Barker
  • Porongurup
  • Frankland River

These names describe smaller areas within Great Southern. They are not alternative names for the Great Southern GI.

The region is too extensive to understand as one compact coastal district or single valley. It includes locations with different relationships to the Southern Ocean, inland weather patterns, elevation and terrain. That scale is one reason the wines can show such varied personalities.

Why does Great Southern have so many different wine styles?

The main reason is site variation. Climate is not identical across the GI, and neither are the conditions in individual vineyards.

Albany and Denmark have stronger maritime influences because of their proximity to the coast. Ocean influence can affect temperature, humidity, wind and rainfall. Mount Barker, Porongurup and Frankland River are more inland, with greater continental influence and greater differences between daytime and nighttime temperatures.

Moving inland and north generally increases continental influence and temperature variability. Elevation, aspect, latitude and distance from the coast add further differences. A vineyard’s slope and orientation can affect how much sunlight it receives, while local soils influence drainage, water availability and vine growth.

This does not mean that every coastal wine is light or every inland wine is powerful. It means that geography creates possibilities. The final wine also depends on grape variety, vintage, vineyard decisions and winemaking.

This is a practical example of how climate affects wine: climate contributes to ripening conditions, but it does not work alone.

The five Great Southern subregions

The five commonly recognised subregional names help make the GI’s scale easier to understand.

Albany and Denmark are the more coastal areas in regional descriptions, with stronger maritime influence. Their proximity to the ocean contributes to a different climate setting from the inland parts of Great Southern.

Mount Barker, Porongurup and Frankland River are generally discussed as more inland areas. They have greater continental influence, and their elevation, rainfall and local site conditions vary between and within each area.

These names are useful clues when they appear on a label, but they do not tell the whole story. A subregion is still a broad geographic description. The vineyard site, grape variety, vintage and winemaking method remain important.

The five areas should not be treated as five identical wine styles. Nor should a list of towns or subregions be used as a substitute for the official legal GI boundary.

Which grapes is Great Southern known for?

Wine Australia identifies Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon, Sauvignon Blanc and Chardonnay among the region’s main varieties. Riesling is also one of Great Southern’s strongest regional associations.

Shiraz

Great Southern Shiraz is often associated with savoury spice, pepper, liquorice, black cherry and plum. Some examples show a balance between fruit and oak, while others may be more restrained, fuller-bodied or strongly structured.

The useful expectation is not that every bottle will taste peppery. Instead, Great Southern can produce Shiraz with a savoury, spicy and dark-fruited character, depending on its site and winemaking.

You can learn more about the variety in what is Shiraz?.

Riesling

Great Southern Riesling is commonly associated with crisp acidity, citrus character and refreshing intensity. Inland areas such as Frankland River, Mount Barker and Porongurup are particularly important reference points when discussing the region’s Riesling.

Some examples can develop positively with age, but that does not mean every bottle should be cellared. Ageing depends on the individual wine’s acidity, structure, concentration, vintage, winemaking and storage.

For a general explanation of the variety, see what is Riesling?.

Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon is another important Great Southern variety. Regional descriptions associate it with deeply coloured, structured wines, and some examples may develop over time.

That is a tendency rather than a guarantee. Not every Great Southern Cabernet Sauvignon needs years in the cellar, and not every bottle will improve with age. The wine’s balance and structure matter more than the region name alone.

Read more in what is Cabernet Sauvignon?.

Chardonnay

Chardonnay is made in several styles across Great Southern. Coastal and inland sites can provide different fruit and acid profiles, while choices such as oak, lees contact and malolactic fermentation can further change the wine.

A Great Southern Chardonnay may therefore be fresh and lightly handled, richer and oak-influenced, or somewhere between those descriptions. The label and producer information are useful clues.

See what is Chardonnay? for the variety basics.

Sauvignon Blanc

Sauvignon Blanc is also an important regional variety. The evidence supports treating it as a significant Great Southern grape, but not assigning one universal regional flavour profile to every bottle.

As with Chardonnay, look beyond the region name. The variety, vintage and winemaking choices will help explain whether the wine is likely to be crisp, aromatic, textured or oak-influenced.

What soils and elevation add

Great Southern soils vary across the region. Wine Australia describes brown to grey-brown sandy loams, lateritic gravelly sandy loams and soils associated with granite and gneissic bedrock. Clay content, fertility and drainage can change from one site to another.

Soil is part of the wider idea of terroir, but it should not be treated as a shortcut to flavour. Saying that a wine comes from granite or gravel does not, by itself, tell you exactly what it will taste like.

Elevation is also part of the story, but it needs careful interpretation. Great Southern’s geographic elevation range extends from near sea level to more than 1,000 metres. That describes the highest and lowest points in the GI, not the elevation of every vineyard.

Elevation can influence temperature, exposure and ripening. Aspect, latitude, rainfall and distance from the coast matter too. The best way to think about Great Southern is as a combination of influences rather than a single explanation.

How to read a Great Southern wine label

“Great Southern” on a label communicates geographic origin within Australia’s GI system. It tells you that the wine is associated with this legally recognised area.

It does not guarantee:

  • Shiraz, Riesling or another particular grape
  • a specific Great Southern subregion
  • a particular elevation or soil
  • a sweetness level
  • a winemaking method
  • a vintage
  • a quality ranking
  • one universal flavour profile

To understand what the wine may be like, read the region name alongside the variety, producer, vintage and any winemaking information. A Great Southern Riesling and a Great Southern Shiraz will naturally offer different experiences. Two wines made from the same variety may also differ because they come from different sites or were made in different ways.

Frequently asked questions

Is Great Southern a wine region or a grape variety?

Great Southern is a wine geographical indication, not a grape variety. It is a region in Western Australia within the South West Australia zone.

Is Great Southern the same as South West Australia?

No. South West Australia is the broader zone, while Great Southern is a region within it.

What is the most famous Great Southern grape?

Great Southern is strongly associated with Riesling and Shiraz, but it is not a single-variety region. Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and Sauvignon Blanc are also important.

Are all Great Southern wines cool-climate wines?

No. Great Southern includes coastal and inland areas with different maritime and continental influences. It is too diverse for one simple climate label to describe every site.

Can Great Southern wines age?

Some Great Southern Riesling, Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay and Shiraz wines may develop with age. Ageing potential depends on the individual wine, vintage, winemaking and storage, not the GI name alone.

What should I expect from a Great Southern wine?

Expect variety. The region can offer fresh, citrus-driven whites, savoury and spicy Shiraz, and structured Cabernet Sauvignon, among other styles. Check the grape, producer and vintage for a more useful prediction than the region name by itself.
