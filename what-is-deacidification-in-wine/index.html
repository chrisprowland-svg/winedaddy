What is deacidification in wine?

Deacidification is the controlled reduction of excessive acidity in wine. Learn how biological conversion, blending and technical treatments can change a wine’s acid balance, and why pH is not the same as titratable acidity.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Deacidification is the controlled reduction of excessive acidity in wine.
  • Its goal is better balance—not the removal of all acidity.
  • pH and titratable acidity are related, but they measure different things.
  • Malolactic fermentation can reduce certain acids, but it is not the same as deacidification.
  • Winemakers may use biological, blending, physical, ion-exchange or authorised chemical approaches.
  • Deacidification is the opposite directional adjustment to acidification.

Quick answer

Deacidification in wine is a winemaking intervention that reduces excessive acidity to help a wine taste more balanced. Technically, it generally involves lowering titratable acidity and increasing pH, although the exact change depends on the wine and the method used.

Acidity is valuable in wine: it provides freshness, shape and energy. But if it is too pronounced for the wine’s style, the wine may taste sharply sour or feel unbalanced. That does not mean every high-acid wine needs treatment. High acidity can be deliberate and can help a wine remain fresh as it develops.

pH and titratable acidity are not the same

Two measurements commonly matter when winemakers assess acidity:

  • pH describes the strength of the wine’s acidic environment.
  • Titratable acidity estimates the amount of acid that can be neutralised under a particular test.

They are connected, but they are not synonyms. Two wines can have a similar titratable acidity but different pH, or a similar pH but different titratable acidity. The types of acids present and the wine’s buffering compounds help explain why.

That is why deacidification is a technical decision rather than simply an attempt to make a wine taste less sour.

How can winemakers reduce acidity?

The approach depends on the wine’s acid composition, its stage of production and the desired style.

Biological reduction

During malolactic fermentation, lactic acid bacteria convert malic acid into lactic acid. This can make acidity seem softer and may contribute to biological deacidification.

However, malolactic fermentation is a broader winemaking decision, not merely another name for deacidification. It can affect aroma, texture and microbial stability, and it is not always undertaken specifically to reduce acidity.

Blending

A winemaker may blend a more acidic wine with a less-acidic wine or another suitable component. The final wine can then have a different acid balance without directly neutralising the acids.

Blending is not just a way to hide a problem. It is one of the tools used to shape a wine’s composition and style.

Physical, ion-exchange and chemical approaches

Other technical options can include physical or membrane-based treatments, ion exchange, or authorised chemical treatments. These may remove, separate, exchange or neutralise some acids.

Chemical deacidification can affect both titratable acidity and pH. It must be controlled carefully: reducing acidity too far can leave a wine lacking freshness or feeling flat. These are winery decisions, not treatments to attempt at home.

What does deacidification change?

Depending on the method and the starting wine, deacidification may change:

  • the wine’s sharpness and perceived sourness;
  • freshness and overall balance;
  • pH and titratable acidity;
  • texture and mouthfeel; and
  • microbial stability.

The result is not automatically “smoother” or “better”. The aim is to make the wine more suitable for its intended style and composition.

Deacidification versus acidification

These are separate, opposite-direction adjustments:

  • Deacidification reduces acidity.
  • Acidification increases acidity.

Neither is automatically desirable. A winemaker may choose either—or neither—depending on the grapes, the wine’s chemistry and the style being made.

In Australia, permitted wine-production treatments and substances are governed by the Australia New Zealand Food Standards Code and other applicable requirements. International guidance from the OIV is useful technical context, but it is not automatically Australian legal advice. Production and compliance decisions belong with appropriately qualified winemaking personnel.

Frequently asked questions

Is deacidification the same as sweetening wine?

No. Sweetness can make acidity seem less sharp, but sweetening does not necessarily reduce the wine’s measured acidity.

Is cold stabilisation deacidification?

Not exactly. Cold treatment can encourage tartrate precipitation and may overlap with acidity reduction in some circumstances, but cold stabilisation has a distinct primary purpose and is not a synonym for deacidification.

Does malolactic fermentation always deacidify wine?

It can reduce malic acid and soften the impression of acidity, but its effects are broader and vary between wines. It is not always performed specifically for deacidification.

Is deacidification used for every high-acid wine?

No. High acidity may be intentional, stylistically appropriate or useful for freshness and development.
