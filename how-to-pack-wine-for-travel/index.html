How to pack wine for travel

Learn how to protect wine from breakage, leaks and avoidable heat when travelling by car, plane or permitted courier, with practical Australian baggage and border-rule checks.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Wine travels best when you treat it as fragile glass containing liquid: protect each bottle from impact, stop it moving, contain any leak and avoid prolonged heat. A layer of clothing in a suitcase may provide some cushioning, but it is not a reliable packing system on its own.

The right approach also depends on how you are travelling. A bottle carried in a car faces movement and Australian summer heat. Checked baggage faces rough handling and pressure from other luggage. International travel adds airline, customs, import and biosecurity requirements.

Highlights

You will need:

  • a rigid wine shipper or sturdy box with dividers;
  • cushioning for the sides, top and bottom;
  • a leak-resistant bag or liner for secondary containment;
  • packing tape;
  • space between the bottles and any hard objects.

Then pack the wine in this order:

  1. Inspect each bottle.
    Look for cracks, seepage, a loose closure or a wet capsule. A bottle that is already damaged needs to be isolated, and may not be sensible to transport.

  2. Separate the bottles.
    Each bottle should sit in its own compartment or be individually wrapped. Glass should not knock against glass.

  3. Keep bottles away from the box walls.
    Cushion the sides, base and top. The bottles should not touch the outer packaging.

  4. Stop movement.
    Fill empty spaces with suitable cushioning so the bottles cannot shift when the box is gently moved. The package should close without bulging.

  5. Add a leak barrier.
    Put each bottle, or the whole inner group, inside a sealed or spill-resistant bag or liner. This will not prevent breakage, but it can limit the damage from a leak.

  6. Keep hard objects separate.
    Shoes, toiletries, chargers and tools should not sit directly against the glass.

  7. Seal the outer package.
    Reinforce the seams with packing tape. An internal note with your name and contact details is a sensible precaution if an outside label is damaged.

  8. Check the weight and rules.
    Before travelling by air, check the operating airline’s current rules for alcohol strength, baggage type, weight, dimensions and quantity.

A rigid box with separate compartments is generally a better starting point than an unstructured suitcase compartment. Packaging guidance from shipping and postal operators commonly recommends individual protection, cushioning, filling empty spaces and leak-resistant inner packaging. These are practical precautions, not guarantees.

If you are travelling by car

Car travel usually means dealing with movement, vibration, direct sun and heat building up in a parked vehicle. These risks matter particularly during Australian summer and on long drives.

Keep the wine shaded and immobilised. Avoid leaving it in a parked car for prolonged periods, especially in direct sunlight. Plan stops so the bottles are not left unattended in extreme conditions.

Insulation or a cooler bag may slow temperature change, but it does not guarantee that the wine will remain in ideal conditions. The Australian Wine Research Institute identifies temperature, light, humidity and bottle orientation as factors that can affect wine during transport and storage. There is no single orientation that is right for every bottle, closure, wine or journey, so follow the instructions of the packaging system you are using rather than treating “upright” or “on its side” as a universal rule.

If wine is going in checked baggage

Checked baggage is exposed to handling shocks and the weight of other luggage. Use a rigid inner packing system with dividers or fitted inserts, cushioning and leak containment. Do not rely on wrapping bottles in clothes alone.

Do not assume that wine can be carried in cabin baggage, or that every airline uses the same limits for checked baggage. Airport screening, the operating airline, the route and whether the journey is domestic or international can all matter.

For example, Qantas publishes rules that distinguish alcohol by strength and baggage type. That is Qantas-specific guidance, not a universal rule for all airlines. Check the current rules of the airline actually operating your flight shortly before departure.

Domestic and international travel are different

For domestic Australian travel, verify:

  • whether the wine is going in cabin or checked baggage;
  • the operating airline’s alcohol and baggage conditions;
  • weight and size limits;
  • any airport or screening instructions;
  • how the package should be presented if inspected.

For international travel, also check the departure airport, transit countries and destination. Customs, import limits, duties, taxes and permits may apply. Rules for taking wine into another country are separate from Australia’s rules for bringing it back.

When returning to Australia, Australian Border Force currently lists a 2.25-litre alcohol concession for each passenger aged 18 or over. This is operational information that can change, so check the current Australian Government guidance before travelling. Australia also has strict biosecurity requirements. Wine does not automatically make every item in your luggage unrestricted, and other goods or packaging may need to be declared.

Courier or shipping is not the same as luggage

Sending wine by courier or post is a separate arrangement from carrying it with you. The carrier may have its own acceptance rules, packaging requirements, insurance exclusions and conditions for alcohol. The destination may also impose import duties, taxes, permits or restrictions.

Before paying for shipment, confirm that the carrier accepts wine from private senders and that the destination permits the shipment. Do not assume that a general fragile-item service authorises alcohol transport.

Check the wine when you arrive

Before opening the bottles, look for:

  • broken or cracked glass;
  • wet or stained packaging;
  • wine odour outside the bottle;
  • a pushed, loose or leaking cork;
  • seepage around a screwcap or capsule;
  • a damaged capsule;
  • unusual pressure or bulging packaging;
  • a bottle that has shifted out of its insert.

If glass is broken, avoid handling it casually. Isolate the package, protect your hands and follow the carrier’s damage process.

If an intact bottle has been exposed to suspected heat or freezing, its appearance alone may not reveal the extent of any quality change. Open it separately and assess it rather than assuming that every heat-exposed bottle is ruined—or that every bottle is unaffected.

Departure and arrival checklist

Before leaving

  • Each bottle is intact.
  • Bottles are separated and immobilised.
  • Sides, top and base are cushioned.
  • A leak-resistant inner barrier is in place.
  • Hard objects are kept away from the glass.
  • The package is sealed and within the relevant weight limit.
  • Airline, airport, customs, destination and biosecurity rules have been checked.
  • Wine will not be left in a hot, sunny parked car.

On arrival

  • Check for breakage, leaks and wet packaging.
  • Inspect closures and capsules.
  • Isolate damaged bottles.
  • Record or photograph damage before disposing of packaging.
  • Open any bottle showing possible transport damage separately.

Frequently asked questions

Is wrapping wine in clothes enough?

Usually, it is better than leaving a bottle unprotected, but clothing alone does not reliably separate bottles, prevent movement or contain a leak. A rigid container with dividers and cushioning is a safer approach.

Should wine travel upright or on its side?

There is no universal answer for every bottle and journey. Use the orientation recommended by the packing system and prioritise immobilisation, cushioning and temperature protection.

Can I take wine on a plane?

Do not assume that you can take it in cabin baggage or that the same conditions apply to every airline. Check the current rules of the operating airline, airport and destination. Checked-baggage rules can also vary according to alcohol strength, quantity and weight.

Will heat-damaged wine be unsafe to drink?

Heat can affect wine quality, but suspected heat exposure does not automatically prove that a bottle is unsafe. Inspect the bottle, open it separately and assess its condition. Seek appropriate advice if the bottle is leaking or the glass is damaged.

Can I post wine overseas?

Only if the carrier and destination permit it. Courier and postal services may impose separate alcohol, packaging, customs and import conditions.
