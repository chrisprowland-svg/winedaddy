Vintage vs non-vintage Champagne: what’s the difference?

The simplest way to tell Vintage and non-vintage Champagne apart is to look for a year on the label.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Vintage Champagne is made from grapes harvested in one declared year.
  • Non-vintage Champagne blends wines from multiple harvest years, often including reserve wines.
  • A vintage year tells you the harvest year, not the bottle’s age.
  • Vintage does not automatically mean better, sweeter or more mature.
  • Non-vintage does not mean inferior: blending helps create continuity and house style.
  • Brut, Extra-Brut and Demi-Sec describe sweetness, not vintage status.
  • Vintage Champagne has a longer minimum maturation requirement under Champagne rules: 36 months, compared with 15 months for non-vintage.

Quick answer

The simplest way to tell Vintage and non-vintage Champagne apart is to look for a year on the label.

Vintage Champagne carries a declared harvest year and is made from grapes harvested in that year, subject to Champagne’s appellation rules. Non-vintage Champagne does not declare one harvest year. Instead, it is made by blending wines from different years, commonly including reserve wines from earlier harvests.

This is not a straightforward quality ladder. Vintage Champagne is intended to express the character of a selected harvest, while non-vintage Champagne is generally blended to create continuity and a recognisable house style from release to release.

The year on a Vintage Champagne label also does not tell you how old the bottle is. It identifies the harvest. Bottle age, disgorgement date, storage and maturation after release are separate questions.

What does the year on a Champagne label mean?

On Vintage Champagne, the year is the year in which the grapes were harvested. In French, this style is known as millésimé.

It is not the year the bottle was purchased, released or opened. For example, a bottle marked 2015 tells you about the 2015 harvest. It does not, by itself, tell you when the wine was disgorged or how it was stored afterwards.

A producer may choose to declare a vintage when the character of a harvest suits the intended wine. This decision is not necessarily made every year, and producers do not all have to declare the same harvests.

Why blend non-vintage Champagne?

Blending wines from several harvests gives the producer more tools to shape the final wine. Reserve wines from earlier years can be combined with newer wine to help maintain a familiar house style despite variation between growing seasons.

That makes non-vintage Champagne a deliberate style, not a fallback. The exact number of harvests and the proportion of reserve wine can vary between producers and cuvées.

A useful way to think about it is this: Vintage Champagne places the harvest in the foreground; non-vintage Champagne places the intended blend and house style in the foreground.

Does vintage Champagne taste better?

Not automatically. Vintage status describes how the wine was made and labelled; it is not a universal quality ranking.

Vintage Champagne may show more distinctive year-related character, while non-vintage Champagne may offer greater stylistic continuity. Those are tendencies rather than guarantees. Grape composition, lees maturation, dosage, producer decisions, bottle age and storage can all influence the wine.

Non-vintage Champagne can be complex and carefully made. Vintage Champagne can also vary in style and maturity. The label category alone cannot predict exactly what will be in the glass.

Vintage status is separate from sweetness

Look for dosage terms to understand sweetness:

  • Brut Nature, Pas Dosé or Dosage Zéro: driest categories, with little or no added sugar depending on the designation
  • Extra-Brut: very dry
  • Brut: dry, and a common Champagne category
  • Extra-Dry, Sec, Demi-Sec and Doux: progressively sweeter categories

Both Vintage and non-vintage Champagne can be made in different dosage categories. Vintage does not mean sweet, and non-vintage does not necessarily mean dry.

Minimum maturation is not the same as bottle age

Champagne rules require a minimum maturation period of:

  • 36 months for Vintage Champagne
  • 15 months for non-vintage Champagne

These are minimum maturation periods under Champagne rules, not simply the time since harvest or the time since you bought the bottle. Producers may mature wines for longer.

Lees maturation—the period when the wine remains in contact with yeast sediment in bottle—can contribute to texture and developed aromas. But a longer legal minimum does not guarantee that every Vintage Champagne will taste more mature or suit every drinker.

A disgorgement date, when provided, gives different information: it indicates when the lees were removed and the bottle received its final closure. It is not the harvest year.

Reading the label in Australia

Champagne is the protected regional name for qualifying sparkling wine from France’s Champagne appellation. An Australian sparkling wine may be made using the traditional method, but it is not Champagne.

When comparing bottles in an Australian bottle shop, ask:

  1. Is a harvest year shown? If so, it is Vintage Champagne.
  2. What is the dosage term? Brut and Extra-Brut describe sweetness, not vintage status.
  3. Is a disgorgement date provided? This is separate from the harvest year.
  4. Is the wine Champagne or Australian sparkling wine? Similar production methods do not make the names interchangeable.
  5. Am I mistaking “reserve”, “cuvée” or “prestige” for vintage? These terms do not, by themselves, mean the wine is Vintage Champagne.

Australian wine labels may also use a vintage year to communicate a harvest year, but Australian and Champagne labelling rules should not be assumed to be identical.

Common misconceptions

“Vintage means the bottle is old”

No. Vintage identifies the harvest year. The bottle’s age also depends on when it was released, disgorged and stored.

“Non-vintage means there is no vintage wine in the blend”

No. Non-vintage Champagne commonly blends wines from several harvests. “Non-vintage” means that no single harvest year is declared on the label.

“Vintage is always better”

No. Vintage and non-vintage Champagne have different production purposes. Choose your understanding of the style—not an automatic quality assumption.

“Cuvée or reserve means vintage”

No. A cuvée is a particular blend or bottling. Reserve wine is wine retained from an earlier harvest for later blending. Neither term is the same as a declared vintage.

FAQs

Is Vintage Champagne always more expensive?

Not necessarily, and price was not the reason for the distinction explained here. Vintage status reflects the declared harvest and production choices; price also depends on factors such as producer, release and market conditions.

Can non-vintage Champagne age?

Non-vintage status does not mean a wine cannot develop. However, vintage status alone cannot guarantee longevity, and the way any bottle develops depends on its wine, closure, storage and age.

Is Brut the same as non-vintage?

No. Brut is a dosage category describing sweetness. A Champagne can be Vintage Brut or non-vintage Brut.

Is Australian vintage sparkling wine the same as Vintage Champagne?

No. An Australian traditional-method sparkling wine may use a comparable production technique, but Champagne is the protected regional name for qualifying wine from France’s Champagne appellation.
