What is Mavrodaphne?

Mavrodaphne is a Greek red grape best known for sweet, often fortified wines from Patras and Cephalonia. Learn how the name works on a wine label.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Mavrodaphne is a Greek red grape variety.
  • The name is also used for wines made from the grape.
  • It is best known for sweet, often fortified red wines.
  • The classic associations are Patras, in the northern Peloponnese, and Cephalonia in the Ionian Islands.
  • Dry red wines made from Mavrodaphne also exist.
  • Traditional examples can show dark fruit, dried fruit, spice and mature, nutty flavours.
  • “Mavrodaphne of Patras” and “Mavrodaphne of Cephalonia” are separate protected regional wine names.

Quick answer

Mavrodaphne is a Greek black-skinned wine grape variety. It is also the name you may see on wines made from the grape.

The name is most strongly associated with sweet, often fortified red wine, particularly Mavrodaphne of Patras. However, it is not safe to assume that every Mavrodaphne wine is sweet or fortified: dry red expressions are made as well.

That means “Mavrodaphne” can describe three closely connected things:

  1. The grape variety
  2. A wine made from that grape
  3. A protected regional wine name, such as Mavrodaphne of Patras or Mavrodaphne of Cephalonia

For an Australian drinker, the easiest way to understand it is as a Greek grape with a famous sweet-wine tradition and a broader range of modern expressions.

Where does Mavrodaphne come from?

Mavrodaphne is associated with Greece, especially Achaia and the northern Peloponnese. Patras is the major regional centre linked with its best-known traditional style.

The grape is also associated with Cephalonia, an Ionian island west of mainland Greece. Cephalonia’s wine identity is distinct from Patras, even though both places use the Mavrodaphne name in protected regional wine designations.

These geographical names matter on a label:

  • Mavrodaphne may refer to the grape or a wine made from it.
  • Mavrodaphne of Patras is a specific protected regional designation.
  • Mavrodaphne of Cephalonia is a separate protected regional designation.

A town, island or broader regional reference is not automatically the same thing as a protected wine name.

Is Mavrodaphne sweet or fortified?

Traditionally, Mavrodaphne is best known as a sweet red wine, often made in a fortified style. Fortification means adding grape spirit or another permitted distillate during or after fermentation, increasing the wine’s alcohol level.

Traditional sweet examples are often deeply coloured and aromatic. They may develop dark-fruit and dried-fruit flavours, along with spice and mature, nutty or oxidative characters as they age.

Mavrodaphne of Patras is particularly associated with this style. Its protected production rules may allow Mavrodaphne to be used alone or blended with the Corinthian black grape, so the appellation should not automatically be treated as a 100 per cent varietal wine.

The important point is that the grape and the style are not identical. Dry red wines made from Mavrodaphne also exist, including examples associated with the Peloponnese and Cephalonia. If you want to know whether a particular bottle is sweet, dry or fortified, read the full label and producer description rather than relying on the grape name alone.

What does Mavrodaphne taste like?

There is no single flavour profile for every Mavrodaphne wine. Sweetness, fortification, blending, oak ageing and bottle age can all change the result.

Traditional sweet examples may suggest:

  • dark or ripe fruit;
  • dried fruit;
  • spice;
  • nutty or mature flavours;
  • deep colour;
  • noticeable but sometimes silky tannin.

With age, some wines can become more complex and develop savoury, oxidative or nut-like notes.

Dry examples are more likely to behave like structured red wines. They may show ripe fruit, aromatic lift, tannin and oak influence. They are not simply dry versions of a sweet fortified wine; the winemaking choices and ageing can create a different experience.

How should you serve Mavrodaphne?

Traditional sweet Mavrodaphne is best approached slightly chilled rather than served warm. A useful starting point is around 12–14°C, in a small dessert-wine or compact red-wine glass.

It can work with:

  • dark chocolate desserts;
  • nut-based desserts;
  • blue or spicy cheeses;
  • or a small glass on its own.

For a dry Mavrodaphne, use the individual wine as your guide. Its structure may suit the same kinds of foods you would serve with other dry red wines, but the bottle’s description will tell you more than the grape name alone.

After opening, recork the bottle and keep it cool and dark. How long it remains enjoyable will depend on its sweetness, alcohol, age and production style, so avoid treating one storage time as a rule for every bottle.

How might Australians encounter it?

Mavrodaphne is not an Australian grape variety or an Australian regulated wine style. In Australia, you are most likely to see the name in a Greek restaurant, a specialist retailer, an imported-wine section or a wine list featuring less familiar international wines.

Look for clues such as:

  • Mavrodaphne of Patras or Mavrodaphne of Cephalonia;
  • a description such as “sweet”, “fortified” or “dessert wine”;
  • an indication that the wine is dry;
  • serving advice on the bottle or wine list.

The name “Mavrodaphne” tells you about the grape or regional identity, but it does not always tell you the wine’s sweetness. That is the useful label-reading lesson.

Frequently asked questions

How do you pronounce Mavrodaphne?

A useful English approximation is “May-row-daff-nee”. You may also see transliterations such as Mavrodafni or Mavrodaphni.

Is Mavrodaphne the same as Port?

No. Mavrodaphne is a Greek grape and wine name. Its traditional sweet, fortified expression may invite comparisons with other fortified wines, but it is not Port and should not be treated as interchangeable with it.

Is Mavrodaphne always a dessert wine?

No. It is strongly associated with sweet fortified wine, but dry red wines made from Mavrodaphne also exist.

What does “Mavrodaphne of Patras” mean?

It is the protected regional name for a traditional Mavrodaphne wine associated with the Patras area. It is a specific designation, not simply another name for every wine made from the grape.
