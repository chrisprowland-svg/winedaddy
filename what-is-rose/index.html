What is rosé?

Rosé is a wine style, not a single grape or sweetness level. Learn how it gets its colour and how to choose, serve and enjoy it.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Rosé is a wine style with a colour ranging from pale pink and salmon to deeper pink and copper.
  • It is usually made from red grapes, but with much less skin contact than red wine.
  • Rosé is not defined by one grape, region, sweetness level or production method.
  • It can be dry, off-dry or sweet, and pale colour does not automatically mean dry wine.
  • Direct pressing, short skin contact and saignée are three ways to make rosé.
  • Rosé is generally not made by mixing finished red and white wine.
  • Australian rosé is made from many grape varieties and regions.
  • Serve it chilled, then reseal and refrigerate any leftovers.

Quick answer

Rosé is a wine style known for its pink-to-copper colour and fresh, fruit-driven character. It is most commonly made from red grapes, but the juice spends only limited time with the grape skins. That brief contact gives the wine some colour without the extended extraction associated with most red wine.

Rosé is therefore not a single grape variety or a synonym for a sweet wine. A bottle can be dry, off-dry or sweet; pale or deeply coloured; light-bodied or more structured; still or sparkling.

The colour usually comes from controlled contact between juice and red grape skins. Winemakers may press the grapes quickly, leave the juice with the skins for a short time, or use a technique called saignée, in which some pink juice is drained from a red-wine ferment and fermented separately.

The simplest way to understand rosé is this: it is usually made from red grapes, but handled so that the wine gets less colour and tannin from the skins than a typical red wine.

How does rosé get its colour?

Grape juice is not always deeply coloured simply because the grapes have dark skins. Much of the pigment that gives red wine its colour is extracted from the skins during winemaking.

For rosé, the winemaker controls how much colour and structure are extracted. Less skin contact generally means less colour and less tannin, although grape variety, ripeness, temperature, pressing and other decisions also affect the result.

That is why rosé can range from almost onion-skin or peach shades through salmon and bright pink to copper and deeper magenta. Colour can offer clues about how the wine was made, but it does not tell you everything about how it will taste.

A pale rosé is not automatically sweeter, drier or better than a darker one. Colour, sweetness, acidity and body are related to winemaking, but they are not the same thing.

Three common ways to make rosé

Direct pressing

With direct pressing, red grapes are pressed soon after arriving at the winery. The juice is separated from the skins early, so it takes on only a small amount of colour.

This approach can produce a particularly delicate-looking rosé, but there is no universal colour or flavour outcome. The grape variety and the winemaker’s choices still matter.

Short maceration

Maceration means leaving juice or crushed grapes in contact with the skins so colour, flavour and phenolic compounds can be extracted.

For rosé, that contact is limited. The winemaker separates the juice when the desired colour and structure have been reached. A little more contact may produce a deeper-coloured wine with more texture, but it does not create a fixed style every time.

Saignée

Saignée—French for “bleeding”—involves draining some pink-coloured juice from a tank of crushed red grapes. The removed juice can then be fermented separately as rosé.

The technique is associated with a red-wine ferment, so saignée is not simply another name for all rosé production. Much rosé is made deliberately through direct pressing or short skin contact instead.

Is rosé made by mixing red and white wine?

Not as a general method. Most rosé is made by controlling colour extraction from grapes, usually red grapes, rather than by pouring finished red wine into finished white wine.

Rules around blending differ between countries, wine categories and protected regional designations. Some sparkling-wine categories and regional specifications have their own provisions. It is therefore more accurate to say that red-and-white blending is not the standard explanation for rosé—not that it is universally illegal or that it never occurs in any category.

The important beginner distinction is between blending and saignée. Saignée removes juice from a red-wine ferment before that juice is fermented separately. It is not the same as mixing finished red and white wines.

Rosé compared with red and white wine

Rosé and red wine are both commonly made from red grapes. The main difference is how long the juice remains with the skins. Red wine generally receives longer skin contact, extracting more colour and tannin. Rosé normally receives less, producing a paler wine with less tannic grip.

White wine is commonly made by pressing grapes and fermenting the juice with little or no skin contact. Rosé may also be fermented as separated juice, but it usually has some colour and flavour influence from red grape skins.

Rosé often shows fresh red-fruit notes such as strawberry, raspberry or cherry. Depending on the grape and winemaking, it may also suggest citrus, melon or flowers.

Is rosé sweet?

It can be—but it does not have to be.

Rosé is made in dry, off-dry and sweet styles. Dry means the wine has little perceived sweetness, although acidity and fruit flavours influence how dry it feels. Sweetness is mainly affected by the amount of residual sugar left in the finished wine and by the balance between sugar and acidity.

A pale rosé can be completely dry. A darker rosé can also be dry. If sweetness matters to you, check the label, producer notes or ask the person selling the wine rather than judging by colour.

Australian rosé

Australian rosé is not one regional or grape variety style. It is made across many regions from varieties including Shiraz, Grenache, Merlot, Nebbiolo and Pinot Noir, among others.

That variety gives you a useful starting point when reading a label. A grape name can suggest the kind of fruit or structure you might encounter, but it is not a guarantee.

Australian labels may include a grape variety, vintage or Geographical Indication, commonly called a GI, which identifies a wine-growing region. These details are optional rather than requirements for every bottle. Where a single variety, vintage or GI claim is made, Australian labelling rules apply, including the relevant 85 per cent threshold.

How to serve and store rosé

Chill still rosé before serving. In warm conditions, it may need time in the refrigerator or an ice bucket to reach a refreshing temperature. Avoid serving it so cold that its aroma and flavour disappear.

After opening, reseal the bottle and refrigerate it. Still rosé is usually at its freshest over the next few days. Sparkling rosé loses its bubbles more quickly, so use a proper sparkling-wine stopper and drink it sooner.

You do not need special glassware. A regular white-wine glass is a useful choice.

What food goes with rosé?

Rosé is a flexible partner at the table. Try it with salads and grilled vegetables, seafood and shellfish, grilled chicken, charcuterie, picnic foods, fresh or soft cheeses, tomato-based dishes and mildly spiced food.

A dry, lively rosé may suit seafood and salads particularly well. A fuller or more deeply coloured example can stand up to grilled foods. Sweeter rosé may work with spicy dishes or fruit-based foods. These are starting points, not rules.

In summary

Rosé is a broad wine style, not a single grape, region or sweetness level. Its colour usually comes from limited contact between red grape juice and skins. Direct pressing, short maceration and saignée are different ways to achieve that style.

Rosé can be dry or sweet, pale or deep, still or sparkling. When choosing one, use the label and producer description rather than assuming colour tells you everything.
