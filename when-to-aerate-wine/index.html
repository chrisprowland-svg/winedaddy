When to Aerate Wine

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Aerating wine means giving it controlled exposure to air after opening.
  • Many wines are ready to drink as soon as they are poured.
  • Taste first: a young, concentrated or closed wine may benefit from air.
  • A small pour and gentle swirl are the safest starting points.
  • Young Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon may respond well, but neither automatically needs aeration.
  • Old, delicate or already expressive wines can fade with too much exposure.
  • Aeration can change wine, but it cannot repair oxidation, cork taint or other faults.
  • There is no universal “aerate for 30 minutes” rule.

Quick answer: when should you aerate wine?

Aerate wine when a sound wine tastes tight, muted, harsh or mildly reductive and seems likely to benefit from controlled exposure to air. Start by pouring a small amount into a glass, tasting it, swirling gently and tasting again.

If the wine becomes more expressive, its harshness softens or mild struck-match aromas recede, continue serving it from the glass. If it remains closed and is young, concentrated and robust, you could transfer the remaining wine to a clean decanter for more air contact.

Be more cautious with old, delicate, aromatic or already expressive wines. They may show beautifully with gentle handling but lose fruit, freshness or complexity if exposed to too much air. Many everyday wines need no aeration at all.

What does aerating wine mean?

Aerating wine means exposing it to air after opening. You can do this by pouring wine into a glass, swirling it, transferring it to a decanter or using an aerator.

The amount and speed of air contact varies. Pouring a small serve into a glass gives the wine some exposure; swirling increases it. A decanter usually exposes more wine to air at once, while an aerator is designed to increase air contact during pouring.

“Letting a wine breathe” is a common phrase, but it does not mean that every bottle needs to sit open for a set period. Wine changes at different rates depending on its age, structure, condition, serving temperature and how it is exposed to air.

Aeration is not the same as decanting

Aeration and decanting can overlap, but they have different purposes.

  • Aeration is about exposing wine to air to influence its aroma, texture or perceived openness.
  • Decanting means transferring wine into another vessel. It may aerate the wine, but it is also used to leave sediment behind.
  • Sediment is solid material that can collect in some bottles over time. It is not necessarily a fault.

If your main concern is sediment, read when to decant wine. If your question is whether a wine needs air, taste it before reaching for a decanter.

Which wines might benefit from aeration?

The clearest candidates are young, structured or concentrated wines that taste closed, austere or harsh. “Structured” usually means the wine has noticeable elements such as tannin, acidity, alcohol and body that give it shape.

A young Australian Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon can be a useful example. If it tastes very tannic, concentrated or muted, a little air may make its fruit and aroma easier to recognise. That does not mean every Australian Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon should be aerated. Variety, region and country are not automatic instructions.

A wine with mild reductive aromas may also be worth swirling. Reduction is associated with a lack of oxygen exposure and can sometimes smell like struck match or sulphur. Gentle air contact may allow some of these aromas to dissipate. If the smell persists or the wine tastes faulty, do not keep aerating indefinitely. See what is reduction in wine? for the broader explanation.

When is pouring into a glass preferable?

A glass is often the best first tool because it lets you monitor the wine one serve at a time. This is particularly useful when:

  • you are unsure how the bottle will respond;
  • the wine is old or delicate;
  • the wine is already aromatic and expressive;
  • you are serving wine at home and do not want to expose the whole bottle at once;
  • you suspect the wine may fade quickly.

For an older bottle, a decanter may still be useful for carefully separating sediment. But sediment removal does not require prolonged aeration. Pour carefully, taste as you go and avoid assuming that an old wine will improve simply because it is older.

A cautious way to aerate wine

Use this glass-first method before making a decision about the whole bottle:

  1. Check the serving temperature. A wine that is too warm may seem soft or alcoholic, while one served too cold may seem tighter. Check wine serving temperature explained before blaming every problem on a lack of air.
  2. Open and smell the wine. Notice whether it seems expressive, closed, reductive or faulty.
  3. Pour a small amount. A suitable glass gives you room to swirl. Glass shape can affect how aromas gather; see wine glass types explained.
  4. Taste before aerating further. This gives you a useful starting point.
  5. Swirl gently and taste again. Look for more expressive aroma, clearer fruit, softer harshness or better-integrated texture.
  6. Increase exposure only if the wine improves. Continue from the glass, or consider a clean decanter for a young, robust wine that remains closed.
  7. Stop when the wine declines. Muted fruit, reduced freshness, diffuse aromas or a loss of energy are signs that more air is not helping.

This method uses your senses rather than a stopwatch. WSET describes aerative decanting as a traditional but disputed practice and notes that swirling in the glass may be enough in many cases. There is no standard timing that works reliably for every wine.

When can aeration make wine worse?

Air is not automatically beneficial. Too much exposure can make a wine’s aromas and flavours seem diffuse, reduce freshness or mute its fruit. Old and delicate wines are especially worth monitoring because they may lose complexity quickly once poured.

A wine that is already open and expressive may not need more air. Nor does a bottle bought for an ordinary weeknight dinner need a decanter simply because it is red. Taste it first.

Sparkling wine is not a routine aeration case. Pour it carefully into an appropriate glass and avoid treating it like a still red wine. Specialist views can differ for particular bottles, but broad claims that all sparkling wine must or must not be decanted go beyond the guidance for this beginner decision.

What aeration cannot fix

Aeration may help a sound wine show its character more clearly, but it cannot turn a damaged wine back into a fresh one. It cannot repair cork taint, heat damage, oxidation or another fault. It is also not a guaranteed remedy for severe reduction or every unpleasant smell.

Oxidation is a chemical and sensory change involving oxygen. Controlled air contact can influence a wine, but it cannot reverse freshness and fruit that have already been lost. Learn more in what is oxidation in wine?.

The most useful rule is simple: taste, expose gradually and stop when the wine stops improving. If the wine changes in the glass, that is useful information—not a test you have to pass. For more on that gradual evolution, see why wine changes in the glass when available.

Frequently asked questions

Should every red wine be aerated?

No. Many red wines are ready to drink when poured. Aeration is optional and should respond to what the wine tastes like, not simply its colour or grape variety.

How long should you aerate wine?

There is no universal time. The result depends on the wine and the method. Taste before and during aeration instead of relying on a fixed number of minutes.

Is opening a bottle enough to aerate it?

Opening the bottle exposes only a small surface area of wine to air. Pouring and swirling generally provide more contact, but the most practical approach is still to taste first.

Should old wine be aerated?

Treat old wine cautiously. Pouring may help separate sediment, but extended exposure can cause a delicate wine to fade. Taste it in the glass and increase air contact only when useful.

Is a decanter better than an aerator?

They are different ways of increasing air contact, and their effects vary. This article focuses on when to aerate rather than equipment comparisons. See decanter vs aerator when that guide is available.
