What is Saperavi?

Saperavi is a Georgian red wine grape known for deeply coloured, structured wines. Learn what it tastes like, how it is made and why Australians may encounter it.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Saperavi is a red wine grape variety from Georgia, especially associated with Kakheti.
  • It is a teinturier grape: its flesh and juice are coloured as well as its skin.
  • That helps Saperavi produce deeply coloured, sometimes opaque wines.
  • Typical wines can be high in acidity, tannic, dark-fruited and substantial.
  • Saperavi is not always dry, full-bodied or made as a red wine.
  • It can be made in Georgian qvevri or using European-style cellar methods.
  • Saperavi has also been grown commercially in Australia for decades.

Quick answer

Saperavi is an old Georgian red wine-grape variety. It is particularly associated with Kakheti, one of Georgia’s principal wine-producing regions.

What makes it unusual is that Saperavi has pigmented flesh as well as dark skin. Most red grapes have clear flesh, so their colour mainly comes from contact with the skins during winemaking. Saperavi can contribute colour from both parts of the grape. The result is often a deeply coloured, inky-looking wine.

In the glass, Saperavi commonly brings firm acidity, noticeable tannin, dark-fruit flavours and substantial structure. But the grape name is only a starting point. Growing conditions, ripeness, winemaking, sweetness and maturation all influence the final wine.

Saperavi is commonly pronounced something like “sah-per-AR-vee”.

Why is Saperavi so deeply coloured?

Saperavi is a teinturier grape. The term describes a red grape whose flesh and juice contain pigment, rather than only its skin.

That characteristic can help explain why Saperavi wines are often so dark. During red-wine production, colour is extracted from grape solids into the fermenting juice. With Saperavi, both the skins and the flesh can contribute pigment.

This does not mean every Saperavi wine will look identical. Rosé and sparkling versions are also made, and winemaking choices affect the colour that reaches the bottle. Still, deep colour is one of the variety’s clearest signatures.

The name Saperavi is often translated as relating to colouring or dyeing—a reference that fits the grape’s unusual pigmentation.

What does Saperavi wine taste like?

A typical dry Saperavi may feel like a substantial red wine, with:

  • dark berry fruit, such as blackberry or dark cherry;
  • firm or refreshing acidity;
  • noticeable tannin;
  • floral, liquorice, spicy or savoury notes; and
  • a full or weighty impression in some examples.

Those are tendencies, not a tasting checklist. Cooler growing conditions may produce fresher red-fruit characters and greater elegance, while warmer conditions can encourage darker fruit, meatier flavours and more alcohol. Extraction, vessel, maturation and residual sugar can change the texture and balance too.

The combination of acidity and tannin is important. Saperavi can be deeply coloured and powerful without being simply soft or heavy. Acidity can give the wine energy, while tannin contributes grip and structure.

Suitable dry examples may have good ageing potential, particularly when they have enough acidity, tannin and concentration. That does not mean every bottle of Saperavi is designed for long-term cellaring.

Where does Saperavi come from?

Saperavi is native to Georgia and is closely connected with the country’s wine tradition. It is especially widespread in Kakheti, in eastern Georgia, where both traditional and modern styles are produced.

Kakheti is a region, not another name for the grape. The same distinction matters when reading a Georgian label. Names such as Mukuzani, Napareuli, Kindzmarauli and Akhasheni communicate regional or protected wine-designation information; they are not synonyms for Saperavi.

Saperavi is now grown in other wine-producing countries as well, including Australia. The grape’s Georgian identity remains central, but a wine made from Saperavi can reflect the place and methods used wherever it is grown.

Is Saperavi always a dry red?

No. Dry red wine is an important expression of Saperavi, but it is not the only one.

The variety is also used for:

  • semi-sweet and sweet wines;
  • fortified wines;
  • rosé; and
  • sparkling wine.

That range is useful to remember when you encounter Saperavi on a wine list or bottle. The grape name tells you the variety, but it does not by itself tell you how sweet the wine will be, how it was made or how it will taste.

Some Saperavi is made in qvevri, traditional Georgian earthenware vessels used for fermentation and maturation. Other examples are made using European-style cellar methods. Qvevri is a production method, not a synonym for Saperavi and not a requirement for making the wine.

Why might Australians encounter Saperavi?

Saperavi is a less familiar international variety in Australia, but it is not new to the country. The Australian Wine Research Institute records commercial Saperavi plantings and production in Australia going back several decades, with historical plantings particularly associated with regions including the King Valley, Alpine Valleys and McLaren Vale.

Australian winegrowers have explored Saperavi as an alternative variety partly because it can provide strong colour and retain acidity while producing structured wines. Australian examples should be understood as local interpretations, rather than assumed copies of Georgian styles.

An Australian reader may encounter the variety on an imported Georgian wine, in specialist wine contexts or in an Australian wine made from an emerging international grape. The available evidence supports its established Australian production history, but not current national planting totals or market figures.

What should you remember from the label?

If a bottle says Saperavi, that is primarily telling you about the grape variety.

If it also names a place such as Kakheti or a Georgian protected wine designation, that adds information about origin and classification. Those terms should not be treated as interchangeable. A town, region and grape variety each communicate different things.

The most useful expectation is therefore broad: Saperavi often makes dark, structured wines with firm acidity and tannin, but the final style depends on more than the grape alone.

Saperavi in one sentence

Saperavi is a Georgian teinturier red grape that can make deeply coloured, high-acid, tannic and dark-fruited wines in a range of styles—including in Australia—without every bottle sharing exactly the same profile.

Suggested FAQs

Is Saperavi a red or white grape?

Saperavi is a black-skinned red wine grape. Its flesh is also pigmented, making it a teinturier variety.

Is Saperavi sweet?

It can be dry, semi-sweet, sweet or fortified. The grape name alone does not determine sweetness.

Is Saperavi always made in qvevri?

No. Some Saperavi is made in qvevri, while other wines use European-style production methods.

Is Saperavi the same as Otskhanuri Sapere?

No. Despite related names and strong colour associations, the Georgian National Wine Agency identifies Otskhanuri Sapere as genetically distinct from Saperavi.
