What Is Durif? A Beginner’s Guide

Durif is a dark-skinned red Vitis vinifera grape variety. It originated in France and is documented as the offspring of Syrah and Peloursin.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Durif is a red wine grape variety, not a region or a wine style.
  • It originated in France and is documented as a cross between Syrah and Peloursin.
  • It commonly makes deeply coloured, full-bodied wines with firm tannin.
  • Typical flavours include concentrated dark fruit, with possible pepper, spice, herbal, chocolate or savoury notes.
  • Australia is an important context for Durif, especially inland wine-growing areas and Rutherglen.
  • Petite Sirah is commonly used as another name for Durif, but that name has a complicated history and is not universally straightforward.

Quick answer: what is Durif?

Durif is a dark-skinned red Vitis vinifera grape variety. It originated in France and is documented as the offspring of Syrah and Peloursin.

In the glass, Durif commonly produces deeply coloured, full-bodied red wine with firm or substantial tannin and concentrated dark-fruit flavour. Depending on where it is grown and how the wine is made, you may also find pepper, spice, herbs, chocolate or savoury notes.

That is a description of common tendencies, not a guarantee. Climate, site, ripeness, crop level, harvest timing and winemaking can all change the result. A Durif from a warm inland site may taste riper and more generous, while a cooler or earlier-picked example may feel fresher and more linear.

For Australian wine learners, Durif is worth recognising because it has an established local production base and appears as a named grape on Australian wine labels.

Durif is not Shiraz

Durif and Shiraz are separate grape varieties.

Syrah—known as Shiraz in Australia—is one of Durif’s parents. Peloursin is the other. That parentage may help explain why both varieties can be associated with dark fruit, spice and structure, but Durif is not a form, clone or synonym of Shiraz.

It is therefore more accurate to think of Durif as a distinct grape with one familiar parent than as “a stronger Shiraz”. The two wines can overlap in flavour and weight, but the grape identity remains different.

What does Durif taste like?

Durif is often recognised by its colour and structure.

Colour and body

Durif commonly produces very deeply coloured red wine. It is also usually full-bodied, meaning it can feel weighty and substantial across the palate rather than light or delicate.

Tannin

Tannin can create a firm, drying or slightly grippy sensation, particularly around the gums. Durif often has plenty of it. That firmness can make the wine feel powerful even when it is not especially high in alcohol.

Fruit and aroma

Common fruit descriptions include blackberry, blueberry, plum and other dark-fruit characters. Depending on the fruit’s ripeness and the winemaking, Durif may also show pepper, spice, herbal, chocolate or savoury notes.

Dark or ripe fruit aromas do not necessarily mean a wine is sweet. A wine can smell richly of ripe berries while still tasting dry, because dryness refers to the amount of residual sugar left in the wine—not how fruity it smells.

Why does Durif vary from bottle to bottle?

The grape gives a wine its starting point, but it does not write the entire tasting note.

  • Climate: Warmer conditions can encourage riper fruit, more body and potentially higher alcohol. Cooler conditions or earlier picking can preserve more freshness.
  • Site: Water availability, elevation, exposure and other local conditions influence how grapes develop.
  • Harvest timing: Picking earlier or later changes the balance between freshness, ripeness and alcohol.
  • Crop level: The amount of fruit carried by the vines can affect concentration and ripeness, although there is no single rule that explains every vineyard.
  • Winemaking: Extraction, oak, blending and maturation can alter tannin, texture, aroma and fruit intensity.

This is why “Durif” on a label tells you something useful, but not everything. It suggests a grape and a family of likely characteristics rather than one fixed flavour recipe.

Durif and Australia

Australia is an important place for understanding Durif. Wine Australia’s 2025 variety snapshot recorded 13,867 tonnes of Durif crushed nationally. The leading reporting areas were the Riverina, Riverland, Murray–Darling–Swan Hill VIC and Rutherglen.

Those figures provide orientation rather than a complete list of every place where Durif is grown. They also do not mean every Australian Durif tastes the same. The regions differ, and individual vineyard and winemaking decisions matter.

Rutherglen is a particularly established Australian context for the variety, while the Riverina, Riverland and Murray–Darling–Swan Hill VIC account for substantial reported production. If you see Durif on an Australian label or wine list, it is usually being presented as the grape variety used to make the wine, alongside any geographic information the label provides.

What does “Petite Sirah” mean?

Petite Sirah is commonly used as another name for Durif. Wine Australia lists Petite Sirah as a synonym of Durif, and UC Davis also records Petite Sirah and Petite Syrah among the variety’s common names.

However, the history is complicated. “Petite Sirah” has been applied to more than one grape or selection in different places and periods. As a result, it is safer to say that Durif is commonly known as Petite Sirah than to claim that every wine labelled Petite Sirah is automatically identical in genetic history.

For an Australian learner, the practical approach is simple: treat Durif as the primary grape name, and understand Petite Sirah as a related alternative name that requires some historical and regional qualification.

How to approach a Durif

If you are choosing Durif for dinner or tasting it at a winery, expect a red wine that may be dark in colour, full in body and firm in tannin, with concentrated fruit. Serving temperature, food and the wine’s individual style will affect how it feels, so do not assume every bottle will be equally heavy or alcoholic.

The most useful question is not whether Durif always tastes one particular way. It is whether the bottle’s combination of fruit, structure and freshness suits what you enjoy.

Frequently asked questions

Is Durif a red wine?

Durif is a red wine grape variety. Wine made from it is generally red, deeply coloured and structured.

Is Durif the same as Shiraz?

No. Syrah, called Shiraz in Australia, is one parent of Durif, but Durif is a separate grape variety.

Is Durif sweet?

Not necessarily. Durif can have ripe, dark-fruit flavours while still being a dry wine. Fruitiness and sweetness are not the same thing.

Is Petite Sirah the same as Durif?

Petite Sirah is commonly used as a name for Durif, including in Australian references. However, the historical name has been applied to more than one grape or selection, so universal equivalence should not be assumed.

Where is Durif grown in Australia?

Wine Australia’s 2025 production snapshot identifies the Riverina, Riverland, Murray–Darling–Swan Hill VIC and Rutherglen among the leading reported areas for Durif. These are not a complete directory of all growing locations.
