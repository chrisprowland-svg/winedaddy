What is Timorasso?

Timorasso is an Italian white grape from Piedmont known for dry, textured wines with fresh acidity and the ability to develop savoury complexity with age. Learn what the grape is, what its label terms mean and where Australian drinkers may encounter it.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Timorasso is a white wine-grape variety from Piedmont in north-west Italy. It is especially associated with the hills around Tortona, in the province of Alessandria, where it is used mainly to make dry, still white wines.

A bottle made from Timorasso may be fresh and citrusy when young, or fuller, more savoury and complex after bottle ageing. The variety is best understood through its combination of acidity, texture and capacity to develop—not through one fixed flavour.

Highlights

  • Timorasso is a grape variety, not a town or a winemaking method.
  • It comes mainly from the Tortonese area of Piedmont.
  • Its core style is dry white wine with fresh acidity, body and texture.
  • Young examples can show citrus, white flowers and orchard fruit.
  • With age, some wines develop flint, honey, balsamic, toasted or petrol-like notes.
  • “Colli Tortonesi” is a regional DOC; “Derthona” is a related wine and regional identity.
  • In Australia, imported bottles are most likely to appear through specialist retailers, importers and restaurants.

Where does Timorasso come from?

Piedmont—Piemonte in Italian—is a region in north-west Italy. Timorasso is particularly linked with the Tortona hills in south-eastern Piedmont, within the province of Alessandria.

Tortona is the useful town to remember. The wider Colli Tortonesi DOC covers a defined territory around it, including localities such as Monleale, Sarezzano, Costa Vescovato and Volpedo. These places help explain the geography, but no single town represents the whole growing area.

From Milan, Tortona is roughly 74 kilometres away by road, or about 55 minutes in favourable conditions. Treat that as broad orientation rather than a precise travel promise: traffic, route and starting point will change the journey.

What do the names on the label mean?

This is where Timorasso can become confusing.

Timorasso is the grape variety. The technical name recorded in the Italian Vitis Database is Timorasso B., while Timorasso is the usual public name.

Colli Tortonesi is a Piedmont DOC—a regulated geographical designation. A label reading Colli Tortonesi Timorasso DOC connects the wine with both the grape and that recognised regional designation.

Derthona is a name associated with Tortona, derived from the town’s ancient name, and is used as a wine and regional identity connected with Timorasso. It should not automatically be treated as the grape’s synonym, or as interchangeable with Piedmont or Colli Tortonesi.

You may also see Piedmont or Piemonte on a label. That identifies the larger region, not necessarily the Colli Tortonesi DOC. Gavi, another well-known Piedmont wine designation, is separate and is principally associated with the Cortese grape.

What does Timorasso wine taste like?

Many Timorasso wines are dry, fresh and relatively substantial for a white wine. They often combine:

  • noticeable acidity;
  • a firm, rounded or textured mouthfeel;
  • moderate to full-feeling body;
  • savoury, mineral or salty impressions; and
  • a persistent finish.

Younger wines may offer citrus, white flowers and white-fleshed or orchard fruit. With bottle age, some examples develop aromas described as flint, honey, balsamic, toasted, dried-fruit or petrol-like.

These are tendencies, not rules. Timorasso does not always smell of petrol, and every bottle is not necessarily full-bodied or strongly mineral. Vineyard site, vintage, ripeness, fermentation vessel, lees contact, skin contact, oak and bottle age can all influence the result.

The variety is sometimes described in technical research as “non-aromatic”, meaning that strong primary grape aromas do not necessarily dominate. That does not mean the wine has no aroma. It means its character may come as much from texture, freshness, maturation and winemaking as from immediately obvious fruit perfume.

How is Timorasso made?

The central style is dry, still white wine, but winemaking choices can create quite different expressions.

Some wines are matured in neutral vessels or with time on lees, which can help build texture without adding obvious oak flavour. Oak maturation may contribute toasted, dried-fruit or caramel-like notes. Skin contact can also influence aroma, colour and mouthfeel, although neither oak nor skin contact should be assumed in every bottle.

Timorasso is therefore not the same thing as “orange wine”, oaked white wine or natural wine. Those descriptions refer to production choices, while Timorasso identifies the grape.

Some Colli Tortonesi wines also carry a Riserva designation. The regional specification gives that category a minimum ageing requirement of 21 months. That is a regulatory classification, not a universal instruction that every Timorasso must be aged for 21 months.

Can Timorasso age?

Yes—some Timorasso wines can develop interesting complexity in bottle. This is one of the variety’s most useful qualities for curious drinkers.

Younger bottles tend to show fresher fruit and floral notes. In some studied wines, five- to six-year-old examples showed more kerosene-like, balsamic or toasted character, while other mature wines developed honeyed, nutty or dried-fruit notes.

Ageing is not guaranteed to improve every bottle. The result depends on the wine’s structure, winemaking, closure, storage conditions and vintage. If you open a younger bottle, expect freshness; if you find a mature example, look for greater savoury and developed complexity rather than simply more fruit.

How should you serve Timorasso?

For a dry, still Timorasso, try serving it at approximately 10–12°C. A fuller or more mature bottle may show more detail if it warms slightly in the glass.

A standard white-wine glass with enough bowl volume gives the wine room to release its aromas. There is no need for specialised glassware to begin exploring the variety.

For food, use the wine’s acidity and texture as your guide. It can be a useful match for seafood, shellfish, richer fish, roast or grilled poultry, savoury vegetable dishes, fresh cheeses, pasta and risotto. These are starting points, not strict rules: preparation, sauce and overall richness matter as much as the main ingredient.

Can you find Timorasso in Australia?

Imported Timorasso is commercially available in Australia, although it is more likely to be found through specialist wine retailers, Italian-wine importers, restaurants and broader wine lists than through a typical supermarket range.

Australian evidence confirms imported listings, but does not justify claims that Timorasso is widely available or that demand is rapidly growing. No reliable evidence in the approved research confirms commercial Australian plantings, so Australian-grown Timorasso should not be assumed.

If you see Timorasso on a list or shelf, the simplest way to approach it is to expect a dry white with freshness, texture and the possibility of bottle-developed savoury character. It is an appealing choice when you want something more substantial than a very light white, without treating the grape as a copy of Riesling, Chablis or another variety.

Frequently asked questions

Is Timorasso a white wine or a grape?

Timorasso is a white wine-grape variety. Wines made from it are generally dry, still whites, although the exact style depends on the designation and winemaking.

Is Derthona the same as Timorasso?

Not exactly. Timorasso is the grape. Derthona is a wine and regional identity associated with Tortona and Timorasso. The terms should not be treated as universal synonyms.

Is Timorasso sweet?

The main style is dry white wine. A wine’s fruitiness or honeyed aroma, especially after ageing, does not necessarily mean it contains noticeable sweetness.

Does Timorasso taste like petrol?

Some mature examples can develop petrol- or kerosene-like notes, but this is not guaranteed and is not the defining characteristic of every Timorasso.

What food goes with Timorasso?

Try seafood, shellfish, richer fish, poultry, savoury vegetables, fresh cheese, pasta or risotto. Match the wine to the dish’s richness and sauce rather than following a rigid rule.
