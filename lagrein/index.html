What Is Lagrein? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what Lagrein is, where it comes from, how red and rosé expressions differ, what it may taste like and what Australian wine learners should expect from the label.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Lagrein is a distinct black-skinned red wine grape, not a region or a general wine style.
  • It is principally associated with Alto Adige/Südtirol in northern Italy, especially around Bolzano, and is also grown in neighbouring Trentino.
  • Red Lagrein is often deeply coloured, with dark berry, cherry, violet, spice and earthy characters.
  • Lagrein can also be made as a rosé called Lagrein Rosato or Lagrein Kretzer.
  • A bottle labelled Lagrein tells you the grape variety, not exactly how sweet, full-bodied or tannic the wine will be.
  • Australian learners may encounter the name because Lagrein is recognised in Australia and has documented local viticultural interest, although its current national scale is not established.

Quick answer: what is Lagrein?

Lagrein is a black-skinned Vitis vinifera wine-grape variety from northern Italy. It is most strongly associated with Alto Adige/Südtirol, particularly the area around Bolzano, and also has a long connection with neighbouring Trentino.

The grape is used for both red and rosé wines. Red Lagrein is commonly associated with deep colour, dark fruit, floral notes such as violet, spice and a full or velvety texture. Depending on where and how it is made, it may also feel fresher, lighter or more structured.

The rosé expression is known as Lagrein Rosato and, in Alto Adige, may be called Lagrein Kretzer. It comes from the same grape, but shorter skin contact during winemaking changes the colour, tannin and texture.

The most useful thing to remember is that “Lagrein” identifies a grape, not one fixed flavour profile. Climate, ripeness at harvest, skin contact, extraction, oak and alcohol all influence the finished wine.

Where does Lagrein come from?

Lagrein’s principal modern identity is Alto Adige/Südtirol, an Italian wine region near the country’s northern border. Bolzano is an important local reference point, but Lagrein should not be understood as a grape belonging only to that town.

The variety is also associated with Trentino, the neighbouring wine region to the south. These are separate regional wine contexts, even though both are in northern Italy and both have a historical and current connection with Lagrein.

On a label, you may see “Lagrein” as the grape name. In Alto Adige, regional language traditions can also appear alongside Italian names. “Lagrein Kretzer” is not a different grape: it refers to the rosé expression of Lagrein.

What does red Lagrein taste like?

Red Lagrein is often dark in colour and fruit-driven, but it is not automatically heavy or powerful. Commonly reported characters include:

  • blackberry and other dark berries;
  • fresh cherry;
  • violet or other floral notes;
  • spice;
  • earthy or savoury tones.

In the mouth, it may feel rounded, velvety and generous, or more structured, depending on the wine. Tannin—the drying, grippy sensation also found in strong tea—can contribute shape, particularly when the wine has had substantial contact with grape skins. Oak maturation may add further texture and spice.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. A cooler site, earlier harvest or gentler extraction may produce a fresher and less dense wine. Riper fruit, longer skin contact, higher alcohol or oak maturation can make the wine feel fuller and more concentrated.

That is why two wines made from the same grape can taste quite different. The variety provides possibilities; the vineyard and winery determine how those possibilities appear.

Red Lagrein and Lagrein Kretzer: what is the difference?

The simplest distinction is colour and texture.

Expression What to expect
Red Lagrein Usually darker in colour, with more skin-derived tannin and a fuller or more structured texture
Lagrein Rosato / Kretzer A rosé expression made with less skin contact, generally showing less colour and tannin

Lagrein Kretzer should not be treated as a separate grape or as a guaranteed light, sweet or delicate wine. It is a different expression of the same variety. As with any rosé, its final weight, freshness and fruit character depend on the fruit and winemaking choices.

A useful beginner’s distinction is this: dark fruit flavours do not necessarily mean a wine is sweet. A red Lagrein can be dry while still tasting richly of ripe berries. “Dry” describes the amount of residual sugar left in the wine; fruitiness describes its aroma and flavour.

What should Australian wine learners expect?

Lagrein is included in Wine Australia’s recognised grape-variety list. Australian industry and technical sources also document Lagrein planting material, maintained vine collections and at least some Australian planting activity.

That establishes Australian viticultural interest, but it does not prove that Lagrein is widely planted, produced in large quantities or commonly available nationwide. If you see the name on an Australian wine list or label, read it first as the name of a grape variety with northern Italian roots.

An Australian-grown Lagrein may not taste identical to one from Alto Adige or Trentino. Differences in climate, harvest timing, vineyard site and winemaking can change its balance of fruit, alcohol, freshness, tannin and oak. There is not enough evidence to define one universal Australian Lagrein style.

Is Lagrein related to Teroldego?

Lagrein and Teroldego are separate northern Italian grape varieties. Reference material records a relationship between them in Lagrein’s pedigree, but that does not make the grapes interchangeable or guarantee that they taste alike.

The same caution applies to older or technical names recorded in some grape references. “Lagrein” is the clearest name for Australian readers to recognise. “Lagrein Kretzer” refers to a rosé expression, not a synonym for a different variety.

How should you serve Lagrein?

For a first taste, approach red Lagrein as a dry red that may combine dark fruit with floral, spicy or savoury detail. Serve it slightly cool rather than warm; excessive warmth can make a full or alcoholic wine seem heavy. A rosé expression can be approached more like other dry rosé wines, with its freshness and texture given room to show.

Rather than expecting one “correct” Lagrein flavour, notice three things:

  1. How dark or bright is the fruit?
  2. Does the wine feel soft, velvety or grippy?
  3. Does the finish bring freshness, spice or savoury character?

Those observations will tell you more about the individual bottle than the grape name alone.

Frequently asked questions

Is Lagrein a red or white grape?

Lagrein is a black-skinned grape used mainly for red wine, but it is also made into rosé, including Lagrein Rosato or Lagrein Kretzer.

Is Lagrein always full-bodied?

No. It can make deeply coloured and full-feeling wines, but body depends on climate, ripeness, extraction, alcohol and maturation.

Is Lagrein sweet?

Not necessarily. Red and rosé Lagrein can be dry. Ripe fruit aromas may make a dry wine seem fruitier, but fruitiness is not the same as sweetness.

Where is Lagrein grown?

It is principally associated with Alto Adige/Südtirol and is also grown in Trentino. Australian sources document some local planting and industry interest, but not widespread national production.

Is Lagrein the same as Lagrein Kretzer?

No. Lagrein Kretzer is a rosé expression made from the Lagrein grape.
