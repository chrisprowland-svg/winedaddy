# Mendoza subregions: a beginner’s guide to Argentina’s wine geography

## Highlights

- Mendoza is a broad wine-producing province and geographic indication in western Argentina, close to the Andes.
- It is not one uniform wine area. Elevation, climate, water, soils, vineyard site and winemaking can vary considerably.
- The simplest useful framework is **Primera Zona** and **Uco Valley**.
- Primera Zona is an industry grouping centred on **Luján de Cuyo** and **Maipú**. It is not automatically a standalone legal GI.
- **Uco Valley** is a recognised geographic indication comprising the departments and GIs of **Tupungato, Tunuyán and San Carlos**.
- Mendoza is strongly associated with **Malbec**, but it also produces Cabernet Sauvignon, Bonarda, Cabernet Franc, Chardonnay, Semillón and other varieties.
- A place name on a label is a geographic clue, not a guarantee of flavour, quality or winemaking style.

## Quick answer: what are Mendoza’s main subregions?

Mendoza is Argentina’s principal wine province and a broad geographic designation rather than a single, uniform wine region. For an Australian wine learner, the most useful starting point is to compare **Primera Zona**, around Luján de Cuyo and Maipú, with the higher-elevation **Uco Valley** to the south-west.

Luján de Cuyo and Maipú are associated with Mendoza’s established central wine areas. Luján de Cuyo is a recognised geographic indication and also has DOC status. Uco Valley is another recognised geographic indication, officially comprising **Tupungato, Tunuyán and San Carlos**.

Mendoza is especially associated with Malbec. However, the wine in your glass also reflects the vineyard site, altitude, water access, vintage, producer and winemaking choices. A Mendoza label tells you where the grapes came from; it does not tell the whole story.

## Where is Mendoza?

Mendoza is in western Argentina, east of the Andes. Mendoza city is the most useful regional reference point for understanding the province’s wine geography.

Luján de Cuyo and Maipú sit within the broader Mendoza city and wine-region context. Uco Valley extends south-west from Mendoza city towards the Andes. As a broad orientation, parts of Uco Valley are roughly 80–100 kilometres from Mendoza city, while travel times vary according to the destination and route.

The important point for wine is not the driving distance. It is that Mendoza covers a wide area, with vineyards at different elevations and in different landscape settings.

## The terminology: province, department, GI and DOC

Mendoza can be confusing because several types of geographic name appear together.

- A **province** is an administrative area. Mendoza Province is part of Argentina.
- A **department** is an administrative subdivision within the province. Tupungato, Tunuyán, San Carlos, Luján de Cuyo and Maipú are examples.
- An **IG**, or *Indicación Geográfica*, is Argentina’s term for a legally recognised geographic indication.
- A **DOC**, or *Denominación de Origen Controlada*, is a more specific controlled-origin designation. Luján de Cuyo is listed as both an IG and a DOC.
- A **paraje** is a named place or locality. It may have its own recognised geographic indication, but it should not automatically be treated as a department or valley.

This is not exactly the same system as Australia’s GI framework. The useful comparison is functional: both systems can communicate geographic origin, but their legal categories and labelling rules differ.

## Primera Zona: Luján de Cuyo and Maipú

**Primera Zona** is a commonly used wine-industry framework for the traditional areas of **Luján de Cuyo and Maipú**. Treat it as regional shorthand rather than automatically as a separate legal GI.

### Luján de Cuyo

Luján de Cuyo lies south-west of Mendoza city, near the Andes foothills. It is a recognised IG and DOC, making it one of the more specific geographic references a drinker may encounter on a Mendoza label.

Luján de Cuyo has a strong association with Malbec, while Cabernet Sauvignon and other varieties are also grown. Industry and regional descriptions may refer to ripe fruit, structure or concentration in some wines from the area. These are tendencies, not rules. Vineyard elevation, soil, irrigation, vine age, harvest decisions and winemaking can all change the result.

### Maipú

Maipú lies to the east or south-east of Mendoza city and is a recognised IG. It is part of the established central Mendoza wine landscape and is associated with Malbec and other traditional red varieties.

Maipú should not be treated as a synonym for Luján de Cuyo. Both sit within the Primera Zona framework, but they are separate geographic references.

## Uco Valley: Tupungato, Tunuyán and San Carlos

**Uco Valley**, also called **Valle de Uco**, is a recognised IG. The official framework identifies three constituent departments: **Tupungato, Tunuyán and San Carlos**.

The valley extends towards the Andes and includes substantial elevation variation. Broad regional material places vineyards in Uco Valley at approximately **860 to 1,610 metres**, although this is an orientation range rather than a universal elevation for every vineyard.

Uco Valley is strongly associated with Malbec. Cabernet Franc, Chardonnay, Semillón and other varieties are also important in modern regional discussions.

### Tupungato and Gualtallary

Tupungato is the northern department of Uco Valley and includes high-elevation areas. It is associated with Malbec and, increasingly, white varieties such as Chardonnay.

**Gualtallary** is widely used as a specific geographic reference within the Tupungato context. However, its exact current legal treatment should not be assumed from industry usage alone. It is safer to describe it as a recognised regional place name unless the applicable regulatory register confirms more precise wording.

### Tunuyán, Los Chacayes and San Pablo

Tunuyán forms the central part of Uco Valley. It is both a Mendoza department and a recognised GI, and includes more specific geographic references such as **Los Chacayes** and **San Pablo**.

These names can provide a more detailed sense of origin than simply “Mendoza” or “Uco Valley”. Wines from these areas are sometimes described as fresh, structured or lifted, particularly in relation to higher-elevation sites. Those descriptions indicate possible tendencies rather than guaranteed outcomes.

### San Carlos and Paraje Altamira

San Carlos is the southern department of Uco Valley and is also a recognised Mendoza GI. It includes specific geographic references such as **Paraje Altamira**.

Paraje Altamira is a recognised IG in San Carlos. It is not a synonym for all San Carlos wine or all Uco Valley wine. Its legal boundary has been addressed through a specific regulatory instrument, including boundary language involving the 1,000-metre contour. That contour is a boundary reference, not a claim that every vineyard in the area sits at exactly that elevation.

## Why do the wines vary?

Mendoza is arid to semi-arid, and viticulture depends heavily on irrigation. Water from Andean catchments is distributed through managed systems, so water access and irrigation design are important parts of the vineyard environment.

Elevation generally increases towards the Andes and through parts of Uco Valley. Higher sites can have different temperature patterns, sunlight exposure, growing-season conditions and day–night variation. These factors may help retain acidity or influence ripening in some vineyards.

Altitude is not a magic explanation for wine character. Soil depth and drainage, aspect, vine material, crop load, irrigation, harvest timing and winemaking also matter. This is why two wines from the same broad subregion can still taste different.

## How to read a Mendoza label

A useful hierarchy is:

1. **Mendoza**: the broadest geographic clue.
2. **Luján de Cuyo** or **Maipú**: more specific recognised geographic references.
3. **Uco Valley**: a recognised GI covering Tupungato, Tunuyán and San Carlos.
4. **Tupungato, Tunuyán or San Carlos**: department-level or GI references that narrow the origin.
5. **Paraje Altamira, Los Chacayes or San Pablo**: more specific geographic references where the applicable designation is used.

The place name can help you form a starting expectation, but it cannot guarantee a particular level of oak, alcohol, tannin, acidity, ripeness or quality. Read it alongside the grape variety, vintage, producer and technical information.

## FAQs

### Is Primera Zona an official GI?

Not automatically. Primera Zona is a commonly used industry and regional grouping centred on Luján de Cuyo and Maipú. The individual geographic indications should be treated as the formal references unless a specific regulation says otherwise.

### Is every wine from Mendoza Malbec?

No. Malbec is Mendoza’s strongest variety association, but Cabernet Sauvignon, Bonarda, Cabernet Franc, Chardonnay, Semillón, Syrah, Tempranillo and Sauvignon Blanc are also grown.

### Is Uco Valley the same as Mendoza?

No. Uco Valley is a recognised GI within Mendoza’s wine geography. Mendoza is the broader province and geographic indication.

### Does higher altitude always mean fresher wine?

No. Higher elevation can influence temperature, sunlight and ripening, and may contribute to freshness in some sites. But wine style depends on several interacting factors, including vineyard conditions and winemaking.
