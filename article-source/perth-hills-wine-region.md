# Perth Hills wine region: a beginner’s guide

Perth Hills is a protected Australian wine region in Western Australia, located within the **Greater Perth wine zone**. It sits on and around the Darling Scarp east of Perth, where elevation, slopes, airflow and local exposure create more variation than the region’s name might suggest.

The region is associated most strongly with **Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Chardonnay and Shiraz**. Its wines can be generous and full flavoured, but there is no single “Perth Hills style”. The conditions differ from site to site, and the winemaking choices behind each bottle matter too.

## Highlights
- Perth Hills is a **Geographical Indication (GI) region** within the Greater Perth wine zone.
- It is roughly **30–45 minutes east of Perth by road**, depending on your starting point and destination.
- The region is linked to the elevated, sloping country of the Darling Scarp.
- Wine Australia records an elevation range of **10–573 metres** across the GI.
- The principal varieties best supported by regional evidence are Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Chardonnay and Shiraz.
- A Perth Hills label indicates geographic origin; it does not guarantee a particular grape, flavour, quality level or price.
- Separate nearby GIs include **Swan District** and **Peel**.

## Where is the Perth Hills wine region?

Perth Hills is in Western Australia, east of the Perth metropolitan area. Kalamunda is a useful regional centre for the northern and central Hills, while Armadale is a practical reference point for the southern end.

Readers may also encounter localities such as Mundaring, Bickley, Glen Forrest, Darlington, Pickering Brook, Jarrahdale and Serpentine-Jarrahdale when learning about the area. These are useful place names, but they should not be treated as a list of the legal GI boundary. The broader Perth Hills tourism area and the regulated wine region are not necessarily identical.

In Australian wine terms, the hierarchy is:

**Western Australia → Greater Perth wine zone → Perth Hills region**

Perth Hills was entered in Australia’s Register of Protected Names on **25 March 1999**. Its legal boundary is defined by Wine Australia’s official textual GI description. A map can help with orientation, but it does not replace that legal description.

## What makes Perth Hills different?

The simplest way to understand Perth Hills is to picture a landscape of ridges, slopes, valleys and changing aspects rather than one flat vineyard area.

Wine Australia records a regional elevation range of **10–573 metres**, although vineyards are commonly discussed in the approximate **150–400 metre** band when explaining the effects of altitude. Elevation can bring cooler nights and greater airflow, while slopes and aspect—the direction a site faces—can alter sunlight and ripening conditions.

Afternoon sea breezes are another influence. In many elevated sites, airflow and altitude can delay ripening compared with the Swan Valley by around 10–21 days. That does not mean every Perth Hills vineyard ripens at the same time. Exposure, slope, elevation and individual vineyard conditions all contribute.

The region’s climate is broadly characterised by warm, dry summers and mild to cooler winters, with rainfall concentrated in winter and spring. Wine Australia’s regional profile records approximately **900–1,200 millimetres of annual rainfall** and about **183 millimetres of growing-season rainfall**. These figures describe different periods, so they should not be compared as though they were the same measurement.

Soils also vary, including sandy and gravelly loams, gravelly loams over clay, brown clays, ironstone and other gravelly materials. Soil is one part of a vineyard’s environment—not a shortcut to predicting exactly how every wine will taste.

## What wines is Perth Hills known for?

### Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot

Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot are two of the region’s important red varieties. They may be bottled separately or combined in Cabernet–Merlot blends.

Perth Hills examples are broadly associated with full-flavoured berry, chocolate and earthy characters. That is a regional tendency, not a promise that every bottle will be rich or powerful. Earlier-picked fruit, lighter extraction, different oak choices and site conditions can all produce a different result.

### Chardonnay

Chardonnay is one of Perth Hills’ recognised white varieties. Regional descriptions commonly associate it with generous, ripe-fruited characters such as melon and peach.

That does not tell you whether a particular Chardonnay will be crisp, creamy, oaky or lightly textured. Variety, vineyard site and winemaking approach can shift the style considerably.

### Shiraz

Shiraz is another principal variety for Perth Hills. It is generally associated with generous, fruit-driven red wines, including red-berry and cherry character.

Again, “fruit driven” does not necessarily mean sweet. A dry wine can smell and taste strongly of ripe fruit while containing little residual sugar. The wine’s acidity, tannin, alcohol and texture help determine how full or fresh it feels.

Other varieties, including Chenin Blanc and Tempranillo, may also be found in the wider regional planting mix. However, the strongest evidence for a beginner’s overview points to Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Chardonnay and Shiraz as the core varieties to know first.

## How does Perth Hills compare with nearby regions?

**Swan District** is a separate GI within the Greater Perth zone. It is generally warmer to hotter and is particularly associated with varieties such as Chenin Blanc, Verdelho and Chardonnay. Swan Valley is a subregion within Swan District, not part of Perth Hills.

**Peel** is another separate Greater Perth GI, south of Perth Hills. Its broader coastal and inland setting differs from the elevated Darling Scarp landscape, and Shiraz is strongly associated with the region.

These comparisons are useful for identity, not for ranking. All three regions contain variation, and a regional tendency cannot describe every wine made there.

## What does “Perth Hills” mean on a wine label?

When “Perth Hills” appears as a geographic indication on an Australian wine label, it tells you that the wine has a regulated connection to that geographic area under Australia’s wine-labelling framework.

It does **not** guarantee:

- a particular grape variety;
- a specific flavour profile;
- a certain quality level or price;
- a particular winemaking method;
- that every vineyard shares the same elevation, rainfall or soil;
- that the wine was made in a particular town.

For the precise percentage requirements applying to geographic or variety claims, consult current Wine Australia labelling guidance. For everyday reading, the useful point is this: **the GI tells you where the wine comes from, not exactly what it will taste like**.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Perth Hills a wine region or a town?

It is a protected wine-region GI. “Perth Hills” is also used more broadly for local tourism and geographic orientation, so the everyday area and the legal wine boundary should not be assumed to be identical.

### Is Perth Hills a cool-climate wine region?

It is better understood as a varied elevated region. Warm Western Australian conditions are modified in many sites by altitude, cool nights, airflow and sea breezes. Conditions are not uniform across the GI.

### What should I try first from Perth Hills?

Start by choosing a variety you already understand. Cabernet Sauvignon or Merlot can introduce the region’s red-wine side, while Chardonnay offers a white-wine comparison. Shiraz is another useful starting point. The label gives you a place clue, but the variety and producer style remain important.

### Is Swan Valley part of Perth Hills?

No. Swan Valley is a subregion within the separate Swan District GI. Both are within the broader Greater Perth wine zone.

## The takeaway

Perth Hills is an elevated and topographically varied Western Australian wine region east of Perth. Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Chardonnay and Shiraz are the key varieties to know, but the region is not stylistically uniform. Its GI name provides useful geographic information; to understand the bottle in front of you, also consider the grape variety, vintage and winemaking style.
