# What preservative-free wine means

## The quick answer

**Preservative-free wine usually means wine made without added preservative sulphur dioxide or sulphites.** But it does not necessarily mean the wine contains no sulphur compounds, no other additives or no naturally occurring substances.

Yeast can produce small amounts of sulphites during fermentation. That is why **“no added preservatives”**, **“preservative-free”** and **“contains sulphites”** are not interchangeable statements.

Sulphur dioxide is widely used in winemaking because it helps limit oxidation and unwanted microbial activity. Without that protection, some wines may be more vulnerable to changes in flavour, aroma and freshness. However, there is no single taste, quality or shelf-life profile for every preservative-free wine.

## Highlights

- Preservative-free is usually a claim about added sulphur dioxide or sulphites.
- “No added preservatives” does not necessarily mean zero sulphites.
- Yeast can naturally produce sulphites during fermentation.
- Sulphur dioxide helps protect wine from oxidation and unwanted microbes.
- Preservative-free does not automatically mean organic, biodynamic, natural or healthier.
- In Australia, added sulphites at 10 mg/kg or more must be declared.
- The bottle’s style, packaging, storage and producer guidance matter more than the phrase alone.

## What does “preservative-free” refer to?

The word **preservative** is broad. In wine, “preservative-free” commonly refers to avoiding added sulphur dioxide, also called **SO₂**, or related sulphite preservatives.

Sulphur dioxide has two particularly useful roles. It helps protect wine from oxygen-related deterioration and slows unwanted microbial activity. Winemakers may use it alongside careful hygiene, filtration, low-oxygen handling, packaging choices and temperature control.

“Preservative-free” does not mean “chemical-free”. Wine is a fermented drink containing many naturally occurring compounds. More importantly, yeast can produce sulphites during fermentation, even when the winemaker has not deliberately added them.

That creates an important distinction:

- **No added preservatives** describes what was deliberately put into the wine.
- **Preservative-free** is a stronger claim that needs appropriate substantiation.
- **Contains sulphites** is an Australian disclosure statement used when the relevant added-sulphite threshold is met.

Wine Australia advises that “preservative free” should only be claimed when no quantifiable sulphur dioxide is present. It also indicates that “no added preservatives” may be suitable in some wines where naturally occurring sulphur dioxide is below 10 mg/kg.

## Why is sulphur dioxide used in wine?

Wine can change when exposed to oxygen. It can also be affected by unwanted microorganisms. Sulphur dioxide provides useful protection against both processes.

When that protection is absent or reduced, some wines may be more susceptible to:

- premature oxidation;
- microbial spoilage;
- volatile acidity;
- changes in aroma and flavour;
- reduced freshness during storage.

These are **possibilities, not guarantees**. A wine’s pH, hygiene, filtration, packaging, oxygen exposure, storage temperature and overall composition also influence how stable it is.

So, a preservative-free wine is not automatically fragile, faulty or inferior. It simply may rely more heavily on other winemaking and storage controls.

## Does preservative-free wine taste different?

There is no universal “preservative-free flavour”.

Some wines made with little or no added sulphur dioxide may develop oxidative or microbial characters more readily. In other bottles, those changes may be minimal. The result depends on the wine and how it was made and stored.

It is therefore misleading to say that preservative-free wine is always fresher, fruitier, funkier, more natural or more expressive. The phrase describes a production or labelling claim, not a reliable flavour style.

## How does Australian labelling work?

Under Australian and New Zealand food standards, **added sulphites must be declared at 10 mg/kg or more**. For Australian wine labelling, the prescribed plain-English allergen term is **“sulphites”**; a declaration such as **“Contains sulphites”** may appear on the label.

A sulphite declaration tells you that the relevant disclosure threshold has been reached. The absence of that declaration does **not** prove that the wine contains no naturally occurring sulphites, nor does it explain every detail of how the wine was made.

Retailer descriptions may shorten or repeat producer wording. If the distinction matters to you, look for the exact phrase on the bottle and ask the producer or retailer:

- Was sulphur dioxide added?
- Does “preservative-free” refer to testing of the finished wine?
- Are there storage or consumption instructions?

A generic label visual such as **VIS-009** may help readers compare these kinds of statements, provided it is checked for current Australian wording.

## Is preservative-free the same as organic, biodynamic or natural?

No. These terms describe different things.

| Term | What it mainly describes | Does it guarantee no added sulphites? |
|---|---|---|
| Preservative-free | A claim about the absence of quantifiable preservative residue, commonly sulphur dioxide | No; it needs producer substantiation |
| No added preservatives | Preservatives were not deliberately added | No; naturally occurring sulphites may remain |
| Low-sulphite | Relatively low sulphite use or levels | No universal equivalence |
| Organic | Organic production inputs and standards | No; permitted sulphur dioxide may still be used |
| Biodynamic | A separate biodynamic production system or claim | No |
| Natural | A variable, low-intervention industry term | No; it is not a universal synonym |

Organic wine may contain permitted sulphur dioxide. “Natural wine” may involve limited or no SO₂ in some cases, but natural wine and preservative-free wine are not exact equivalents. One term should not be used as proof of another.

## What does this mean for storage?

Preservative-free status alone cannot tell you how long a wine will stay fresh.

For an unopened bottle, follow the producer’s storage advice and keep the wine away from heat and large temperature swings. After opening, limit oxygen exposure by resealing the bottle and storing it appropriately; refrigeration may help for wines suited to it.

Do not rely on a fixed number of days. Some bottles will change quickly, while others may remain enjoyable for longer. The wine’s style, packaging, fill level, storage conditions and production choices all matter.

## Is preservative-free wine healthier?

Not automatically. “Preservative-free” is not evidence that a wine is healthier, safer, purer or less likely to cause a headache.

Food Standards Australia New Zealand says sulphites are safe for most people, although some sulphite-sensitive people may react. If you think you react to sulphites, check the label and ask the producer or retailer for product-specific information. For recurring, severe or uncertain reactions, seek advice from a qualified medical professional or allergy specialist.

Preservative-free wine should not be used as a substitute for medical advice, and it should not be assumed to prevent headaches.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does no added preservatives mean no sulphites?

No. Yeast can produce sulphites during fermentation, so naturally occurring sulphites may remain.

### Does preservative-free mean additive-free?

No. Preservative-free is not the same as free from all additives or processing aids.

### Is organic wine preservative-free?

No. Organic production claims and preservative claims are separate. Permitted sulphur dioxide may still be used in organic wine.

### Should preservative-free wine be drunk immediately?

There is no universal drinking window. Follow producer guidance and reduce oxygen exposure after opening.

### Is preservative-free wine natural wine?

Not necessarily. The terms may overlap in some bottles, but neither is a guaranteed synonym for the other.

The most useful rule is simple: **read the exact wording, ask the producer when necessary, and treat each bottle as an individual wine rather than assuming the phrase tells you everything.**
