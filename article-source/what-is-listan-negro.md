# What is Listán Negro?

## Highlights

- Listán Negro is a dark-skinned red wine-grape variety.
- It is best known from Spain’s Canary Islands, especially Tenerife.
- Wines are often dry, fresh and red-fruited, with low to moderate tannin.
- Smoke, spice, mineral and herbal notes may appear, but they are not guaranteed.
- **Almuñeco** is a documented synonym; **Negra Común** is used regionally for Listán Negro.
- Listán Negro is a different variety from Listán Prieto and Listán Blanco.
- Australian drinkers are most likely to encounter it in imported or specialist wine selections.

## Quick answer

Listán Negro is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* grape variety used to make red wine. It is strongly associated with Spain’s Canary Islands, particularly Tenerife, where it is well suited to volcanic soils and varied local climates.

The wines are commonly described as dry, fresh and red-fruited, sometimes with peppery, smoky, mineral, herbal or savoury notes. Many examples are light to medium-bodied with low to moderate tannin, although the finished wine can vary considerably. Vineyard site, climate, ripeness, winemaking and maturation all influence what ends up in the glass.

The name **Listán Negro** identifies the grape. It is not a region, denomination or winemaking method.

## Where is Listán Negro from?

Listán Negro is principally associated with the Canary Islands, an autonomous community of Spain. It is especially important in Tenerife and is described by the Canary Islands wine industry as the archipelago’s most planted red grape variety. It is also used in other island wine areas, including Gran Canaria.

On a bottle, you might see **Listán Negro** alongside the name of an island, denomination, producer or other place. These describe different things: the grape is the variety, while the island or denomination identifies geographic origin.

## What does Listán Negro wine taste like?

There is no single guaranteed Listán Negro flavour profile, but broad descriptions often include:

- dry, fresh-tasting wines;
- red-fruit flavours such as cherry or strawberry;
- noticeable acidity;
- low to moderate tannin; and
- possible pepper, smoke, mineral, herbal or savoury characters.

Some wines may feel bright and relatively light, while others can be more concentrated or savoury. The grape is only one part of the explanation. Altitude, climate, harvest timing, crop level, fermentation choices, extraction, maturation and blending can all change the wine’s colour, aroma, texture and weight.

This is why a label reading Listán Negro is a useful starting point, not a promise that every bottle will taste the same.

## Is Listán Negro the same as Listán Prieto?

No. Listán Negro and Listán Prieto are distinct grape varieties, although they are commonly confused because their names are similar.

**Listán Blanco** is also different: it is a white-skinned variety. Similarly, Listán Negro should not automatically be treated as another name for Mission, País, Criolla Chica or Negra Peruana. Historical grape names and genetic relationships can be complicated, so similar-sounding names are not enough to establish that two varieties are identical.

The Canary Islands government lists **Almuñeco** as a synonym for Listán Negro. Official Gran Canaria material also uses **Negra Común (Listán Negro)** as a regional naming form.

## How might Australians encounter it?

For an Australian wine drinker, Listán Negro is most likely to appear in an imported Canary Islands wine, a specialist retailer’s selection, a restaurant with a broad European list or a wine-education tasting. The available Australian industry evidence does not support describing it as widely planted or commonly produced in Australia.

If you encounter the grape on a label, expect a red wine with freshness and red-fruit character as possible starting points—but remain open to smoke, spice, savoury detail and differences in body or tannin. The grape name tells you what variety is involved; the place and winemaking details help explain the particular bottle.
