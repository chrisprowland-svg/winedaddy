# What is Poulsard?

## Highlights

- Poulsard is a red wine-grape variety, especially associated with France’s Jura region.
- It is also known as **Ploussard**, particularly around Arbois and Pupillin.
- Its wines are often pale ruby, translucent red or rosé-like in appearance.
- Fresh acidity, red-fruit character and delicate structure are common tendencies.
- Poulsard is a grape, not a wine style or appellation.
- The finished wine can vary significantly depending on the vineyard, ripeness, blending and winemaking.

## Quick answer

Poulsard is a red grape variety from eastern France, best known through the wines of the Jura. Although the grape has dark skins, its relatively thin skins and limited colour extraction often produce wines that are much paler than a typical deep red.

A Poulsard wine may look light ruby or almost translucent, and can sometimes resemble a rosé. In the glass, you might expect fresh acidity, red-fruit aromas and a lighter, more delicate structure than you would find in many wines made from Trousseau or heavily extracted red varieties.

That description is a starting point, not a guarantee. Poulsard can be made in different ways, and blending with varieties such as Pinot Noir or Trousseau can add colour and body.

## Poulsard and Ploussard: are they the same grape?

Yes. **Ploussard is a local name for Poulsard**, especially in and around the Jura towns of Arbois and Pupillin. The two names refer to the same grape variety rather than two different grapes.

Poulsard is the main term used in this article, but a bottle or wine list may use either spelling when referring to a Jura wine.

## Why is Poulsard wine so pale?

Wine colour comes largely from contact with the skins of dark-coloured grapes. Poulsard has relatively thin skins and pale-coloured juice, so it generally gives up less colour during winemaking than a thick-skinned variety.

The result can be a red wine with a light ruby colour, a translucent appearance or coral and orange-tinged hues. This is one of Poulsard’s most memorable traits: a wine can look almost fragile but still be unmistakably red in flavour and structure.

A pale colour does not automatically mean the wine is rosé. Rosé is a wine category made through a particular style of production and presentation; Poulsard is a grape variety. Poulsard can be used for rosé, and some Poulsard reds may look rosé-like, but the terms are not interchangeable.

## What does Poulsard taste like?

Typical Poulsard wines are associated with:

- fresh or lively acidity;
- red-fruit aromas;
- small-red-fruit character;
- light ruby colour;
- a delicate or finely structured impression.

The exact flavour may change with the site, crop level, ripeness, skin extraction and ageing. A lightly made Poulsard may feel bright and airy, while another example can have more concentration or savoury complexity.

It is also worth separating **fruit flavour** from **sweetness**. A wine can smell and taste of fresh red fruit while remaining dry. Poulsard’s red-fruit character does not mean the wine contains noticeable sugar.

## Is Poulsard light-bodied?

Often, but not always. Poulsard commonly produces a lighter-bodied wine than darker, more tannic red varieties. Its colour and texture can make it feel refreshing and nimble rather than dense or weighty.

However, grape variety does not determine the whole wine. Ripeness, yields, extraction, maturation and blending all matter. A Poulsard wine is not necessarily thin, simple or tannin-free.

In Jura, Poulsard may be blended with Pinot Noir or Trousseau. These varieties can contribute additional colour and body, changing the final balance. This is why two wines carrying the Poulsard name may look and taste quite different.

## Poulsard compared with other Jura grapes

Poulsard is one of the Jura’s principal grape varieties, but it is not the same as the region’s other well-known grapes.

**Trousseau** is a separate red variety. It generally produces more intensely coloured and more tannic wines than Poulsard, although individual wines vary.

**Pinot Noir** is also a separate grape and may be blended with Poulsard. Pinot Noir is not simply a darker version of Poulsard.

**Savagnin** is another distinct Jura variety, most closely associated with white wines. It is not a synonym for Poulsard.

These grapes may appear together in discussions of Jura wine, but their names should not be treated as interchangeable.

## What style of wine is Poulsard?

The most useful expectation is a **pale, fresh red wine with red-fruit character**. Depending on how it is made, Poulsard may also appear as a rosé or as part of a regional Jura blend.

The grape itself does not dictate one winemaking method. Poulsard is not inherently an oxidative wine, a natural wine, a low-alcohol wine or a wine with no tannin. Those descriptions relate to particular wines and production choices, not to the grape as a fixed rule.

This distinction can help when reading a label: the grape tells you one important part of the story, while the region, appellation and winemaking provide the rest.

## Where does Poulsard come from?

Poulsard is most strongly associated with France’s Jura wine region, where it has a long-standing regional identity and is used in several protected Jura wine frameworks. Arbois and Pupillin are particularly associated with the Ploussard name.

Jura is Poulsard’s principal geographic home in wine culture, but that does not mean the grape can only be grown there. A regional association describes where the variety is best known; it is not a claim that every Poulsard vine in the world is in Jura.

## How might Australians encounter Poulsard?

For Australian wine drinkers, Poulsard is likely to be a specialist discovery rather than a mainstream variety. You may encounter it through an imported Jura wine, a specialist retailer or a restaurant with a broader European-focused list. Occasional Australian examples may also appear, but the supplied industry evidence does not establish a confirmed national planting base, production scale or standard local availability.

Treat the name as a useful clue, not a promise of one exact taste. If you are choosing a bottle, look for the region, whether the wine is red or rosé, and any description of blending or winemaking. Those details can help explain why one Poulsard looks very pale while another has more colour and structure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Poulsard a red or white grape?

Poulsard is a red wine-grape variety. Its relatively pale colour potential can produce wines that look lighter than many other red wines.

### Is Ploussard a different grape from Poulsard?

No. Ploussard is a local name for the same grape, particularly in parts of the Jura including Arbois and Pupillin.

### Is Poulsard the same as Trousseau?

No. They are separate red grape varieties associated with Jura. Trousseau generally contributes more colour and tannin, while Poulsard is often paler and more delicate.

### Is Poulsard a rosé?

Not exactly. Poulsard is a grape variety. It can be used to make rosé, and some red Poulsard wines may look rosé-like, but “rosé” describes a wine category rather than the grape.

### Is Poulsard always light-bodied?

No. It often makes a lighter, delicately structured wine, but the final body depends on growing conditions, ripeness, extraction, blending and winemaking.

### Is Poulsard an oxidative or natural wine?

Not by definition. Oxidative or low-intervention character belongs to particular wines and methods, not to every wine made from Poulsard.
