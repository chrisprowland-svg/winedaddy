# What Is Torrontés? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Torrontés is an aromatic white wine grape, best known through the wines of Argentina.
- In Argentina, the name refers to three distinct varieties: Torrontés Riojano, Torrontés Sanjuanino and Torrontés Mendocino.
- A bottle labelled simply “Torrontés” usually refers to Torrontés Riojano, the principal commercial type.
- Torrontés wines are often intensely floral and fruity, yet commonly dry.
- Floral aromas can make a wine smell sweet without the wine actually containing much residual sugar.
- Argentina is the central geographic context, although Torrontés is also grown and made in Australia on a small documented scale.
- Serve it chilled, but not so cold that its aromas disappear.

## Quick answer: what is Torrontés?

Torrontés is a white wine grape associated most strongly with Argentina. It is known for highly perfumed wines that can suggest white flowers, rose, jasmine, peach, apricot, citrus and sometimes tropical fruit or herbs.

The name is slightly more complicated than it first appears. Argentina recognises three genetically distinct Torrontés varieties: Torrontés Riojano, Torrontés Sanjuanino and Torrontés Mendocino. Torrontés Riojano is the main practical reference for Australian wine learners because it is the dominant commercial type and the one most commonly intended when a label simply says “Torrontés”.

Despite its expressive aroma, Torrontés is commonly made as a dry white wine. In other words, it may smell richly floral and fruit-driven while tasting fresh rather than sweet. The grape can also be made in different styles, so no single bottle represents every Torrontés wine.

## What does Torrontés taste like?

Torrontés is usually an aromatic white wine: its grape character contributes pronounced primary aromas, meaning fruit, floral and herbal smells that are evident early in the wine’s life.

Common descriptors include:

- white flowers, rose or jasmine;
- peach and apricot;
- citrus;
- occasional tropical fruit;
- fresh herbs or a lightly spicy note.

On the palate, a typical dry Torrontés is fresh, fruit-driven and light- to medium-bodied. Its acidity can feel moderate to lively, depending on the climate, ripeness and winemaking. Some wines are smooth and rounded, while others feel leaner and more energetic.

These are patterns, not guarantees. Variety, vineyard conditions, harvest timing, residual sugar and choices such as fermentation vessel or lees contact can all change the result.

## Is Torrontés sweet?

Not necessarily. Many Torrontés wines are dry.

This is one of the most useful things to understand about the grape: **intense aroma is not the same as sweetness**. A wine can smell of ripe peaches, grapes or flowers while containing little residual sugar. Residual sugar is the sugar left in a wine after fermentation, and it—not the perfume alone—determines sweetness.

Torrontés often smells sweeter than it tastes. That contrast is part of its appeal.

## Why are there different Torrontés varieties?

The three Argentine types are distinct varieties rather than three names for exactly the same grape:

- **Torrontés Riojano:** the principal commercial type and the usual reference when a wine is labelled simply Torrontés.
- **Torrontés Sanjuanino:** a separate variety especially associated with San Juan.
- **Torrontés Mendocino:** another distinct variety, best mentioned as part of the naming picture rather than treated as the default style.

DNA research indicates that Torrontés Riojano and Torrontés Sanjuanino are likely natural offspring of Muscat of Alexandria and Criolla Chica. Torrontés Mendocino also appears to involve Muscat of Alexandria, although its other parent has not been identified.

That history does **not** mean Torrontés is simply another name for Moscato or Muscat. The varieties are related in their story, but they should not be collapsed into one identity.

You may also see the accent omitted in English retail systems: **Torrontés** and **Torrontes** refer to the same written name. A practical pronunciation is **tor-ron-TESS**, with the emphasis on the final syllable.

## Where is Torrontés grown?

Argentina is Torrontés’ essential modern context. It is especially associated with:

- **Salta**, including Cafayate, where high-altitude Torrontés Riojano is a familiar regional reference;
- **La Rioja**, an important historic and production context;
- **San Juan**, particularly relevant to Torrontés Sanjuanino;
- **Mendoza**, a major Argentine wine province with significant Torrontés plantings.

These are production associations, not a guarantee that every bottle from one province will taste the same. The grape’s style can shift with altitude, climate, ripeness and winemaking.

Australian learners are more likely to encounter imported Argentine Torrontés than a widely established Australian category. However, Australian production is also documented, including small-scale planting and wine production in Victoria’s Pyrenees area. There is no current national planting statistic in the approved evidence, so it is more accurate to describe Torrontés as a niche Australian presence than as a major local variety.

## How should you serve Torrontés?

Serve a still, dry Torrontés chilled—around **8–10°C** is a useful starting point. Avoid serving it extremely cold, because excessive chilling can mute the floral perfume that makes the wine distinctive.

A normal white-wine glass is all you need. Give the wine enough room to swirl gently and release its aromas; specialist glassware is unnecessary.

Torrontés can be a useful match for aromatic, lightly spiced foods. Starting points include:

- grilled prawns or other seafood;
- prawn salad with herbs;
- Vietnamese or Thai-style dishes;
- lightly spiced chicken;
- fresh salads with aromatic herbs.

Treat these as suggestions rather than rules. A sweeter, richer or more textured Torrontés may work differently from a lean, dry example. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is Torrontés the same as Muscat?

No. Torrontés is not simply another name for Muscat or Moscato. Some Argentine Torrontés varieties have Muscat of Alexandria in their genetic parentage, but they remain distinct varieties.

### Is Torrontés always floral?

It is generally known for pronounced floral and fruit aromas, but the intensity varies with variety, site, ripeness and winemaking.

### Is Torrontés only from Argentina?

No. Argentina is its central modern context, but Torrontés is also grown and made in Australia on a small documented scale. The name can also be used in other wine contexts, so it should not automatically be assumed to mean the same grape everywhere.

### What should I look for on the label?

Look for **Torrontés** or **Torrontes**. If a specific type is stated, it may say Torrontés Riojano, Torrontés Sanjuanino or Torrontés Mendocino. An unqualified Argentine Torrontés label will usually point to the Riojano type.

### What kind of wine is Torrontés?

It is most commonly a dry, still, aromatic white wine, but individual wines can vary in sweetness, body, texture and production style.
