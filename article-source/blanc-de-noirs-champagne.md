# Blanc de Noirs Champagne

## Highlights

- Blanc de Noirs means a **white wine made from dark-skinned grapes**.
- In Champagne, those grapes are principally **Pinot Noir and Meunier**.
- It is not red Champagne: the grape juice is pale, and careful pressing limits colour extraction.
- The term does not tell you whether the wine is sweet, vintage, powerful, dark in colour or expensive.
- Blanc de Noirs and traditional method describe different things: one describes grape composition, the other describes sparkling-wine production.
- Style can vary considerably depending on the blend, source, ageing, reserve wines, dosage and producer decisions.
- Champagne is a protected French geographic name. Australian sparkling wine, even when made by the traditional method, is not Champagne.

## Quick answer: what is Blanc de Noirs Champagne?

Blanc de Noirs Champagne is **white Champagne made exclusively from dark-skinned grapes**. In Champagne, the relevant varieties are principally Pinot Noir and Meunier, used alone or together.

The surprising part is that dark grape skins do not automatically make red wine. The flesh and juice inside these grapes are pale. When the grapes are handled carefully and pressed with limited skin contact, the winemaker can separate pale juice from the dark skins and ferment it as a white base wine.

The name **Blanc de Noirs** answers one question: *what colour were the grape skins?* It does not answer questions about sweetness, vintage, quality, colour intensity or production method.

## How can dark grapes make a white wine?

Colour is extracted mainly when juice spends time in contact with dark grape skins. For Blanc de Noirs Champagne, grapes are brought to the press intact and pressed progressively and gently within the appellation framework. Limiting contact between juice and skins helps avoid extracting significant colour.

Champagne pressing also separates juice fractions, including the **cuvée** and **taille**, which are vinified separately. These terms describe different portions of the press juice; they do not mean that every Blanc de Noirs Champagne tastes the same.

The exact choices can vary between wines. What matters for the basic explanation is simple: **dark skins are pressed carefully, pale juice is separated, and the juice is made into white Champagne**.

## Which grapes are used?

Champagne Blanc de Noirs is made from dark-skinned grapes, principally:

- **Pinot Noir**, which may contribute body, structure, power and fruit weight.
- **Meunier**, which may contribute fruitiness, roundness and an immediately approachable character.

These are broad tendencies, not guarantees. A Blanc de Noirs may contain Pinot Noir, Meunier or both. The varieties may also behave differently depending on where they are grown, the harvest conditions and how the wine is made.

That is why it is misleading to describe Meunier as inferior or merely a blending grape. It can make an important contribution to a blend, and the balance between Pinot Noir and Meunier is one of the things that can shape the finished wine. Our guides to [Pinot Noir](/what-is-pinot-noir/) and [Pinot Meunier](/pinot-meunier/) provide more variety context.

## What does Blanc de Noirs taste like?

Blanc de Noirs Champagne is often associated with structure, density, vinosity and a sense of substance. It may show red-fruit, citrus, floral, spice or broader fruit characters.

Treat those descriptions as a starting point rather than a tasting promise. Not every Blanc de Noirs is full-bodied, dark-coloured, tannic, red-fruited or powerful. Champagne’s bubbles and acidity can make even a structured wine feel energetic and fresh.

Style may change with:

- the proportions of Pinot Noir and Meunier;
- vineyard and harvest sources;
- pressing and juice-fraction selection;
- vintage or non-vintage blending;
- the proportion of reserve wines;
- time ageing on lees;
- dosage, meaning the final sugar adjustment;
- the producer’s blending and winemaking choices.

Reserve wines, for example, can add developed, rounded or structured flavours, while younger wines may contribute freshness and liveliness.

## Blanc de Noirs, Blanc de Blancs and rosé

These names are easy to confuse, but they describe different categories.

**Blanc de Noirs** means white Champagne made exclusively from dark-skinned grapes, principally Pinot Noir and/or Meunier.

**Blanc de Blancs** means Champagne made exclusively from white-skinned grapes, principally Chardonnay. Neither term automatically specifies sweetness, age, quality or vintage status.

**Rosé Champagne** is a different colour category. It can be made by blending or by allowing more colour to be extracted from dark grapes through skin contact, depending on the approved production approach. Blanc de Noirs, by contrast, is a white Champagne category made from dark grapes with colour extraction limited.

For broader Champagne categories, see [Champagne styles and classifications](/champagne-styles-and-classifications/).

## Blanc de Noirs versus traditional method

“Blanc de Noirs” and “traditional method” are not interchangeable.

- **Blanc de Noirs** describes the colour of the grape skins used.
- **Traditional method** describes a way of producing sparkling wine.

Champagne is made within a regulated sparkling-wine system associated with the traditional method. But traditional-method sparkling wines are also made in Australia and elsewhere. A sparkling wine can therefore be traditional-method without being Blanc de Noirs, and it can be Blanc de Noirs-style without being Champagne.

Our guide to [traditional-method sparkling wine](/traditional-method-sparkling-wine/) explains that distinction in more detail.

## How to read a Blanc de Noirs label

When looking at a bottle, keep these terms separate:

- **Champagne** identifies the protected French geographic origin.
- **Blanc de Noirs** indicates white Champagne made from dark-skinned grapes.
- **Pinot Noir and/or Meunier** may identify the varieties used.
- **Brut, Extra Brut or Brut Nature** describes a sweetness category associated with dosage.
- **A vintage year** indicates a vintage wine; its presence is separate from Blanc de Noirs status.
- **Producer and cuvée details** may help explain the individual wine, but they do not redefine Blanc de Noirs.

In other words, Blanc de Noirs does not mean Brut, does not mean vintage and does not mean a particular level of quality. For more label terminology, see [how to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/).

## What does Champagne mean in Australia?

For Australian drinkers, the most important distinction is geographic. **Champagne is a protected name for wine from the Champagne region in France and made under its appellation rules.**

An Australian sparkling wine may be made from Pinot Noir and Meunier. It may also be made using the traditional method. It is still not Champagne, because it does not come from the Champagne appellation.

On an Australian label, “traditional method” is the relevant production description where accurate. “Champagne method” and “methode champenoise” should not be used as generic descriptions for Australian-origin wine.

You can learn more about the place and appellation in our guide to [Champagne](/champagne/).

## Serving and enjoying it

Serve Blanc de Noirs Champagne chilled, but avoid making it excessively icy. Very cold temperatures can make aromas and texture harder to notice.

Its structure and fruit weight may make it a comfortable match for richer savoury food, but there is no single correct pairing. It can be enjoyed with a meal, as an aperitif or simply as a way to explore how grape variety and pressing choices affect sparkling wine.

The most useful thing to remember is this: **Blanc de Noirs tells you where the colour began—not exactly how the finished wine will taste.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Blanc de Noirs Champagne red?

No. It is white Champagne made from dark-skinned grapes. Careful pressing and limited skin contact help keep the juice pale.

### Is Blanc de Noirs always made from Pinot Noir?

No. It may be made from Pinot Noir, Meunier or both.

### Is Blanc de Noirs Champagne sweet?

Not necessarily. Sweetness is described separately through dosage categories such as Brut, Extra Brut or Brut Nature.

### Is Blanc de Noirs always vintage?

No. It can be vintage or non-vintage. Vintage status is a separate label consideration.

### Can Australian sparkling wine be called Champagne?

No. Champagne is a protected French geographic name. Australian sparkling wine can be made by the traditional method, but it is not Champagne.
