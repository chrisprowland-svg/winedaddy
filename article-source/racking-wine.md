# What Is Racking Wine? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Racking is the controlled transfer of wine from one vessel to another.
- It usually leaves settled sediment or lees behind.
- “Racked off the lees” means the wine has been moved away from settled solids.
- Racking can help clarify wine and manage lees contact.
- It can also expose wine to oxygen, so timing and handling matter.
- Racking is not the same as filtering, fining, lees stirring or decanting.
- Not every wine needs to be racked in the same way or on the same schedule.

## Quick answer: what is racking wine?

**Racking is the controlled transfer of wine from one vessel to another, usually after sediment or lees have settled, so that some of the clearer wine can be separated from the solids.**

In a winery, wine may be moved from a tank, barrel or another vessel while heavier material remains behind. That material can include yeast sediment left after fermentation, grape solids and other particles that have settled over time.

You may see the phrase **“racked off the lees”** in a winemaking description or technical note. It means the wine was transferred away from settled lees. The phrase does not automatically mean the lees were faulty or that the wine became better. Winemakers may remove, retain or stir lees depending on the wine, the vessel and the result they want.

Racking is both a separation operation and a way of moving wine between vessels. It can support clarification and influence how the wine develops during maturation. However, transferring wine can also create an opportunity for oxygen exposure. That is a consideration to manage, not proof that the wine has oxidised.

## Why do winemakers rack wine?

### To separate wine from heavier solids

After fermentation or settling, solids can collect at the bottom of a vessel. Some may be unwanted in a particular wine or production stage. Racking allows the clearer wine to be moved elsewhere while leaving some of this sediment behind.

The material may be called **gross lees** when it is relatively heavy or coarse. It can include grape material and substantial fermentation residue. The exact composition varies with the fruit, processing and stage of winemaking.

### To help clarify the wine

Clarification means reducing suspended material so the wine becomes clearer. Racking can contribute to clarification when solids have already settled, but it is only one possible approach. Filtering and fining are separate processes that can also be used for clarification.

Racking does not necessarily remove every particle. Its effectiveness depends on how well the solids have settled and how carefully the wine is transferred.

### To manage lees contact

Lees are not simply waste. Selected lees contact may be deliberate because lees can influence a wine’s texture, aroma and development. Fine lees are generally finer, often more yeast-rich sediment left after heavier material has settled, although these terms are broad cellar descriptions rather than rigid universal categories.

Racking can reduce or end contact with a particular layer of lees. In another situation, a winemaker may choose to retain the wine on selected fine lees or stir them back into suspension. The decision depends on the condition of the lees and the desired style.

### To move wine between vessels

Wine may need to move from one production or maturation vessel to another. Racking can achieve that movement while also leaving settled material behind. The operation is therefore part of both cellar organisation and wine management.

## Racking and oxygen exposure

Moving wine can expose it to oxygen. The amount depends on the transfer system, the vessel, the headspace and the way the wine is handled.

Oxygen is not automatically a problem. In some situations, controlled oxygen exposure is part of winemaking. In others, excess exposure can contribute to oxidation and unwanted changes. This is why winemakers consider the timing and manner of a racking operation.

The useful beginner’s distinction is:

> Racking can expose wine to oxygen, but racking is not the same thing as oxidation.

A wine that has been racked has not necessarily been damaged. Nor does racking guarantee freshness or prevent oxidation. The result depends on the wine and the conditions of the transfer.

## Is racking used for every wine?

No. Racking is not a universal rule with one required schedule.

Australian winemakers use terms such as “racking” and “racking off the lees” in cellar and technical descriptions, but practice is not uniform. Choices can vary according to:

- the amount and type of sediment;
- the wine’s stage of production;
- the vessel being used;
- the desired lees contact;
- the wine’s condition;
- oxygen-management priorities.

Red, white and rosé wines may be handled differently, but broad rules such as “all red wines are racked more often” are not supported here. The same applies to claims about particular grape varieties, regions or wineries.

## Racking versus similar winemaking terms

### Racking versus filtering

**Racking** relies on settled solids and a controlled transfer between vessels.

**Filtering** passes wine through a filtration system to remove particles, and sometimes other material, to a selected degree. Filtering can be used alongside racking, but the two processes are not interchangeable.

### Racking versus fining

**Fining** uses an added material that interacts with selected compounds or particles, helping them settle or be removed.

Racking itself is a physical vessel transfer. It does not inherently require a fining material.

### Racking versus lees stirring

**Lees stirring** deliberately resuspends settled lees so they come back into contact with the wine.

Racking generally moves clearer wine away from settled lees. They are different management choices, although a winemaker may use both at different stages.

### Racking versus decanting

**Decanting** is usually a service-stage action: finished bottled wine is poured into another container, often to separate bottle sediment or allow air contact.

Racking is a production-stage cellar operation. A bottle being decanted at dinner has not been “racked” in the winemaking sense.

## What does “racked off the lees” tell you?

It tells you that the wine was transferred away from settled sediment, often yeast-derived lees after fermentation. It may suggest a clarification or maturation decision, but it does not tell you that the wine is automatically lighter, fresher or higher quality.

Lees can have useful effects, and they can also create problems in particular conditions. The phrase is best understood as a description of cellar handling rather than a promise about flavour.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does racking improve wine?

It can help with separation, clarification or lees management, but it does not automatically improve every wine. The result depends on the wine, the sediment and the winemaker’s purpose.

### Does racking cause oxidation?

Not necessarily. Racking can create an opportunity for oxygen exposure, but the amount of exposure and its consequences depend on the transfer and cellar conditions.

### Are lees bad for wine?

No. Lees can be deliberately retained for their potential contribution to texture and aroma. Other lees, or excessive contact in particular circumstances, may be undesirable.

### How often should wine be racked?

There is no universal schedule. Racking frequency varies with the wine, its sediment, vessel, production objective and oxygen-management needs.

### Is racking the same as decanting?

No. Racking happens during wine production, while decanting usually happens when serving a finished bottle.
