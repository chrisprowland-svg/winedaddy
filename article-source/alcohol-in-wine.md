# Alcohol in wine: what you need to know

## Highlights

- Wine alcohol is principally **ethanol**, made when yeast converts grape sugar during fermentation.
- **ABV** tells you the concentration of alcohol, not the total amount in your glass.
- Alcohol is not the same as sweetness, quality or “dryness”.
- Alcohol can add warmth and weight, but acidity, tannin, sugar and serving temperature affect how it feels.
- A larger pour contains more alcohol, even when the ABV stays the same.
- In Australia, the label’s **standard-drink** statement helps show how much pure alcohol the package contains.

## Quick answer

The alcohol in wine is mainly ethanol. It forms when yeast converts fermentable grape sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide during alcoholic fermentation. You can read a short introduction to that process in [What is fermentation?](/what-is-fermentation/) or explore the more specific explanation in [Alcoholic fermentation in wine](/alcoholic-fermentation/).

Wine labels usually express alcohol as **alcohol by volume**, or **ABV**. A wine labelled 13% ABV contains approximately 13 mL of ethanol per 100 mL of wine. That is a measure of concentration—not the total amount of alcohol you will drink.

Total alcohol depends on both the wine’s ABV and the size of the serving. A 150 mL glass at 13% ABV contains less alcohol than a 250 mL glass at the same strength. This is why a standard restaurant pour is not automatically one Australian standard drink.

ABV also does not tell you whether a wine is sweet, good quality or dry. “Dry” concerns sweetness, while ABV concerns alcohol concentration. A dry wine can have relatively high or low alcohol. For the separate meaning of dry wine, see [What does dry wine mean?](/dry-wine-meaning/).

## How alcohol gets into wine

Grapes contain sugars, principally glucose and fructose. During alcoholic fermentation, yeast uses those sugars and produces ethanol and carbon dioxide. The more fermentable sugar available, and the further fermentation proceeds, the greater the potential alcohol—although the final result depends on several decisions and conditions.

Grape maturity matters because sugar concentration generally changes as grapes ripen. But maturity is not a simple synonym for high alcohol or better wine. Winemakers also consider acidity, flavour and phenolic development.

Yeast health, yeast type, temperature, nutrients and other fermentation conditions can influence how efficiently fermentation proceeds. If fermentation stops before all the fermentable sugar is converted, the wine may retain more sugar and finish with less alcohol than a complete fermentation would produce.

Fortification is another route. Fortified wine has grape spirit, brandy or both added to it, increasing its alcohol concentration. Blending wines with different ABVs can also change the final figure. Other adjustments may be possible in some products, but they are not universal winemaking steps.

## What alcohol feels like in wine

Alcohol can contribute a warming or “hot” sensation, particularly when it stands out from the rest of the wine. It may also contribute to perceived body, weight and texture. These effects are real, but they are not a simple scale where more alcohol always means a fuller wine.

Acidity can make a wine feel fresher and can counterbalance weight or sweetness. Tannin and other phenolic compounds add structure, bitterness or astringency. Residual sugar can add sweetness and body. Glycerol, polysaccharides, pH, flavour concentration and serving temperature can also change how alcohol is perceived.

That is why two wines with the same ABV can feel quite different. One may seem warm and broad; another may seem fresh and lighter because its acidity and other components are more prominent. Alcohol can affect balance, but balance is an interaction among several parts of the wine—not a score determined by the ABV number.

Alcohol is not a reliable shortcut to quality, grape variety, region or ageability. A higher number on the label is not automatically a virtue or a fault.

## Alcohol is not sweetness

A wine’s alcohol level and sweetness are separate attributes. Sweetness is chiefly related to residual sugar and how the wine tastes, while ABV measures ethanol concentration.

This is also why a fruity wine may taste sweet even when it is technically dry, and why a wine with noticeable alcohol is not necessarily sweet. The existing [What is sweetness in wine?](/what-is-sweetness-in-wine/) guide explores that distinction in more detail.

If alcohol feels warm, that sensation can sometimes be mistaken for sweetness or richness. Tasting the wine alongside its acidity, tannin and texture gives you a more accurate impression. [How to taste wine](/how-to-taste-wine/) can help you separate those sensations.

## Reading an Australian wine label

Australian packaged alcoholic beverages generally show two useful pieces of information:

- **Alcohol content**, such as `13.0% alc/vol`; and
- **Standard drinks per package**, which indicates the amount of pure alcohol in the container.

For beverages above 1.15% ABV, alcohol content is expressed as a percentage by volume or equivalent wording. Beverages above 0.5% ABV also require a standard-drink statement under the relevant Australian food-labelling rules.

Imported wine may include additional information from its country of origin, so labels do not always look identical. For wine sold in Australia, use the Australian-compliant information—especially the declared standard drinks—when it is provided. [How to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/) explains other common label details.

### A worked example

Consider **150 mL of wine at 13% ABV**:

- 150 mL × 0.13 = 19.5 mL of ethanol
- 19.5 mL of ethanol is approximately 15.4 g
- 15.4 g ÷ 10 g = approximately **1.5 Australian standard drinks**

In Australia, one standard drink contains 10 g of pure alcohol. The example shows why both ABV and volume matter. A larger pour of the same wine contains more alcohol, while a smaller pour of a stronger wine may contain a similar amount.

Serving sizes vary at home, in restaurants and at tastings. For packaged wine, use the label’s declared standard-drink figure rather than assuming a glass equals one standard drink.

## A final practical note

This article explains wine and labels; it does not estimate intoxication, blood-alcohol concentration, driving status or how alcohol affects an individual. For health, legal or personal consumption questions, use current guidance from the Australian Government or [healthdirect](https://www.healthdirect.gov.au/know-your-standard-drinks-infographic).
