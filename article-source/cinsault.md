# What Is Cinsault? A Beginner’s Guide

## Cinsault in brief

Cinsault is a red wine-grape variety. It is a black-skinned *Vitis vinifera* grape that can produce soft, fruity and aromatic red wines, as well as rosé. It is often relatively low in tannin, but that does not mean every Cinsault is light-bodied or simple.

The variety is also written **Cinsaut**. In Australia, Wine Australia’s January 2024 OIV-derived list uses **Cinsaut** as the prime name and lists **Oeillade** and **Blue Imperial** as synonyms. **Cinsault** is the familiar French and international spelling. These names refer to the same grape variety rather than separate wine styles.

## Highlights

- Cinsault is a red grape variety, not a region or a wine style.
- Australian sources commonly use the spelling **Cinsaut**, while **Cinsault** remains widely used internationally.
- It is generally associated with Provence and the south of France.
- In Australia, Cinsaut has a long history but is planted on a limited scale, mainly in South Australia.
- Its wines are often soft, fruity, aromatic and relatively low in tannin.
- Yield, site, vine age and winemaking can make the wine lighter and delicate or more balanced and concentrated.
- Cinsaut is used for red wines, blends and rosé.
- A wine can smell of strawberries or cherries and still be dry.

## What does Cinsault taste like?

A typical Cinsault may be light- to medium-bodied, with a soft texture, fresh fruit character and relatively gentle tannins. Strawberry and cherry notes can appear, particularly in wines made from low-yielding, dry-farmed bush vines. Some examples may also show perfume or spice.

These are broad tendencies, not a tasting guarantee. A grape variety provides a starting point, but the finished wine also reflects climate, soil, crop level, vine age, harvest decisions and winemaking. A high-yielding vineyard may produce a less concentrated wine, while carefully managed, low-yielding vines can produce more balanced and aromatic results.

## Where does Cinsault come from?

Cinsault is generally associated with southern France. Plantgrape, a French institutional grape-variety resource, says the variety appears to originate from Provence. The wording matters: this is a cautious origin association, not an absolute claim that its genetic history is fully settled.

Cinsault has also been grown in places including southern France, Morocco, Algeria, South Africa, Italy, Chile, Lebanon and Australia. Its ability to cope with hot, dry conditions helps explain why it has a place in a range of warm wine-growing areas.

In Australia, the Australian Wine Research Institute describes Cinsaut as a long-established but limited-scale variety, with the main concentration in South Australia. The AWRI source reports an approximate historical figure of 60 hectares and at least 20 producers, but this should not be treated as a current national planting statistic.

## Why can Cinsault taste so different from one bottle to another?

Cinsault is fertile and productive, meaning the vines can carry substantial crops. In fertile conditions, excessive yields may reduce concentration and quality. Drier, poorer sites and lower yields can encourage a more supple, fruity and balanced style.

Vine age can also be part of the story. AWRI describes low-yielding, dry-farmed bush-vine examples as capable of producing medium-bodied wines with balance and delicate perfume. That does not mean old vines automatically make better wine. It means that vine age, farming method and yield can combine to create a different expression of the variety.

The final wine may also be a blend. In southern France and Australia, Cinsaut has commonly been used with varieties including Carignan and Grenache. In a blend, the finished character belongs to the combination rather than to Cinsaut alone.

For a beginner, the useful lesson is that “Cinsault” on a label does not prescribe one exact flavour profile. It identifies the grape, while the vineyard and winery help determine the style. ## Is Cinsault used for rosé?

Yes. Cinsaut can be used to make rosé as well as red wine. Its fruit character and relatively soft tannin can suit a fresh, lighter style, although the final result depends on how the grapes are grown and made.

It is also possible to find Cinsaut as a varietal wine, meaning the grape is presented on its own, or as part of a blend. The available evidence does not establish how common each format is across the Australian market, so it is better to treat these as possible styles rather than fixed expectations.

## Is Cinsault sweet?

Not necessarily. Cinsault can smell and taste fruity without being sweet.

A dry wine may have aromas or flavours that remind you of strawberries, cherries or other ripe fruit. Sweetness refers to sugar in the wine, while fruitiness describes the way the wine smells or tastes. A fruity Cinsault can therefore be completely dry.

## How should you approach a Cinsault?

When tasting one, look first for texture rather than trying to identify a single “correct” flavour. Does it feel soft or firm? Is the fruit delicate or concentrated? Does the wine seem light- or medium-bodied? Do the aromas suggest fresh berries, perfume or spice?

A Cinsault red may be a useful choice when you want to explore a red wine with gentler tannins and expressive fruit, but do not assume every bottle will be light or easy-drinking. A blend may taste quite different from a varietal bottling, and a wine from low-yielding bush vines may have more depth and structure.

## Cinsault FAQs

### Is Cinsault a red wine grape?

Yes. Cinsault is a black-skinned red wine-grape variety. It can be used for red wines, blends and rosé.

### Is Cinsault the same as Cinsaut?

Cinsaut and Cinsault are spelling variants used in different wine contexts. Wine Australia uses Cinsaut as the Australian prime name, while Cinsault is common in France and international writing.

### Is Cinsault always light-bodied?

No. It is often described as light-bodied, soft and low in tannin, but lower yields, dry-farmed bush vines, site and winemaking can produce a more balanced, medium-bodied wine.

### Is Cinsault a sweet wine?

No variety guarantees sweetness. Cinsault may have fruity aromas while the wine itself is dry.

### Is Cinsault grown in Australia?

Yes. It has a long history in Australia, although AWRI evidence describes it as a limited-scale variety, concentrated mainly in South Australia. The available planting estimate is historical and should not be read as a current national figure.
