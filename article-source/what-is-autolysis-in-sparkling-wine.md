# What is autolysis in sparkling wine?

## Highlights

- Autolysis is the gradual self-breakdown of dead yeast cells.
- In sparkling wine, it usually develops while the wine rests on yeast lees.
- It can contribute to bready, biscuity, brioche-like, savoury or nutty aromas, as well as a creamier texture.
- Autolysis is not the same as the second fermentation that creates the bubbles.
- More time on lees can create more opportunity for autolytic development, but it does not guarantee one particular flavour.
- Traditional-method sparkling wine is especially associated with this process, including some Australian sparkling wines.

## Quick answer

Autolysis is the natural self-breakdown of spent yeast cells after fermentation.

In traditional-method sparkling wine, a second fermentation takes place in the bottle. Once that fermentation is complete, the inactive yeast cells remain as sediment, known as lees. The wine may then age in contact with these lees. Gradually, the yeast cells break down and release some of their components into the wine.

Those released materials can influence the wine’s aroma, flavour, texture, foam and overall impression. They may contribute to descriptions such as bread, toast, biscuit, brioche, dough, savouriness or nuttiness. However, autolysis is only one influence among many. The grape varieties, base wine, yeast strain, ageing conditions, dosage and handling all help shape the finished sparkling wine.

A useful way to remember it is: **secondary fermentation creates the spent yeast; lees ageing gives autolysis time to develop.**

## How does autolysis happen?

Yeast is used to ferment sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. In traditional-method sparkling wine, this additional fermentation happens in a sealed bottle, allowing the carbon dioxide to dissolve into the wine.

After fermentation, many of the yeast cells are no longer active. They settle as lees. While the wine remains in contact with them, the cells gradually lose their structure and break down. Cell-wall and internal materials are released into the surrounding wine.

This is a slow process rather than a sudden change. The released materials can interact with the wine and affect its texture, aroma and behaviour of the foam. The result may be a wine that feels more rounded, creamy or full, although autolysis does not produce exactly the same result in every bottle.

## Where does it fit in traditional-method sparkling wine?

The broad sequence looks like this:

1. A still base wine is prepared.
2. A second fermentation takes place in the bottle.
3. Spent yeast remains in the bottle as lees.
4. The wine ages in contact with those lees.
5. Autolysis gradually develops during that period.
6. Riddling gathers the sediment in the neck of the bottle.
7. Disgorgement removes the sediment.

The process diagram used for traditional-method sparkling wine, **VIS-033**, can help show this broader sequence. It should not be read as a diagram of autolysis itself: the cell-level breakdown happens during the lees-ageing stage and is not something a standard production flowchart can show in detail.

“Traditional method” is the useful general term for sparkling wine that undergoes its second fermentation in the bottle in which it is ultimately sold. Regional names and legal descriptions can differ, so Champagne’s rules should not automatically be applied to every traditional-method wine.

## What does autolysis taste or smell like?

Autolysis may be associated with aromas and flavours described as:

- bready or doughy;
- biscuity;
- brioche-like;
- toasty;
- yeasty;
- savoury;
- nutty;
- honeyed; or
- creamy or buttery.

It can also contribute to a fuller, smoother mouthfeel and influence the persistence or stability of the foam.

These are possibilities, not a checklist. A sparkling wine can spend time on lees and still show prominent fruit or floral characters. Its final profile depends on the base wine, grape variety, yeast strain, pressure, temperature, oxygen exposure, ageing time and winemaking choices.

Autolysis also does not explain every bready or brioche note. Other parts of production and ageing can contribute to the way a wine smells and tastes.

## Does longer lees ageing always mean more autolytic flavour?

Not necessarily.

Longer lees ageing gives autolysis more opportunity to develop, and extended ageing may increase the influence of yeast-derived materials. But time alone does not determine the result. Two wines aged for a similar period can taste quite different because their yeast, base wine and cellar conditions differ.

Nor does a longer-aged wine have to taste dramatically more bready. It may show greater texture, savoury complexity or integration instead. Think of lees ageing as creating an opportunity for change, not setting a flavour timer.

## Autolysis is not the same as these processes

**Secondary fermentation** is the fermentation that produces additional alcohol and carbon dioxide. It happens before the spent yeast undergoes autolysis.

**Oxidation** is a separate chemical process involving oxygen or oxidising conditions. A wine can experience oxidation during ageing, but oxidation is not yeast-cell breakdown.

**Lees stirring** means agitating the sediment to bring it back into contact with the wine. It is a handling technique, not autolysis itself.

**Riddling** involves rotating and tilting the bottle so the sediment collects in the neck.

**Disgorgement** removes that collected sediment. Neither riddling nor disgorgement causes autolysis, although both occur in the wider traditional-method process.

## What does this mean on a wine label?

Terms such as **“aged on lees”**, **“yeast contact”** or **“bottle-aged”** provide clues about how the wine was made. They can suggest that the wine had an opportunity to develop influence from its lees.

They do not guarantee that the wine will taste strongly of brioche, biscuit or toast. Treat them as process information, not as a promise of one fixed flavour profile.

In Australia, some producers use traditional-method production and lees ageing, and Wine Australia identifies yeast autolysis as an important contributor to complex aromas in these wines. But not all Australian sparkling wine is made by the traditional method or aged in the same way. The country of origin alone cannot tell you whether autolysis played a major role.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is autolysis good for sparkling wine?

Autolysis can contribute desirable aroma, texture and foam characteristics, but it is not automatically “better”. Its effect depends on the wine and the balance of its other components.

### Is autolysis unique to Champagne?

No. Autolysis can occur wherever wine remains in contact with spent yeast. It is strongly associated with traditional-method sparkling wine from many regions, including Australia.

### Can you see autolysis in the bottle?

You may see lees as sediment before disgorgement, but autolysis itself is the gradual breakdown of the yeast cells and is not visible as a separate layer or event.

### Does bottle-aged always mean traditional method?

Not necessarily. “Bottle-aged” describes time spent ageing in a bottle, but the full production method should be confirmed rather than inferred from that phrase alone.

### What is the simplest way to explain autolysis?

It is **the slow self-breakdown of spent yeast cells while wine rests on its lees**.
