# What Is Fortification? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Fortification means adding distilled alcohol to wine, grape must or fermenting grape juice.
- In Australia, fortified wine is made with grape spirit, brandy or both.
- Adding spirit before or during fermentation can prevent or stop fermentation.
- Adding spirit after fermentation can produce a dry fortified wine.
- “Fortified” does not automatically mean sweet, red or oxidative.
- Fortification is different from chaptalisation, sweetening, blending and dealcoholisation.
- Australian fortified wines include several different styles, including tawny, vintage fortified wines, Apera, Topaque and Rutherglen Muscat.

## Quick answer: what does fortification mean in wine?

Fortification is the process of adding distilled alcohol to wine, grape must or fermenting grape juice. The added alcohol is usually a grape spirit, brandy or another permitted alcohol source, depending on the country and product.

The timing is crucial. If spirit is added before fermentation, it can prevent fermentation from starting. If it is added during fermentation, it can stop fermentation while some grape sugar remains. If it is added after fermentation, most of the sugar may already have been converted into alcohol, so the finished wine can be dry.

That is why fortified wine is not automatically sweet. Fortification tells you that alcohol has been added; it does not, by itself, tell you how much sugar the wine contains or what colour it is.

## What is added to fortified wine?

In Australian wine terminology, fortified wine is wine to which grape spirit, brandy or both have been added. Grape spirit is distilled from wine, winemaking by-products or fermented liquor made from dried grapes. Brandy is also a wine-derived spirit that must meet relevant production requirements.

The exact permitted alcohol sources and terminology can vary between jurisdictions. For an Australian learner, the useful starting point is simple: fortification adds a distilled, grape-derived alcohol to a wine or grape-based fermenting product.

Fortification raises the wine’s alcohol concentration. Australian production standards specify a maximum alcohol level for fortified wine, but that does not mean every fortified wine reaches that limit.

## When does fortification happen?

### Before fermentation

Adding spirit to grape must or juice before fermentation can prevent fermentation from beginning. This produces a grape-based alcoholic product in which the original grape sugars have not been fermented in the usual way.

A technical example is mistelle, made by adding grape spirit to grape must or juice to prevent fermentation. It is not necessary to remember the term to understand fortification: the important point is that spirit is added before yeast turns grape sugar into alcohol.

### During fermentation

Adding spirit while fermentation is underway can arrest, or stop, fermentation. Some grape sugar is then left as residual sugar — sugar remaining after fermentation has stopped.

This is one way to produce a sweet fortified wine, but the result is not determined by timing alone. Grape ripeness, fermentation progress, blending and any later permitted adjustments can also affect the final sweetness.

### After fermentation

If fermentation is allowed to finish before spirit is added, much of the fermentable sugar may already have been converted into alcohol. The fortified wine can therefore be dry.

This is the easiest misconception to correct: a fortified wine can be dry or sweet. The process is about adding alcohol, not automatically adding sweetness.

## How does fortification affect a wine?

The most direct effect is a higher alcohol concentration. Fortification can also change the wine’s overall composition and influence its balance, texture and maturation behaviour.

The result depends on several factors:

- when the spirit is added;
- how much fermentable sugar remains;
- the character of the base wine and added spirit;
- the wine’s acidity and other structural elements;
- whether the wine is later blended or matured in a particular way.

Some fortified styles undergo oxidative maturation, while others are made in fresher styles. Fortification itself does not mean that a wine is oxidative, and it does not guarantee improved quality or indefinite preservation.

## Fortification compared with other wine processes

These terms can sound similar, but they describe different actions.

### Fortification versus chaptalisation

Fortification adds distilled alcohol directly. Chaptalisation adds fermentable sugar before or during fermentation, so yeast can convert that sugar into more alcohol.

In short:

- **Fortification:** alcohol is added.
- **Chaptalisation:** sugar is added and may later become alcohol through fermentation.

### Fortification versus sweetening

Sweetening changes a wine’s sweetness by adding sugar, grape concentrate, sweet wine or another permitted component. A wine may be fortified and later sweetened, but sweetening is not the same process as adding spirit.

### Fortification versus blending

Blending combines wines or permitted wine components. It can alter alcohol, sweetness, colour, acidity or style, but combining components is not itself fortification.

### Fortification versus dealcoholisation

Dealcoholisation removes alcohol from wine, using techniques such as reverse osmosis or spinning-cone technology. Fortification and dealcoholisation both change alcohol levels, but they move in opposite directions.

## What does “fortified” mean in Australia?

Australian fortified wine is a broad category rather than one single style. It can include dry or sweet wines, red or white wines, and wines made for fresh or oxidative styles of maturation.

You may encounter terms such as:

- **Tawny**, an Australian quality-wine term associated with an aged fortified style.
- **Vintage fortified wine**, a protected Australian term used for a particular style category.
- **Apera**, a controlled Australian certification trade mark for qualifying Australian fortified wines.
- **Topaque**, another controlled Australian certification trade mark for qualifying Australian fortified wines.
- **Rutherglen Muscat**, an Australian fortified-wine example associated with the Rutherglen region.

Protected wine names matter. Terms such as Port and Sherry have protected geographical or traditional meanings, so they should not be treated as unrestricted generic names for Australian wines. Australian terminology may instead use terms such as Apera or appropriate style descriptions.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is fortified wine always sweet?

No. Fortified wine can be dry or sweet. Fortification raises alcohol; sweetness depends largely on fermentation timing and other production choices.

### Is fortified wine always red?

No. Fortified wines can be made in different colours and styles.

### Does fortification always stop fermentation?

No. Spirit added before fermentation can prevent it from starting, and spirit added during fermentation can stop it. Spirit added after fermentation does not need to stop an active fermentation.

### Is fortified wine the same as dessert wine?

Not necessarily. Some fortified wines are sweet and may be served as dessert wines, but “fortified” describes the addition of alcohol, not a guaranteed level of sweetness.

### Where does fortification fit into winemaking?

It is one possible intervention in the winemaking sequence. To see the wider sequence, start with [how wine is made](/how-wine-is-made/) and [what fermentation does in wine](/what-is-fermentation/).

The key idea is simple: fortification adds alcohol, and when that addition happens can change the wine that results.
