# What is dosage in sparkling wine?

## Highlights

- Dosage is a finishing addition made near the end of sparkling-wine production.
- In the traditional method, it is added after disgorgement.
- It commonly contains wine and sugar.
- It replaces liquid lost during disgorgement and helps shape the wine’s final balance and sweetness.
- Dosage is not the same as the secondary fermentation that creates bubbles.
- “Brut” and “extra brut” describe sweetness categories, but their precise rules depend on the relevant regulatory or appellation system.
- Low dosage is not automatically a sign of higher quality.

## Quick answer

Dosage is the liquid added to a sparkling wine shortly before the bottle is finally closed. In the traditional method, it follows **disgorgement**—the removal of the sediment left after the wine has aged on its lees.

The addition commonly consists of wine with dissolved sugar. It replaces liquid lost during disgorgement and helps determine the finished wine’s style, balance and sugar level. The amount is carefully chosen: a very dry sparkling wine may receive little or no added dosage sugar, while a sweeter style receives more.

Dosage does **not** create the bubbles. In traditional-method sparkling wine, the bubbles are created earlier by a second fermentation in the bottle.

## Where dosage fits in the process

Traditional-method sparkling wine undergoes a sequence of steps:

1. A base wine is made.
2. A second fermentation in the bottle creates alcohol and carbon dioxide.
3. The wine ages with yeast sediment, known as lees.
4. **Riddling** moves the sediment towards the bottle neck.
5. **Disgorgement** removes the sediment plug.
6. Dosage is added.
7. The bottle is finally closed with its sparkling-wine cork and secured.

A useful way to remember the order is:

> Riddling moves the sediment, disgorgement removes it, and dosage finishes the bottle.

The French terms **liqueur de dosage** and **liqueur d’expédition** are used for the finishing mixture or addition. “Liqueur” here does not mean a sweet spirit or a separate liqueur-style drink. It refers to the liquid added at this stage.

## What is in dosage?

The most familiar formulation is wine with sugar dissolved in it. The wine component helps the addition fit into the sparkling wine, while the sugar contributes to the finished wine’s final sugar level and texture.

Technical definitions can be broader. The International Organisation of Vine and Wine (OIV) recognises wine, grape must or a blend of wine and grape must, along with sugar and certain permitted additives. So “dosage is always simply sugar and wine” is a useful beginner’s shorthand, but not a complete technical definition.

The amount added is usually small and depends on the intended style. An example given for Champagne is approximately 1 centilitre for a 75-centilitre bottle of Champagne brut. That is an example, not a universal measure for every sparkling wine.

## Why is dosage added?

Dosage has two connected purposes.

First, disgorgement removes the sediment plug, along with some wine. The finishing addition restores the bottle to its intended volume before final closure.

Second, dosage helps the winemaker achieve the intended sensory style. Sugar can add roundness and soften the impression of sharp acidity, although the result depends on the whole wine. Acidity, alcohol, bubbles, fruit character, temperature and texture all influence how sweet or dry a sparkling wine seems.

That is why dosage should not be treated as a simple quality scale. Less dosage can suit a wine with plenty of freshness and structure, while a wine with different proportions may be more harmonious with a little more sugar. The best level is the one that works with the wine—not automatically the lowest possible level.

## Dosage and sparkling-wine sweetness terms

The sugar categories below are **Champagne classifications**, not a universal Australian legal table:

| Champagne term | Sugar classification |
|---|---:|
| Brut nature, pas dosé or dosage zéro | Less than 3 g/L, with no added dosage sugar |
| Extra brut | 0–6 g/L |
| Brut | Less than 12 g/L |
| Extra-dry | 12–17 g/L |
| Sec | 17–32 g/L |
| Demi-sec | 32–50 g/L |
| Doux | More than 50 g/L |

The categories refer to the sugar content of the finished wine, not simply the volume of dosage liquid added. A small amount of concentrated dosage can contribute sugar, and some sugar may also remain from fermentation.

This explains two common misunderstandings:

- **Brut does not mean sweet.** Champagne brut contains less than 12 grams of sugar per litre under the Comité Champagne classification, and its acidity and bubbles may make it taste quite dry.
- **Zero dosage does not necessarily mean zero sugar.** Under Champagne usage, brut nature means no added dosage sugar and less than 3 grams per litre in the finished wine.

## Dosage is not residual sugar

**Residual sugar** means sugar remaining in the finished wine after fermentation and any later additions. Dosage is a winemaking step: the addition made near the end of production.

The two are related but not identical. Dosage can contribute to the finished wine’s sugar level, but the final result also depends on sugar left after fermentation and other production details.

Nor does a numerical sugar level tell the entire sensory story. A sparkling wine with noticeable acidity can taste drier than its sugar figure suggests, while a wine with softer acidity may feel rounder.

## What does this mean for Australian sparkling wine?

Australian drinkers may see terms such as **brut** on local sparkling-wine labels. Wine Australia’s educator material describes brut-style Australian sparkling wine as having no more than 12 grams of residual sugar per litre.

However, Champagne’s appellation rules do not automatically govern Australian wine. Australian wine production and labelling operate under Australian requirements, and the sources available for this article do not establish a complete Australian legal table for every term, including brut nature and extra brut.

The safest approach is to understand the broad meaning, then treat the exact category according to the regulatory or appellation system that applies to that bottle. “Brut” is generally a dry-style signal, but it does not have one identical legal meaning everywhere.

## Does every sparkling wine receive dosage?

No. Sparkling wines are made by different methods, and dosage is not carried out identically in every one.

The term is most strongly associated with the traditional method, where the addition follows disgorgement. OIV guidance also recognises dosage additions in certain tank-based procedures, after fermentation and before bottling. Some wines may receive no added dosage sugar, while others may use a different finishing procedure.

The important point is not to assume that every sparkling wine has followed the same bottle-based sequence.

## The takeaway

Dosage is the final finishing addition made before a sparkling wine is closed. In traditional-method wine, it comes after disgorgement, commonly contains wine and sugar, replaces lost liquid and helps establish the finished wine’s balance and sweetness.

It is neither the source of the bubbles nor a straightforward measure of quality. A low-dosage sparkling wine can be wonderfully precise, but the right dosage level is the one that brings the entire wine into balance.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is dosage the same as adding sugar?

Not exactly. Dosage is the finishing addition, which commonly contains wine and sugar. The term describes the production step, while the sugar is one possible component of the addition.

### Does dosage make sparkling wine sweeter?

It can contribute to the finished wine’s sugar level, but perceived sweetness also depends on acidity, alcohol, carbonation, temperature and texture.

### Is brut nature the driest sparkling wine?

Under Champagne terminology, brut nature, pas dosé or dosage zéro is the driest listed category and contains no added dosage sugar, with less than 3 g/L sugar in the finished wine. Other jurisdictions may use different rules or terminology.

### Is more dosage a sign of poorer winemaking?

No. Dosage is a stylistic and balancing decision, not a quality ranking.
