# Chianti Classico: A Beginner’s Guide to Tuscany’s Sangiovese Red

## Highlights

- Chianti Classico is a legally defined wine appellation in Tuscany, Italy.
- It is separate from the broader Chianti DOCG.
- Sangiovese must make up most of the blend.
- The wines are usually dry reds with noticeable acidity and tannin, but style varies.
- Annata, Riserva and Gran Selezione are regulated categories, not simple quality rankings.
- The Gallo Nero, or Black Rooster, is a useful label clue for Chianti Classico.
- Place, vintage, producer and winemaking choices all affect the wine.
- Chianti Classico’s acidity makes it a natural partner for many savoury foods, including tomato-based pasta and pizza.

## Quick answer: what is Chianti Classico?

Chianti Classico is a protected red-wine appellation in Tuscany, central Italy, located broadly between Florence and Siena. It is classified as DOCG—*Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*—and is also protected within the European PDO system.

The important point is that **Chianti Classico is not simply another name for Chianti**. They are separate appellations with different defined production areas and rules. Chianti is the broader denomination; Chianti Classico is a distinct area with its own regulations.

The wines are centred on Sangiovese, Italy’s important red grape. In the glass, many show fresh acidity, tannin, red or dark fruit and floral, savoury or earthy notes. That is a useful starting point, not a promise. Chianti Classico includes varied sites, elevations, soils, vintages and winemaking approaches, so bottles can differ considerably.

## Where is Chianti Classico?

Chianti Classico is in Tuscany, within Italy. Florence is the most useful major-city reference point for many Australian readers, while Siena is an important reference for the southern part of the appellation. The area generally lies south of Florence and north of Siena.

Familiar localities associated with the defined area include Greve in Chianti, San Casciano in Val di Pesa, Barberino Tavarnelle, Castellina in Chianti, Radda in Chianti, Gaiole in Chianti and Castelnuovo Berardenga. Relevant parts of Poggibonsi are also included.

These towns help you picture the region, but a town name is not automatically interchangeable with the Chianti Classico appellation. The legal boundary is more precise than a simple list of localities.

From central Florence, northern and central parts of Chianti Classico are often roughly 30–60 minutes by car. From Siena, many southern and central areas may be around 20–45 minutes. Treat those figures as broad orientation only: the exact locality, route, traffic and starting point make a difference.

A registered map such as VIS-024 may help show the relationship between Italy, Tuscany, Florence, Siena and the approximate appellation area. It should be treated as an orientation map rather than a substitute for the legal boundary.

## Chianti versus Chianti Classico

On a bottle, the word **Classico** matters. “Chianti Classico” and “Chianti” are separate protected names, not interchangeable descriptions.

The broader Chianti DOCG covers a much larger area across central Tuscany, including parts of several provinces. Chianti Classico has its own delimited territory and production specification.

You may also encounter other separate Tuscan appellations, such as Brunello di Montalcino and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano. They are not subdivisions of Chianti Classico. Tuscany itself is the broader Italian region, not one single wine style.

## What grapes are used?

Sangiovese is the defining grape. Under the consolidated rules dated 22 June 2023:

- Standard Chianti Classico contains 80–100% Sangiovese.
- Chianti Classico Riserva also contains 80–100% Sangiovese.
- Gran Selezione contains 90–100% Sangiovese under the current rules.

Authorised red varieties can make up the remaining proportion, within the limits of each category. Gran Selezione has a narrower specified list of permitted complementary varieties. Because rules can change, the current specification should be checked before publication or relied upon for legal interpretation.

This is one reason Australian Sangiovese and Chianti Classico should not be treated as interchangeable. They may share a grape variety, but they come from different places and may be made under different regulations, climates and winemaking traditions.

## What do Annata, Riserva and Gran Selezione mean?

**Annata** is the ordinary, vintage-dated category. A label may simply say “Chianti Classico” rather than prominently using the word Annata.

**Riserva** is a legally defined longer-aged category. It requires at least 24 months of ageing, including at least three months in bottle.

**Gran Selezione** requires at least 30 months of ageing, including at least three months in bottle. It also has stricter current Sangiovese requirements and estate-controlled sourcing and vinification conditions.

These categories tell you something about composition and minimum ageing. They do not guarantee that every Riserva or Gran Selezione will taste better than every standard Chianti Classico, or that you will prefer it.

The Gallo Nero, or Black Rooster, is the mandatory identifying mark of Chianti Classico DOCG. It supplements the words “Chianti Classico”; it is not a score or a quality rating.

## What does Chianti Classico taste like?

A useful starting description is: **dry, fresh, savoury and structured**.

Sangiovese commonly contributes noticeable acidity and tannin. Acidity can give the wine energy and lift, while tannin creates a drying, slightly grippy sensation on the gums. Depending on the bottle, you may find red or dark fruit, violet-like floral notes, herbs, spice, earth or oak.

The region’s hills, varied elevations, aspects and soils can contribute to different ripening patterns and wine textures. Vintage conditions, vineyard site, grape material, extraction, blending and ageing choices matter too. These influences interact; no single soil or elevation guarantees one flavour.

That is why it is unhelpful to call all Chianti Classico light-bodied, rustic, heavy or oak-driven. Some wines may feel delicate and lifted, while others are broader, darker or more firmly structured.

## Reading the label in Australia

In an Australian bottle shop or restaurant, look first for:

- **Chianti Classico DOCG**
- **Chianti Classico Riserva DOCG**
- **Chianti Classico Gran Selezione DOCG**
- A vintage year
- The Gallo Nero mark

DOCG and PDO are Italian and European origin-and-regulation concepts. They are not simply Italian versions of an Australian Geographic Indication. Australian GIs also communicate regulated geographic origin, but the legal systems and production rules are different.

A protected appellation name tells you where the wine comes from and which rules apply. It does not guarantee a uniform flavour, a particular quality level or personal enjoyment.

## Food context

Chianti Classico’s acidity and tannin can make it a helpful choice with savoury food. Try the general idea with tomato-based pasta, pizza, roast meats, mushrooms or hard cheese.

These are starting points rather than strict rules. The dish’s richness, sauce, seasoning and preparation matter, as does the particular wine. For broader guidance, see [how wine pairing works](/how-wine-pairing-works/) and [wine and cheese pairing](/wine-and-cheese-pairing/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Chianti Classico made only from Sangiovese?

No. Sangiovese must make up most of the wine, but permitted complementary red varieties may be included within the appellation rules.

### Is Chianti Classico always better than Chianti?

No. They are different appellations, not a simple quality ladder. The best choice depends on the wine, food and your preferences.

### Is Gran Selezione a guarantee of quality?

No. It is a regulated category with stricter composition, sourcing and ageing requirements. It is not a universal ranking of taste.

### Is Chianti Classico the same as Tuscany?

No. Tuscany is the broader Italian region. Chianti Classico is one defined appellation within it.
