# What does a sommelier do?

A sommelier is a wine-focused hospitality professional who helps people select, understand, manage and serve wine. In an Australian restaurant, a sommelier might advise guests, help shape the wine list, organise stock, suggest food-and-wine matches and support the wider service team.

The role is broader than simply recommending a bottle—and it is not limited to fine dining.

## Highlights

- A sommelier helps connect wine with the guest, food and occasion.
- They may work with wine lists, purchasing, stock and cellar organisation.
- Guest advice and wine service are important parts of many restaurant roles.
- Some sommeliers work in wineries, retail, wholesale, importing, distribution or education.
- The job varies considerably between workplaces.
- You do not need specialist wine vocabulary to ask a sommelier for help.

## What might a sommelier do in a restaurant?

In some restaurants, a sommelier helps create or maintain the wine list. That can mean choosing wines that suit the food menu, the venue’s style, its customers and its available storage. The work may be shared with an owner, chef, beverage director, manager or supplier.

A sommelier may also help purchase wine, communicate with suppliers, monitor stock and organise a cellar or storage area. These responsibilities are more likely to be central in a venue with a substantial wine programme. Someone who mainly works on the restaurant floor may have less involvement in purchasing or inventory.

For guests, the most visible part of the job is usually advice. A sommelier can listen to what you enjoy, consider what you are eating and suggest a suitable wine. Food-and-wine matching may be part of that conversation, but there is rarely only one “correct” pairing. Personal taste matters.

Wine service can also fall within the role. Depending on the restaurant, this may include presenting and opening a bottle, pouring it, considering temperature and glassware, or checking whether the wine appears sound. More formal service may include decanting or explaining the wine, but not every venue follows the same tableside rituals.

A sommelier may also educate or train other staff, helping them understand the wine list and answer guest questions confidently.

## Is every sommelier the same?

No. The workplace makes a big difference.

Australia’s National Training Register describes a Sommelier Skill Set covering specialist advice on Australian and imported wine and other alcoholic beverages, as well as coordinating wine service and sales. It applies across hospitality organisations, wineries, and retail and wholesale liquor businesses.

That means sommelier work can include winery hospitality, retail wine advice, supplier or distributor education, importing, beverage-programme support and customer education. In each setting, the balance between service, sales, purchasing, teaching and administration can differ.

Australia also lists “Sommelier” as an occupation in the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ OSCA 2024 classification. That classification helps describe occupations; it is not, by itself, proof that everyone using the title holds one particular qualification or licence. Vocational training associated with sommelier work exists, while responsible-service and liquor-law requirements can vary between Australian states and territories.

## How is a sommelier different from other wine professionals?

These roles can overlap, especially in smaller businesses, but their main focus is different:

- A **winemaker** makes and manages the production of wine.
- A **viticulturist** works with grape growing and vineyards.
- A **wine critic or reviewer** evaluates and communicates opinions about wine.
- A **wine educator** teaches wine knowledge or tasting.
- A **waiter or general hospitality server** serves food and beverages but may not have specialist wine responsibilities.

A sommelier’s focus is generally the relationship between wine, hospitality, service and the guest’s decision.

## How should you ask a sommelier for help?

You do not need to name grape varieties or regions. Tell them:

- what you usually like or dislike;
- what you are eating;
- whether you want something familiar or adventurous;
- the occasion; and
- your budget.

For example: “I usually like fresh, dry whites, we’re having seafood, and we’d like to stay around this price.”

That gives the sommelier useful information without turning the conversation into a test. Their job is to make wine selection and service more useful and enjoyable—not to make you prove how much you know.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a sommelier only recommend expensive wine?

No. A useful recommendation should reflect the guest’s preferences, food, occasion and budget. The title does not mean a sommelier must recommend the most expensive option.

### Does every sommelier have a formal qualification?

Not necessarily. Australia has vocational training associated with sommelier work, but the title alone should not be treated as a universal guarantee of one qualification.

### Do I need to know about wine before speaking to a sommelier?

No. Plain descriptions such as “fresh”, “full-bodied”, “not too sweet” or “similar to something I enjoyed recently” are perfectly useful starting points.
