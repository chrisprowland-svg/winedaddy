# What does a winemaker do?

## Highlights

- A winemaker plans, coordinates and makes decisions about turning grapes or grape juice into wine.
- The role can begin with assessing fruit and continue through fermentation, maturation, blending, finishing and bottling.
- Winemakers influence a wine’s style, but they do not control every factor that shapes the final result.
- A winemaker may not personally operate every piece of cellar equipment or perform every practical task.
- Viticulturists and vineyard managers focus mainly on growing grapes; winemakers focus mainly on producing wine.
- In a small Australian winery, one person may combine several roles. In a larger winery, responsibilities are more likely to be shared between specialists.

## Quick answer

A winemaker is the person who plans, supervises and coordinates the production of wine, while making technical and stylistic decisions along the way.

That can include assessing fruit, deciding how grapes should be processed, overseeing fermentation, choosing maturation vessels, blending different wines and helping determine when the finished wine is ready for bottling or release.

The job is not simply “putting grapes into a tank”. A winemaker responds to the condition of the fruit, the progress of the wine and the style the winery is trying to produce. Some decisions are planned before harvest; others are made later, after tasting, testing or observing how a ferment develops.

A winemaker also does not necessarily do every physical cellar task. In a larger Australian winery, winemakers may work with viticulturists, cellar teams, laboratory staff, winery managers and bottling or packaging specialists. In a small business, one person may take on several of these jobs.

## What is a winemaker responsible for?

A useful way to understand the role is to follow the wine from grapes to bottle.

### Assessing fruit and preparing for harvest

Before grapes arrive at the winery, a winemaker may assess samples for ripeness, sugar, acidity and suitability for processing. This information can help guide decisions about when fruit is picked, where it is sent and how it will be handled.

That does not mean the winemaker always controls the harvest date. Vineyard managers, grape growers, viticulturists and winery owners may all be involved. The winemaker’s role is usually focused on what the fruit can contribute to the wine and how it should be handled once it arrives.

### Receiving and processing grapes

At the winery, the winemaker may help decide whether grapes are sorted, crushed, destemmed or pressed. They may also decide which batches go into particular tanks, barrels or other vessels.

These choices depend on the fruit, the intended wine style, available equipment and the winery’s production approach. There is no single processing sequence that every Australian winery follows.

### Managing fermentation

Fermentation is the stage when yeast converts grape sugars into alcohol, producing carbon dioxide and heat along the way. A winemaker may select or approve a fermentation approach, monitor its progress and respond if the wine develops differently from expected.

Practical cellar work can include managing temperature, moving juice or wine, preparing additions and keeping records. Cellar teams may carry out much of this work, following specifications or instructions and communicating results back to the winemaker or cellar manager.

The winemaker therefore acts partly as a decision-maker and partly as a coordinator. They may set the direction without personally operating every pump, tank or piece of equipment.

### Choosing maturation methods

After fermentation, wine may be matured in tanks, barrels or other vessels. A winemaker may decide how the wine should be matured, how long it should remain in a vessel and what should be monitored during that time.

Maturation decisions can influence the wine’s texture, aroma and balance, but no vessel or length of time guarantees one particular flavour. The result also depends on the wine itself, the fruit, the season and the conditions of production.

### Blending and finishing the wine

Blending involves combining wines or batches to create the final composition. A winemaker may compare different lots, varieties, parcels or vessels and decide how they work together.

The aim might be consistency, balance or a particular style. Blending is not necessarily a fixed formula, and it is not always performed by one person working alone. Tasting, analysis, production requirements and business decisions may all contribute.

Finishing can include decisions about clarification, filtration, stability and preparation for bottling. Laboratory or quality staff may provide analysis, while cellar or packaging teams may carry out practical work.

### Bottling and quality decisions

A winemaker may be involved in checking whether a wine is ready for bottling or release. However, the physical bottling and packaging may be handled by a dedicated team or an external provider.

The exact division of responsibility depends on the winery. “Winemaker” is not a universal job description with identical authority everywhere.

## How is a winemaker different from other winery roles?

The boundaries overlap, especially in small businesses, but the broad distinctions are useful.

- **Viticulturist:** focuses primarily on grapevines, vineyards and growing conditions.
- **Vineyard manager:** organises and manages day-to-day vineyard operations.
- **Winemaker:** focuses primarily on turning grapes or grape juice into wine and making production decisions.
- **Cellarhand:** carries out practical winery operations such as equipment setup, transfers, fermentation-related work, cleaning and records.
- **Winery manager:** may coordinate people, equipment, schedules and overall production operations.
- **Laboratory or quality staff:** may support sampling, analysis, records and checks against specifications.

One person can hold more than one of these responsibilities. A small family winery might have a winemaker who also works in the vineyard, drives a forklift, manages records and helps with bottling. A larger commercial winery is more likely to divide the work between specialist teams.

## Does the winemaker make every decision?

No.

The winemaker may make, recommend or approve many important production decisions, but wine is a collaborative process. Decisions can be shared with grape growers, viticulturists, cellar supervisors, laboratory staff, winery managers, owners, consultants and packaging providers.

The winemaker also cannot control every factor affecting the finished wine. Grape variety, vineyard site, season, fruit condition, microorganisms, equipment, timing and storage all play a part. The winemaker guides the process within those conditions; they do not create the wine in isolation.

That is why it is more accurate to think of a winemaker as a guide, decision-maker and coordinator than as the sole author of a bottle’s quality.

## How does a winemaker influence wine style?

Winemaking choices can influence style at several points, including:

- how grapes are processed;
- the fermentation approach;
- temperature, skin contact and extraction management where relevant;
- the vessel used for maturation;
- maturation time;
- blending;
- finishing and release decisions.

These choices matter, but they are influences rather than guarantees. A particular yeast, barrel or processing choice will not produce exactly the same result in every wine. The starting fruit and the conditions of the vintage remain important.

So when you taste a wine and think about the winemaker’s influence, think of a series of decisions made in response to real material: grapes, juice, fermenting wine and maturing wine. It is a combination of intention and response.

## The simplest way to remember the role

A winemaker helps turn an agricultural product into a finished wine by making, coordinating and reviewing decisions from fruit intake to bottling.

They may work with many people, perform some hands-on cellar tasks or oversee others doing them. Their choices can shape a wine’s style, but the final result is shared with the vineyard, the season, the biology of fermentation and the practical conditions of the winery.

## Suggested FAQs

### Is a winemaker the same as an oenologist?

Not exactly. Oenology is the science and study of wine and winemaking. A winemaker is the person involved in the practical production and decision-making process. The terms can overlap in industry use.

### Does a winemaker grow the grapes?

Sometimes, particularly in a small business, but not always. Grape growing is primarily the work of viticulture, while winemaking focuses primarily on producing wine from grapes or juice.

### Does a winemaker work alone?

Usually not. Winemaking can involve vineyard, cellar, laboratory, management, bottling and packaging teams. The number of people and the way responsibilities are divided vary between wineries.

### Can a winemaker change the style of a wine?

A winemaker can influence style through processing, fermentation, maturation, blending and finishing decisions. Those choices do not override the effects of grape variety, site, season, fruit condition and other production factors.
