# What is Fer Servadou?

Fer Servadou is a black-skinned red wine grape from south-west France. It is best known in the regional wines of Marcillac and Gaillac, where it may appear under names such as **Mansois** or **Braucol** rather than Fer Servadou.

The grape can make deeply coloured, fresh and tannic red wines with red or black fruit, herbal notes and sometimes a green-pepper character. Those are tendencies, not rules: ripeness, climate, vineyard site, winemaking and blending all influence the finished wine.

## Highlights

- It is a **grape variety**, not a wine style, region or appellation.
- Other recognised names include **Fer N, Braucol, Mansois and Pinenc**.
- **Braucol** is particularly associated with Gaillac.
- **Mansois** is especially associated with Marcillac and the Aveyron area.
- It is a relatively late-ripening variety with some useful vineyard qualities.
- Its wines can combine fruit, acidity, tannin and herbal or peppery aromas.
- **Ferradou is different**: it is a separate grape created by crossing Fer Servadou with Merlot.

## What do the different names mean?

Wine names can be confusing because one grape may have several regional names. In official French and European variety records, Fer Servadou is associated with the name **Fer N**, as well as Fer Servadou, Braucol, Mansois and Pinenc.

The name you see depends partly on where the wine comes from. In Gaillac, the grape is commonly called Braucol. In Marcillac, Mansois is a familiar regional name. A bottle from one of these regions may therefore identify the wine primarily by its regional designation, rather than putting Fer Servadou on the front label.

These terms should not be confused with the places themselves:

- **Fer Servadou** is the grape.
- **Braucol** and **Mansois** are names associated with the grape.
- **Marcillac** and **Gaillac** are French wine regions and appellations.

A regional wine name does not automatically mean that every wine from that region contains Fer Servadou, or that the grape is used in the same proportion in every bottle.

## Where does Fer Servadou come from?

Fer Servadou is firmly associated with the vineyards of south-west France, particularly the Aveyron and Tarn areas. Its deeper historical origin is less certain. Technical and regional sources describe it differently: one suggests a possible connection with Gironde, while another traces it towards the Spanish Basque Country.

The useful takeaway is not a single definitive origin story, but the grape’s strong identity in south-west French wine culture. It has long been linked with Marcillac and is also important in Gaillac and other regional wine traditions.

## Where is it used?

### Marcillac

Fer Servadou is the principal grape associated with Marcillac, a wine appellation in Aveyron. Here, many drinkers encounter the variety through the name **Marcillac** rather than through the grape name.

That makes Marcillac a good example of why grape and place are not interchangeable. Marcillac describes a regional wine, while Fer Servadou describes one of the key ingredients behind that wine.

### Gaillac

In Gaillac, Fer Servadou is generally known as **Braucol**. It can be an important red grape in local blends, alongside other permitted varieties.

The same grape can therefore create a different labelling experience depending on the region: a wine from Marcillac may highlight Mansois or Marcillac, while a Gaillac wine may refer to Braucol.

## What is Fer Servadou like to grow?

Fer Servadou is relatively late-ripening. In practical terms, it generally needs a longer growing season than early-ripening varieties, although the actual harvest date changes with the vineyard site and the season.

The Institut Français de la Vigne et du Vin describes its fertility as somewhat irregular and notes that it is generally suited to long pruning. The variety also has useful resistance to grey rot and can preserve its bunches well on the vine. It is not disease-proof, however, and is susceptible to leafhoppers.

These vineyard characteristics help explain why the grape’s performance depends on careful site and vineyard management. They do not tell you exactly what every bottle will taste like.

## What does Fer Servadou wine taste like?

Fer Servadou wines often have:

- medium to deep colour;
- fresh acidity;
- noticeable, sometimes firm tannin;
- red and black fruit flavours;
- herbal, leafy or peppery notes.

At lower ripeness, the herbal character may be more prominent. Riper examples can show more fruit, including currant-like flavours. Some wines may show a green-pepper aroma linked with a compound called IBMP, but green pepper is not guaranteed and should not automatically be treated as a fault.

The variety can be structural and savoury as well as fruity. That combination may make it feel quite different from a soft, low-tannin red, especially when the wine is young. Blending and winemaking can soften, broaden or otherwise reshape the grape’s natural profile.

## Why can two Fer Servadou wines taste different?

A grape variety provides tendencies, not a fixed recipe. Fer Servadou’s style can change with:

- how ripe the grapes become;
- the climate and vineyard site;
- soil and growing conditions;
- crop level and vine balance;
- fermentation and maturation choices;
- whether the wine is bottled alone or blended.

A cooler site may preserve more acidity and herbal character, while greater ripeness may bring more fruit and rounder texture. A blend may also make the grape feel less obviously tannic or peppery than a wine where it is the main component.

So if one Fer Servadou tastes leafy and savoury while another tastes fruitier, that does not necessarily mean one is “wrong”. They may simply reflect different growing and winemaking choices.

## Fer Servadou versus Ferradou

The names are similar, but **Ferradou is not a synonym for Fer Servadou**.

Ferradou is a separate French wine-grape variety created by crossing Fer Servadou N with Merlot N. It belongs in a different category from the regional names Braucol and Mansois. If you see Ferradou on a wine label or in a technical description, do not assume it is simply another spelling of Fer Servadou.

## How might Australians encounter it?

Australian drinkers are most likely to come across Fer Servadou through an imported French regional wine, a specialist wine list or a specialist retailer listing. The grape may be described as Fer Servadou, Braucol or Mansois, depending on the wine’s regional tradition.

No verified evidence in this research establishes Australian plantings, commercial production or broad local availability. That does not prove the grape is absent from Australia; it simply means those claims should not be assumed.

If you encounter the name, expect a red wine with the possibility of fresh acidity, tannin, fruit and herbal or peppery character. A bottle labelled Marcillac or Gaillac may give you useful regional context, even when Fer Servadou is not the most prominent name on the front label.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Fer Servadou a red or white grape?

Fer Servadou is a black-skinned grape used for red wine.

### Is Fer Servadou the same as Braucol?

Yes. Braucol is a recognised regional name associated with Fer Servadou, particularly in Gaillac.

### Is Mansois the same as Fer Servadou?

Yes. Mansois is another recognised regional name, especially associated with Marcillac and Aveyron.

### Is Fer Servadou the same as Ferradou?

No. Ferradou is a separate grape variety made by crossing Fer Servadou N with Merlot N.

### Is Fer Servadou always peppery or herbal?

No. Herbal and peppery notes are common tendencies, but the balance changes with ripeness, site, winemaking and blending.

### What food goes with Fer Servadou?

As a practical starting point, try it with hearty foods that can stand up to fresh acidity and tannin, such as grilled vegetables, lentils or roast meats. This is a suggestion rather than a fixed rule: a lighter, fruitier example may suit gentler dishes, while a firmer wine may work better with richer food.
