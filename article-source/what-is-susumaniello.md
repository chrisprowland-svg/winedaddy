# What is Susumaniello?

## Highlights

- Susumaniello is a dark-skinned Italian wine grape.
- It is most closely associated with Puglia, especially the Brindisi area and the wider Salento.
- It can make deeply coloured, fresh and tannic red wines.
- It is also used for rosé and blended wines.
- “Susumaniello” is the grape name—not another name for Puglia, Salento or Brindisi.
- The style can change significantly with vineyard conditions, ripeness and winemaking.
- Australian drinkers are most likely to encounter it in imported Italian wine or through specialist retailers and restaurants.

## Quick answer

Susumaniello is an Italian red-wine grape variety associated mainly with Puglia, in southern Italy. Its strongest geographic associations are with Brindisi and the Salento area.

Red wines made from Susumaniello can be deeply coloured, dry and structured, with noticeable acidity and tannin. Dark-fruit, savoury and spicy notes are commonly reported. However, the grape does not produce one fixed style: a lightly extracted red, a rosé and a concentrated varietal wine can taste quite different.

Susumaniello has traditionally been used in blends, where it can contribute colour, acidity and structure. Today, it is also bottled on its own, including as both red and rosé wine.

## Where does Susumaniello come from?

Susumaniello is associated with **Italy → Puglia → Salento → the Brindisi area**.

Puglia is the long south-eastern region that forms the heel of Italy. Salento refers broadly to its southern peninsula. Brindisi is both a city and a province within this wider area, and is one of the places most strongly connected with Susumaniello.

Nearby localities associated with the variety include San Vito dei Normanni, Mesagne, San Donaci and Cellino San Marco. These are useful orientation points, but they should not be treated as though one town defines the whole grape or its legal growing area.

You may also see Susumaniello in wines connected with appellations such as Brindisi DOC or Ostuni DOC. Those names describe geographic and appellation contexts. They are not synonyms for the grape.

The deeper historical origin of Susumaniello is less certain. Some regional accounts suggest a possible connection with Dalmatia, across the Adriatic Sea, but this should be treated as a theory rather than settled fact.

## What does Susumaniello wine taste like?

A typical red Susumaniello may have:

- deep ruby to purple colour;
- noticeable freshness from acidity;
- moderate to firm tannin;
- a dry palate;
- dark-fruit flavours such as plum, blackberry or blackcurrant;
- savoury, earthy or spicy notes.

That is a set of broad tendencies, not a tasting checklist. Susumaniello does not make every wine in the same way.

A concentrated red made with ripe grapes and significant skin contact may feel dark, full and tannic. A fresher wine, a blend or a rosé may be lighter, brighter and less obviously structured. Oak, fermentation vessel, extraction and time in bottle can also alter the result.

It is safest to think of Susumaniello as a grape capable of providing **colour, acidity and structure**, rather than as a guarantee of high alcohol or a heavy body.

## Is Susumaniello a red or white grape?

Susumaniello is a dark-skinned red wine grape. The Italian authorised grape list records it as **Susumaniello N.** The “N.” identifies it within Italy’s naming system as a black or red grape.

Most people encountering the variety will be thinking about red wine, but Susumaniello is also used for rosé. In rosé winemaking, shorter contact with the grape skins can produce a paler, fresher wine with less tannin.

## Is Susumaniello usually blended?

It has a blending tradition. Historically, Susumaniello was used with other varieties to add colour and acidity. That makes sense for a grape with strongly coloured skins and noticeable structure.

Modern producers also make single-variety Susumaniello wines. So a bottle labelled with the grape may contain wine made entirely from Susumaniello, or it may be a blend where the grape plays one part in the final character.

The label, technical information or producer description is the best way to establish which applies to a particular bottle. The grape name alone does not guarantee a particular blend or flavour profile.

## What are the other names for Susumaniello?

The preferred modern name is **Susumaniello**. Regional and technical sources report several historical or alternative forms, including:

- Susipaniello
- Cuccipaniello
- Cozzomaniello
- Grismaniello
- Susomaniello
- Susumariello
- Susumariello nero
- Zingariello
- Zuzumaniello

These names should be understood as reported synonyms or historical naming forms. They are not necessarily names a modern drinker will see on a bottle.

There is no sufficiently authoritative pronunciation guide in the available evidence, so it is better not to present one spelling-out as definitive.

## Why can two Susumaniello wines taste different?

The grape is only one part of a wine’s identity. Variation can come from:

- the vineyard site and soil;
- vine age and crop level;
- when the grapes are harvested;
- how ripe the grapes become;
- how long the juice stays with the skins;
- the fermentation vessel;
- oak or other maturation;
- whether the wine is red, rosé or blended.

This is why one Susumaniello may seem dense and tannic while another feels medium-bodied, fresh or savoury. Neither necessarily represents the “correct” version.

## Serving and food ideas

For a red Susumaniello, serving it slightly cool rather than warm is a sensible starting point. Its acidity and tannin can work well with food such as grilled or braised meats, tomato-based pasta, mushrooms and firm cheeses.

These are broad pairing suggestions, not rules. A lighter or fresher Susumaniello may suit a wider range of dishes, while a concentrated red may benefit from richer food.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Susumaniello a grape or a wine?

It is a grape variety. “Susumaniello wine” means a wine made partly or wholly from that grape.

### Is Susumaniello the same as Brindisi?

No. Susumaniello is the grape. Brindisi is a place and an appellation context associated with wines from the area.

### Is Susumaniello always tannic?

No. It can contribute tannin and structure, but the final level depends on ripeness, extraction, winemaking, blending and the wine’s style.

### Is Susumaniello rare?

The supplied evidence does not establish a reliable current measure of rarity, plantings or market share. It is best described as an unfamiliar variety for many Australian drinkers, rather than assigning it a market label that has not been verified.

### What should I expect from a bottle?

Expect the possibility of a dark-coloured, dry wine with fresh acidity and noticeable structure. Then allow for variation: it may be fruit-driven, savoury, spicy, lighter in rosé form or softened by blending and maturation.
