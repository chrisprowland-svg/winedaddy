# How to choose wine in a bottle shop

Choosing wine in a bottle shop is easier when you decide what you want before you study the shelf. Start with the occasion, food, budget and one or two preferences. Then choose a broad style, use the label for clues, compare a few bottles and ask staff a specific question.

## Highlights

- Start with the occasion, meal and realistic budget.
- Choose a broad style before worrying about a grape or region.
- Use the label as useful evidence, not a promise.
- “Dry”, “fruity”, “fresh”, “full-bodied” and “tannic” describe different things.
- Price, packaging, medals and scores do not guarantee that a wine will suit you.
- Specific questions usually produce more useful advice than simply asking for “a good wine”.
- Keep a short note of wines you enjoy or dislike.

## A simple bottle-shop method

### 1. Define the occasion

Are you buying wine for a weeknight meal, a celebration, a gift or to explore something unfamiliar? If food is involved, think about its main flavours, richness, spice and intensity.

Then set a maximum budget in Australian dollars. A budget keeps the shelf from making the decision for you.

### 2. Choose a broad style

You do not need to know every grape variety or region. Begin with a direction such as:

- crisp, dry white;
- aromatic white;
- light-bodied red;
- fuller-bodied red;
- rosé;
- sparkling wine; or
- sweet wine.

These are starting points, not guarantees. A rosé can vary in sweetness and body, for example, while two wines made from the same grape can taste quite different.

If you are unsure, describe the feeling you want: fresh, soft, fruity, savoury, low-tannin, full-bodied or lightly oaked. **Dry** means not sweet; it does not mean flavourless. A wine can be dry and fruit-driven at the same time.

For more detail, see [What Is Sweetness in Wine?](/what-is-sweetness-in-wine/), [What Is Wine Body?](/what-is-wine-body/), [What Is Acidity in Wine?](/what-is-acidity-in-wine/) and [What Are Tannins in Wine?](/what-are-tannins/).

### 3. Read the label for clues

Look for the grape variety, country or region, vintage, alcohol percentage, volume, standard-drink information, producer or supplier details, style terms and relevant allergen declarations.

On Australian wine, grape variety, vintage and Geographic Indication (GI) are optional claims. A GI is useful origin information: it connects a wine with a place, but does not guarantee a particular taste or level of quality. Australian and imported bottles may present grape, region, vintage, classification and allergen information differently.

A label can help you narrow the choice, but it cannot tell you exactly whether you will enjoy the wine. For a closer look, read [How to Read a Wine Label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/).

### 4. Compare two or three bottles

Compare bottles using the same questions:

- Which seems drier or sweeter?
- Which is likely to be lighter- or fuller-bodied?
- Which sounds fresher or more acidic?
- Do you want more or less tannin or oak influence?
- Is the region, grape or vintage relevant to this occasion?

Reviews, medals and scores can provide context, but they reflect a judging or reviewing setting and may not match your taste. Price, packaging and closure are also not guarantees of quality or suitability. [Why Wine Prices Vary](/why-wine-prices-vary/) and [How to Understand Wine Reviews](/how-to-understand-wine-reviews/) explain those subjects in more depth.

### 5. Ask a specific question

Try:

> “I’m looking for a dry, fresh white around A$__, with no obvious oak. What are two options, and how do they differ?”

Or:

> “I want a red with plenty of flavour but not much tannin.”

This gives bottle-shop staff something practical to work with. You can also ask for a similar alternative if the first bottle is unavailable. Retailers differ in their shelf layouts and advice, so treat recommendations as helpful input rather than a guarantee.

### 6. Check the bottle and learn from it

Before buying, avoid a bottle that appears damaged, leaking, badly stained or otherwise compromised.

After drinking it, note what you liked or disliked: sweetness, body, freshness, tannin, oak, the food served and whether it suited the occasion. That small record will make your next choice easier.

## The fallback formula

Know almost nothing about wine? Use:

**occasion + budget + one preferred characteristic + one staff question**

For example: “I need a wine for a spicy dinner, up to A$__, and I prefer something fresh rather than sweet. What are two suitable styles?”

That is enough to begin confidently.

## Common questions

### Does a higher price mean better wine?

Not automatically. Price can reflect many factors, and suitability depends on your occasion and taste. For detailed value questions, see [How to Compare Wine Value](/how-to-compare-wine-value/).

### Is a Geographic Indication a quality rating?

No. It is primarily information about origin. It may be useful when you are learning which places or styles you enjoy, but it does not guarantee a particular flavour.

### Is fruitiness the same as sweetness?

No. Fruitiness describes fruit-like aromas or flavours. Sweetness describes the wine’s perception of sugar. A wine can be fruity but dry.
