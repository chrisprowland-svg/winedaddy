# How to pronounce French wine regions

French wine-region names can look intimidating on a wine list. The good news is that you do not need a perfect French accent to say them confidently. Use a clear approximation, remember that French spelling does not always follow English spelling rules, and focus on being understood.

## Highlights

- **Bordeaux**: *bor-DOH*
- **Bourgogne**: *boor-GON-yuh*
- **Burgundy**: *BUR-gun-dee*
- **Champagne**: *sham-PAHN-yuh*
- **Chablis**: *sha-BLEE*
- **Beaujolais**: *boh-zhoh-LAY*

The capital letters show the syllable to emphasise. These are reader-friendly approximations, not a substitute for hearing the French pronunciation. Australian accents will naturally colour the sounds, and that is fine.

## French names and English names are not always the same

The French name for Burgundy is **Bourgogne**, but **Burgundy** is the established English name. Saying “a Burgundy” in an Australian restaurant or bottle shop is entirely normal; you do not need to switch to the French name to sound knowledgeable.

English-speaking wine culture also uses familiar forms of names such as Bordeaux, Champagne and Chablis. Some people pronounce them close to the French, while others use a more Anglicised version. Both can be understood in context.

The practical rule is simple: a respectful, intelligible approximation is better than avoiding the name altogether.

## Common French wine-region names

| Name | Say it approximately like this | Useful note |
|---|---|---|
| **Bordeaux** | *bor-DOH* | The final **x** is not pronounced. English usage may include an English-style **r**. |
| **Bourgogne** | *boor-GON-yuh* | The ending is closer to “nyuh” than “gone”. |
| **Burgundy** | *BUR-gun-dee* | This is the established English equivalent of Bourgogne. |
| **Champagne** | *sham-PAHN-yuh* | The **ch** is close to “sh”; the ending is approximately “nyuh”. |
| **Chablis** | *sha-BLEE* | The final **s** is silent. |
| **Beaujolais** | *boh-zhoh-LAY* | The middle sound is like the **zh** in “measure”; the final **s** is silent. |

If you see other familiar names such as **Loire**, **Rhône**, **Alsace** or **Provence**, listening to a reliable French dictionary recording is more useful than trying to decode every letter on the page. Their French vowels and nasal sounds do not map neatly onto Australian English spelling.

## A few patterns that help

### “Ch” is often like “sh”

In names such as **Chablis**, **Champagne** and **Châteauneuf**, the opening **ch** is generally close to the sound in “sh”.

### “Gn” is approximately “ny”

The ending of **Bourgogne** and **Champagne** resembles the sound in the middle of “canyon”. It is not usually a hard “g” sound.

### Final letters may be quiet

French often leaves some final letters unpronounced. That is why **Chablis** ends in “blee”, while the final **s** in **Beaujolais** is also silent. This is a useful guide, not an absolute rule for every French word or phrase.

### Nasal vowels take practice

Names such as **Champagne** contain French nasal vowels. There is no exact Australian-English equivalent, so do not worry about reproducing them perfectly. A close approximation will still make your meaning clear.

## How to practise before a tasting or dinner

1. Find a reputable French dictionary or clearly attributed native-speaker recording.
2. Listen to the whole word rather than reading it letter by letter.
3. Repeat it slowly, then at normal speaking speed.
4. Put it into a sentence: “Could I try a wine from Bordeaux?” or “I’m looking for a Burgundy.”
5. If your pronunciation is not perfect, keep speaking. Context does much of the work.

At a restaurant, retailer, cellar door or tasting, pronunciation is not a test of wine expertise. It is simply a way to communicate. The wine region’s geography, reputation and style are separate from how closely you reproduce its French sounds.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there only one correct pronunciation?

There is a broadly accepted French pronunciation, but English-speaking wine usage and individual accents vary. Use “a close French pronunciation” rather than treating one accent as the only acceptable option.

### Is saying “Burgundy” wrong?

No. **Burgundy** is the established English name for **Bourgogne** and is perfectly appropriate in Australian English.

### What should I do if I am unsure?

Say the name clearly, use the English equivalent where one is established, or describe the wine by its region in a sentence. Being understood matters more than sounding perfectly French.
