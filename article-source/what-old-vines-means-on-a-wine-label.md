# What “old vines” means on a wine label

## Highlights

- “Old vines” describes the age of the grapevines, not the age of the wine in the bottle.
- In Australia, there is no single universal age threshold for the phrase.
- Its meaning can vary between producers, regions and schemes.
- Older vines may behave differently, but age alone does not guarantee better wine.
- “Old vines” does not automatically mean low-yielding, dry-grown, organic, single-vineyard or premium.
- A producer’s stated vine age and vineyard details make the claim more useful.

## Quick answer

When “old vines” appears on a wine label, it generally means the grapes came from vines that the producer considers old. It is a claim about the vineyard’s planting history—not about how long the wine has been bottled, or how mature it will taste.

The important catch is that “old” does not have one universally accepted legal meaning in Australia. The phrase can mean different things to different producers, regions or voluntary schemes. One producer may state a planting date or age; another may use the phrase more generally.

Older vines can have different growth patterns, root systems and yields. However, there is no hard evidence that old vines automatically make better wine. Site, season, grape variety, genetics and vineyard management can be just as important—or more important—than vine age.

## Why might vine age matter?

As vines mature, they can develop more permanent wood and more extensive root systems. In some circumstances, older vines may produce fewer shoots or lower yields. These features can affect how a vineyard grows and how its fruit develops.

But this is not a simple “older means better” equation. Australian research on Shiraz in the Barossa Valley found that the effects of vine age varied with site and season. Older vines produced higher yields at some sites, challenging the assumption that old vines always produce small crops.

Vine age is also difficult to separate from everything that happened around the vines. Older plantings may use different rootstocks, clones, trellising, irrigation or pruning systems from newer vineyards. The resulting wine reflects that whole combination, not age alone.

## What “old vines” does not tell you

The phrase does not automatically mean:

- the wine came from one vineyard;
- the vines were dry-grown;
- the vineyard is organic or biodynamic;
- the vines produced a low yield;
- the wine will be more complex, concentrated or age-worthy;
- the wine is higher quality or worth a higher price.

Those may be separate claims requiring their own evidence. “Old vines” is best understood as one piece of vineyard context, not a quality score.

It is also different from “old wine”. A wine made from old vines might be bottled recently, while an older bottle could have been made from relatively young vines.

## How to read the claim in Australia

Wine Australia’s educational material says there is no universally accepted legal definition of “old vines”. That does not mean the phrase is meaningless, or that it can be used without responsibility.

Australian consumer-law guidance says claims on packaging should be accurate, truthful and based on reasonable grounds. A label should not create a misleading impression through a vague or unsupported claim.

As a shopper, look for the information behind the phrase. Does the producer state a planting date, a vine age, a particular vineyard or the definition used? More detail gives you a clearer basis for understanding what “old vines” means on that bottle.

## A practical checklist

When you see the phrase, ask:

1. **What age does the producer mean?** Look for a planting year or stated age.
2. **Does it apply to all the grapes?** The wine may be a blend, so check the wording and vineyard details.
3. **Is a scheme or producer definition identified?** This can explain how “old” has been determined.
4. **What other information is provided?** Region, variety, vintage and winemaking may tell you more about the wine’s likely style.
5. **Am I treating the phrase as a guarantee?** It is a clue about vineyard history, not proof of quality.

The next time you see “old vines”, you do not need to dismiss it—or take it on faith. Treat it as an invitation to ask a better question: **old according to whom, and what else shaped the wine?**

## Frequently asked questions

### Does “old vines” mean the wine is old?

No. It refers to the age of the vines that produced the grapes. The wine’s age depends on when it was made, bottled or released.

### Is there a minimum age for “old vines” in Australia?

No universal Australian threshold was identified in the approved sources. Definitions can vary, so check whether the producer gives a specific age or planting date.

### Do old vines always produce better wine?

No. Vine age may influence vineyard performance, but research and industry guidance do not support treating it as a guarantee of superior wine.

### Do old vines always produce lower yields?

No. Older vines may produce lower yields in some circumstances, but yield also depends on site, season, variety and management. Australian research has found higher yields in older vines at some sites.
