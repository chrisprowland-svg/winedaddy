# How to taste wine without feeling intimidated

Wine tasting is not an exam, and you do not need to identify the grape variety, region or exact aroma to do it well. It is simply a way of paying attention.

A useful beginner’s method is:

1. Look at the wine.
2. Smell it before and after a gentle swirl.
3. Taste a small sip.
4. Notice its texture and finish.
5. Decide whether you enjoy it—and why.

There is no single correct reaction. The most useful tasting note is an honest one that helps you understand what you are experiencing.

## Highlights

### 1. Look

Hold the glass by the stem or base where practical and notice the wine’s appearance.

Ask yourself:

- Is it pale, medium or deep in colour?
- Is it clear or hazy?
- If it is sparkling, can you see bubbles?

These are observations, not conclusions. Colour does not prove a wine’s quality, sweetness, age or grape variety. It is just the first thing you notice.

### 2. Smell before swirling

Bring the glass to your nose and take a gentle smell before swirling. This gives you a starting point.

You might notice fruit, flowers, herbs, spice, earthiness or something savoury. You might also notice that the aroma is subtle—or that you cannot identify anything specific. That is completely fine.

Next, gently swirl the wine and smell it again. Swirling can help release aroma above the surface of the wine, but it does not need to become a performance. A small pour is easier to swirl, and there is no prize for making the biggest movement.

Try asking:

- Does it smell strong or subtle?
- Does it seem fresh, fruity, floral, herbal, spicy or savoury?
- Does it remind you of anything familiar?
- Did you notice more after swirling?

You do not need to say “blackcurrant”, “violet” or “wet stone” unless those descriptions genuinely make sense to you. “It smells fresh” or “I am not getting much aroma” are useful observations too.

### 3. Taste a small sip

Take a small sip and let the wine move briefly across your mouth. Notice what happens rather than trying to sound impressive.

You might notice:

- **Sweetness:** whether the wine tastes sweet or not. A dry wine generally does not taste sweet, even if it has plenty of fruit flavour.
- **Acidity:** a lively, fresh or mouth-watering sensation. A wine with noticeable acidity can feel sharp or bright.
- **Flavour:** the particular fruit, floral, herbal, spicy or savoury impressions you notice.
- **Alcohol:** warmth in the mouth or throat. Warmth does not automatically mean quality or poor quality.
- **Intensity:** how strongly the wine’s aromas and flavours make themselves known.

You can swallow the sip or spit it out at a tasting. Neither choice makes you more or less knowledgeable.

### 4. Notice texture and finish

Wine is not only about flavour. It also has physical sensations.

- **Body** is the wine’s sense of weight or fullness in your mouth. A light-bodied wine can feel delicate; a full-bodied wine can feel more substantial.
- **Texture or mouthfeel** describes how it feels. It might seem smooth, creamy, sharp, chalky or grippy.
- **Tannin** is a drying or puckering sensation, most commonly noticed in red wine. It can make your gums feel slightly rough.
- **Finish** is what remains after you swallow or spit: the flavour, freshness, warmth or texture that lingers.

You do not need to assess every feature every time. Even noticing one or two is a useful start.

### 5. Decide whether you enjoy it

This is the part many beginners forget: you are allowed to decide whether you like the wine.

Try completing this sentence:

> “It smells ______, tastes ______, feels ______, and I like it because ______.”

For example:

> “It smells lightly of citrus, tastes dry and fresh, feels light-bodied, and I enjoy it because it is refreshing.”

That is a complete tasting note. It is not a score, and it does not need to be more complicated.

## Observation, interpretation and preference

Wine language becomes less intimidating when you separate three different things.

**Observation** is what you notice:

> “The wine smells like citrus.”

**Interpretation** is what that observation might suggest:

> “It seems fresh.”

**Preference** is your personal response:

> “I like it because it tastes bright.”

These are all valid, but they are not the same. You can dislike a wine that is carefully made, or enjoy one that another taster does not like. Wine tasting is not about replacing your preference with somebody else’s.

Words such as “fresh”, “fruity”, “soft”, “dry”, “light”, “full”, “sharp”, “smooth” and “not for me” are perfectly useful when they describe your experience honestly.

## What if you cannot smell much?

Specific aroma recognition develops with attention and practice. You do not need to recognise a list of fruit, flowers or spices to taste meaningfully.

Start with broader questions:

- Is the aroma strong, subtle or difficult to notice?
- Does it seem fresh, rich, savoury or neutral?
- Does it remind you of anything?
- Does it change after swirling?

Your surroundings can also make tasting harder. Coffee, toothpaste, strong food, perfume, smoke and other room smells can interfere with what you notice. Tiredness or congestion may do the same. None of this means you have failed.

If somebody else smells something you do not, you can simply say, “I am not getting that, but I notice…” Different people can notice different things.

## How to compare two wines

Comparing wines is often easier than judging one wine in isolation because each gives you a reference point.

Where practical, use:

- the same type of glass;
- similar serving conditions;
- similar pour sizes;
- the same tasting sequence.

Look at both wines, smell both before and after swirling, then taste them using the same prompts.

Ask:

- Which smells more intense?
- Which feels fresher or more acidic?
- Which has more body or grip?
- Which finish lasts longer?
- Which one would you choose to drink again?

This is not a professional judging exercise. Consistency simply makes differences easier to notice. You do not need special glassware to begin tasting.

## Tasting at an Australian cellar door

At a cellar door or organised tasting, take the pace offered by the host and ask what they suggest noticing. You are not expected to know the answer before you begin.

A spit bucket or spittoon is normal tasting equipment. You can take a small sip, pay attention to it and spit it out if you prefer. Spitting can reduce how much wine you swallow, but it does not remove alcohol exposure or guarantee sobriety or safe driving.

Taste slowly, use water between samples and remember that even small pours contain alcohol. Australian Government guidance defines a standard drink as 10 grams of pure alcohol, but the number of standard drinks in a serve varies with the amount poured and the wine’s alcohol concentration. Check the label or ask staff if you need that information.

Plan your transport and do not drive after drinking. Alcohol affects people differently, and this is not something to estimate from the number of samples you have tasted or spat out.

The same principles apply in a restaurant or when opening a bottle at home: notice what is there, use ordinary language and decide whether you enjoy it.

## Common worries, answered

### “What if I use the wrong words?”

There is no compulsory vocabulary. Start with what you know: “fresh”, “fruity”, “sweet”, “dry”, “light”, “full”, “smooth” or “too sharp for me”.

### “What if someone disagrees?”

They may be noticing something different, or using a different reference point. You do not have to change your own observation to match theirs.

### “What if I dislike the wine?”

That is useful information. Ask what you disliked: the sweetness, acidity, bitterness, warmth, flavour, texture or overall intensity.

### “Do I have to swirl, slurp or swallow?”

No. Swirling can help release aromas, and a small sip is enough to assess a wine. At a tasting, spitting is also a normal option.

## A relaxed way to practise

Choose two wines and write down three things for each:

1. What do I notice in the smell?
2. What do I notice in the mouth?
3. Would I choose to drink it again?

There is no need to reveal the wines or test yourself. The aim is to become more observant and more comfortable describing your own response.

The next time somebody asks what you think of a wine, you can keep it simple:

> “It is fresh and light, with a subtle fruity smell. I like it because it is easy to drink.”

That is not a beginner’s apology. It is a clear, useful tasting note.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need specialist wine glasses?

No. A clean, suitable glass is enough to begin. Consistent glassware can help when comparing wines, but no particular shape is mandatory for casual tasting.

### Is wine tasting only for experts?

No. Expertise can make it easier to recognise patterns, but tasting itself is simply structured observation. Your preferences and honest observations are valid from the first sip.

### Do I have to identify the exact aroma?

No. You can describe the aroma broadly, comment on its intensity or say that you notice very little. Precise aroma recognition develops with practice.

### Is spitting wine rude?

At an organised tasting or cellar door, using the provided spit bucket is normal. It is a responsible option when you want to assess wine without swallowing every sample.
