# What vegan wine means

## Highlights

- Vegan wine is made without animal products or animal-derived processing aids.
- Wine comes from grapes, but some winemaking aids can come from animals.
- Fining agents may be used to clarify or stabilise wine.
- Common animal-derived fining materials include gelatine, egg, milk-derived casein and fish-derived isinglass.
- Clear wine is not automatically vegan.
- “Vegan” does not automatically mean organic, natural, unfined, healthier or better tasting.
- In Australia, check the producer’s wording rather than relying only on appearance or a standard label.

## Quick answer

Vegan wine generally means wine made without animal-derived products or processing aids. The grapes themselves are vegan, but a winemaker may use a treatment called **fining** to clarify, stabilise or adjust the wine. Some traditional fining materials come from animals.

Common examples include gelatine, egg white or albumen, milk-derived casein and fish-derived isinglass. Other wines may use non-animal options such as bentonite, a clay-based material, or plant- and yeast-based alternatives.

In Australia, Wine Australia says that “vegan” is not defined or regulated by Wine Australia as a specific wine-labelling term. A vegan claim should still be accurate, verifiable and not misleading. If the bottle does not make the position clear, check the producer’s current website or contact the winery.

## Why wine may not be vegan

It is easy to assume that all wine is vegan because wine is made from grapes. The important distinction is what happens during production.

After fermentation, wine can contain tiny particles or compounds that make it look cloudy, feel more astringent or bitter, or become less stable over time. A winemaker may add a fining agent that attracts these compounds. The material and the unwanted particles then settle or are removed through cellar practices such as racking or filtration.

Fining is not automatically used in every wine, and different fining agents have different purposes. The choice can depend on the wine and the result the winemaker is seeking.

## Common animal-derived fining agents

The animal-derived materials most commonly discussed in winemaking include:

- **Gelatine**, made from animal collagen.
- **Egg white or egg albumen**, used especially in some red-wine treatments to help reduce harsh phenolic character.
- **Casein, potassium caseinate or skim milk**, which are milk-derived materials.
- **Isinglass**, a fish-derived collagen preparation.

A wine treated with an animal-derived fining material would generally not meet the usual consumer understanding of vegan wine, even if the material is later removed. Some fining agents may not be completely removed and can occasionally be associated with haze or deposits. However, it is not accurate to say that animal-derived fining materials are always present in the finished bottle, or always entirely absent.

Non-animal alternatives can include bentonite, plant proteins and yeast-based products. These are not interchangeable in every situation: the suitable option depends on the wine and the intended result.

## Can clear wine be non-vegan?

Yes. Clarity tells you about the wine’s appearance, not the complete production method.

Both animal-derived and non-animal fining agents can help produce a clear, stable-looking wine. A bright white wine, polished red, inexpensive bottle or wine made from a particular grape variety therefore cannot be identified as vegan by sight, price or style.

The reverse is also true: a wine that is hazy or has sediment is not automatically vegan. Appearance is not a reliable test in either direction.

## How to check an Australian wine

If vegan suitability matters to you or someone you are buying for, use this practical check:

1. **Read the bottle.** Look for a clear statement such as “vegan” or “vegan-friendly”.
2. **Check the producer’s website.** Product pages or technical notes may explain whether animal-derived fining agents were used.
3. **Treat retailer information as helpful but secondary.** Retailer listings can be incomplete or out of date.
4. **Contact the winery or producer if you are unsure.** Ask whether the particular wine was made without animal-derived fining agents, including gelatine, egg, milk or casein, and fish-derived isinglass.

The producer is usually best placed to answer questions about a particular wine. Production choices can vary between producers and wines, and information may change, so a current answer is more useful than assuming that every wine from a producer follows exactly the same process.

Certification may offer another form of evidence, but there is no universal Australian requirement that every vegan wine carry a particular certification. Nor does the absence of certification prove that a wine is not vegan. Check what a producer’s claim or certification actually covers.

## Vegan is not the same as other wine claims

These terms describe different things:

- **Unfined:** Usually means the producer did not use a fining treatment. It should not automatically be treated as proof that the wine is vegan unless the producer confirms the wider process.
- **Organic:** Describes an agricultural or production standard, depending on the particular claim and certification. Organic does not automatically mean vegan.
- **Biodynamic:** Refers to a particular farming and production approach. It does not automatically establish vegan suitability.
- **Natural or low-intervention:** Broad terms associated with a less interventionist approach, but neither automatically means vegan.
- **Preservative-free:** Describes a preservation claim, not whether animal-derived processing aids were used.
- **Gluten-free or allergen-free:** These are separate questions. An allergen statement may provide information about some milk or egg ingredients, but it is not a complete record of every processing aid used. The absence of an allergen statement is not proof that a wine is vegan.

A wine can be vegan and organic, vegan and unfined, or vegan and low-intervention. One label claim simply does not establish the others.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is all wine vegan because it is made from grapes?

No. Grapes are vegan, but animal-derived processing aids may be used during winemaking.

### Does unfined mean vegan?

Not necessarily. “Unfined” generally indicates that no fining treatment was used, but ask the producer if you need confirmation of the complete process.

### Does a vegan claim mean the wine is healthier?

No. Vegan suitability tells you about animal-derived products or processing aids. It does not, by itself, establish health, sustainability, flavour or quality.

### Is a wine vegan if the fining agent is removed?

The usual consumer understanding of vegan wine concerns how the wine was made, not only what remains visible in the bottle. If this matters, rely on the producer’s explanation rather than making your own judgement from the finished wine’s appearance.

### What is the safest way to choose vegan wine in Australia?

Start with a clear label claim, then check the producer’s current information. If the answer is important and the wording is unclear, contact the winery and ask specifically about animal-derived fining agents.
