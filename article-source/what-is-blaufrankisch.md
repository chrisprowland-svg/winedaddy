# What is Blaufränkisch?

Blaufränkisch is a dark-skinned red wine grape variety, particularly associated with Austria and Central Europe. It is not a wine region, a generic style or another name for Shiraz.

Wines made from Blaufränkisch commonly combine fresh acidity, red and dark berry fruit, spice and noticeable tannin. They can be bright and medium-bodied or darker, fuller and more oak-influenced, depending on where the grapes grow and how the wine is made.

## Highlights

- It is a red *Vitis vinifera* grape variety.
- Austria, especially Burgenland, is its best-known home.
- Germany commonly calls it Lemberger.
- Hungary commonly calls it Kékfrankos.
- Its wines often have berry fruit, freshness, spice and firm structure.
- Climate, ripeness, extraction, oak and ageing can substantially change the style.
- Australian drinkers may find it on local or imported labels, restaurant lists and specialist retail shelves.

## What does Blaufränkisch taste like?

A typical Blaufränkisch wine may show red berries, dark berries or forest-berry flavours, along with fresh acidity and a lightly peppery or savoury spice character. Its colour can range from deep ruby to purple, and its tannins may feel noticeable or firm without necessarily being harsh.

That description is a starting point, not a tasting rule. A cooler-climate or gently made example may taste lively, fragrant and medium-bodied. A riper wine with more extraction and oak may feel darker, denser and more structured.

This combination of freshness and structure is part of Blaufränkisch’s appeal. It can have enough acidity to feel energetic at the table, while its fruit and tannin give it more shape than a very light red.

## Where is Blaufränkisch grown?

Blaufränkisch is strongly associated with Austria, particularly the Burgenland wine region. It is also established in Germany, where it is commonly known as Lemberger, and in Hungary, where it is known as Kékfrankos.

You may also encounter related local names in Slovakia, Czechia, Croatia and other parts of Central and south-eastern Europe. The same grape can therefore appear under different names depending on the country and its labelling traditions.

The name on the bottle tells you about the grape variety. It is separate from the geographic indication, which tells you where the grapes or wine come from, and from style descriptions chosen by the producer.

## Why does the style vary so much?

The grape variety provides potential, but it does not determine the finished wine on its own.

### Climate and ripeness

Blaufränkisch is a late-ripening variety. Growing conditions and harvest timing can affect its balance of fruit ripeness, acidity, alcohol and tannin. A cooler growing season may preserve more freshness, while greater ripeness can bring darker fruit and a fuller texture.

### Winemaking

The amount of contact between grape skins and juice affects colour and tannin. More extraction can create a darker, more structured wine; gentler handling may produce a fresher, lighter expression.

Oak can add flavours such as toast, cedar or spice and can change the wine’s texture. Wines made with little oak may show more direct fruit and varietal character. Ageing can gradually soften tannins and shift fruit flavours towards savoury, earthy or dried-fruit notes.

So it is better to think of Blaufränkisch as a range of wines than as one fixed taste.

## Why might it have another name?

The names **Blaufränkisch**, **Lemberger**, **Blauer Limberger** and **Kékfrankos** are important names for the same grape variety in relevant national and international references.

In Austria, Blaufränkisch is the familiar name. In Germany, Lemberger is commonly used, while Blauer Limberger appears in German classification references. In Hungary, Kékfrankos is the usual local name.

This does not mean that a Lemberger wine is a different grape from Blaufränkisch. It is a little like meeting the same person using different names in different countries: the label tradition changes, but the underlying variety is the same.

## How might Australians encounter Blaufränkisch?

Australian wine drinkers may come across Blaufränkisch in imported Austrian, German or Central European wines, on restaurant wine lists, through specialist retailers or in Australian-made examples.

Australian labels can identify the grape variety, but they may also include a geographic indication and additional producer style wording. These are different kinds of information:

- **Grape variety:** Blaufränkisch, the type of grape used.
- **Geographic indication:** the recognised place associated with the wine.
- **Style description:** wording that may describe the producer’s approach, vineyard or wine character.

An Australian Blaufränkisch does not have to taste exactly like an Austrian, German or Hungarian example. Different climates, sites, harvest decisions and winemaking choices can all shape the result.

## What food goes with Blaufränkisch?

Use the wine’s weight, acidity and tannin as your guide. Blaufränkisch can work well with savoury foods such as:

- grilled sausages;
- roast pork or chicken;
- duck;
- mushroom dishes;
- lentils and other savoury pulses;
- paprika- or pepper-seasoned food;
- moderately flavoured firm cheeses.

These are starting points rather than rules. A lighter wine may suit simply seasoned food, while a fuller, more tannic example can handle richer or more strongly flavoured dishes.

## How should you serve it?

Try serving Blaufränkisch slightly cool rather than warm:

- fresher, lighter examples: about **14–16°C**;
- fuller, more structured examples: about **16–18°C**.

A standard red-wine glass is sufficient. A young wine with firm tannin may benefit from a little time in the glass or brief aeration.

As always, alcohol affects people differently. Follow Australian alcohol-guideline advice, and never drive after drinking.

## The simple takeaway

Blaufränkisch is a Central European red grape variety with a useful balance of fruit, freshness and structure. Look for berry flavours, spice and noticeable acidity and tannin, but expect variation. If you see Lemberger or Kékfrankos on a label, you may still be looking at the same grape under its local name.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Blaufränkisch a grape or a wine region?

It is a dark-skinned red grape variety. A wine region or geographic indication describes place; Blaufränkisch describes the grape.

### Is Blaufränkisch the same as Lemberger?

Yes, in the relevant variety references, Lemberger is a recognised name for Blaufränkisch. Lemberger is especially associated with Germany.

### Is Kékfrankos the same grape?

Yes. Kékfrankos is the commonly used Hungarian name for Blaufränkisch.

### Is Blaufränkisch sweet?

Not necessarily. Most examples encountered as red table wines are dry, but dry wine can still taste fruity. Fruit flavour and sweetness are not the same thing.

### Is Blaufränkisch similar to Pinot Noir or Shiraz?

It can share qualities with both, depending on the wine, but it is neither. Blaufränkisch commonly brings berry fruit, acidity and tannin in a combination that varies with region and winemaking.
