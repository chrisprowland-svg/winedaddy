# Single-vineyard wine: what does it mean?

## Highlights

- A single-vineyard wine is made from grapes sourced from one identified vineyard or vineyard site.
- The term describes **where the grapes came from**, not whether the wine is better.
- It does not automatically tell you the grape variety, wine colour, ownership, winemaking method, price or ageing potential.
- One vineyard can contain several blocks, soil types, elevations, varieties and vine materials.
- A single-vineyard wine is not necessarily estate-grown or single-varietal.
- In Australia, “single vineyard” is a voluntary label claim that must be honest, accurate and capable of substantiation.
- A vineyard name is not automatically an Australian Geographic Indication.

## Quick answer: what is a single-vineyard wine?

A single-vineyard wine is made from grapes sourced from one identified vineyard or vineyard site.

The vineyard may be named on the label as a property, site, block or named vineyard. The exact wording matters: these terms can be used differently by producers, so the best interpretation comes from the producer’s documentation and the applicable labelling requirements.

The important point is that **single-vineyard describes grape origin**. It does not guarantee a particular flavour, quality level, grape variety, wine colour, production method, ownership structure, price or ability to age.

A single-vineyard wine may be bottled separately because the producer wants to show a particular source, keep a small lot apart, preserve a vineyard’s identity, compare sites or support a premium range. None of those reasons proves that the wine is objectively better than a regional wine or a blend.

## What does “single vineyard” tell you?

It tells you that the grapes are claimed to come from one identified vineyard or site rather than from several vineyard sources.

That can make the wine interesting to compare with a broader regional wine or a multi-vineyard blend. The comparison may show how the producer’s choice of source, vintage and winemaking affects the finished wine.

But a vineyard is not necessarily one perfectly uniform patch of land. It may contain different blocks, slopes, elevations, aspects, soil depths, drainage conditions, varieties, clones, rootstocks, planting dates and vine-vigour zones.

This is why “single vineyard” should be understood as a source description, not a promise that every part of the vineyard behaves in exactly the same way.

## What does it not guarantee?

A single-vineyard label does not automatically mean:

- the wine is superior;
- the wine is rare, expensive or more ageworthy;
- the vineyard contains old vines;
- the wine is made with minimal intervention;
- the wine is single-varietal;
- the wine comes from the same owner as the winery;
- every vintage will taste the same.

The finished wine is influenced by seasonal weather, farming, harvest timing, fruit selection and winemaking. Fermentation, maturation, oak, blending and other cellar decisions can emphasise, soften or obscure differences from the vineyard.

So a careful description is that a single-vineyard wine **may be intended to show something about its site**. It is not accurate to say that the site alone determines the wine’s flavour.

## Single-vineyard compared with related terms

### Single-vineyard versus estate-grown

These terms describe different ideas.

**Single-vineyard** concerns the source of the grapes: one identified vineyard or site.

**Estate-grown** generally concerns an ownership, control or integrated production relationship. Its precise meaning should be checked in context, because the evidence reviewed does not establish one complete, legally fixed Australian consumer definition.

A wine can be single-vineyard without being estate-grown. It can also be estate-grown while drawing fruit from more than one vineyard block or source. For more detail, see our guide to [estate-grown wine](/estate-grown-wine/).

### Single-vineyard versus single-varietal

A **single-varietal wine** is labelled around one grape variety, subject to applicable rules. It may come from several vineyards.

A single-vineyard wine may also be single-varietal, but it does not have to be. One vineyard can contain more than one variety, and a wine’s variety claim is a separate piece of information. Read more about [single-varietal wine](/single-varietal-wine/).

### Single-vineyard versus a blend

A multi-vineyard wine uses grapes or finished wine from more than one vineyard source. That does not make it inferior. Blending can be a deliberate way to create balance, consistency or a particular style.

A single-vineyard wine is defined by its source, while a [wine blend](/wine-blends/) is defined by combining sources, varieties or finished wines.

### Single-vineyard versus a region or GI

An Australian Geographic Indication, or GI, is a formally recognised geographic area recorded in Wine Australia’s protected register. It may be a region, zone or subregion.

A named vineyard, block or property is different. It may identify a grape source without being a protected GI. In other words, a vineyard name is not automatically a regional designation. Learn more about [Australian Geographic Indications](/australian-geographic-indications/) and [protected wine names](/protected-wine-names/).

## Australian label context

For Australian readers, Wine Australia treats “single vineyard” as a voluntary label claim. The claim must be honest, accurate and capable of substantiation. Wine Australia specifically warns that the description may mislead if the fruit was not sourced from a single vineyard.

This is different from saying that “single vineyard” is a protected Australian GI. It is not presented in the reviewed Australian guidance as a universal protected classification in the same way as a registered geographic indication.

When reading a label, separate the different claims:

- **vineyard, site or block name:** the claimed grape source;
- **GI or region:** the defined geographic area, where shown;
- **variety:** the grape or grapes used;
- **vintage:** the harvest year, where stated;
- **producer or address:** information about the business, not necessarily the grape origin.

A producer-defined name can be useful, but it should not be treated as proof of a protected regional status. For a broader guide, see [how to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/).

## Why can one vineyard produce different wines?

Vintage variation is one of the most useful ideas to keep in mind.

Weather can change from year to year, affecting flowering, yield, ripeness and harvest timing. Within the vineyard, slope, elevation, aspect, soil depth, drainage, water availability and vine vigour can also vary.

A vineyard may contain different varieties, clones or rootstocks. Farming choices such as pruning, irrigation, canopy management, crop thinning and selective picking add further differences. Winemaking then shapes how those grapes become wine.

Australian research on vineyards in places including the Barossa and the Grampians demonstrates that site, soil, topography, climate, vine performance and management can interact. Research does not support reducing wine character to soil alone.

This is also why a single-vineyard wine from one vintage should not be treated as a permanent flavour template for that vineyard.

## How to approach a single-vineyard wine

When you see the term on a label, ask:

1. What exactly is named: a vineyard, block, property, site or region?
2. Is a recognised GI also listed?
3. What variety and vintage are shown?
4. Does the producer explain the source or the reason for bottling it separately?
5. How does it compare with the producer’s broader regional or multi-vineyard wine?
6. Are descriptions presented as possibilities shaped by site and vintage, rather than guarantees?

You do not need to assume that a single-vineyard wine is better. Instead, treat it as an invitation to ask a more specific question: **what might this particular source contribute, in this particular year, under this particular winemaking approach?**
