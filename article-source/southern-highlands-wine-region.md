# Southern Highlands wine region: a guide to its wines, climate and place

The Southern Highlands is a protected Australian wine region in New South Wales. It sits within the South Coast wine zone and is best understood as an elevated, cool-climate region around Bowral, Mittagong, Moss Vale and Berrima.

The region is known for fresh, often aromatic wines made from varieties including Chardonnay, Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Sauvignon. Pinot Noir and other varieties also contribute to its developing identity. However, there is no single “Southern Highlands style”: elevation, frost exposure, rainfall, soil, vintage and winemaking choices can all change the result.

## Highlights
- Southern Highlands is the official protected wine GI name.
- It is a region within the South Coast wine zone of New South Wales.
- The GI was entered in Australia’s Register of Protected Names on 25 March 2002.
- Bowral, Mittagong, Moss Vale and Berrima are useful places for understanding where the region is, but they are not interchangeable with the legal GI.
- Cool, moist and moderately continental conditions help explain the region’s freshness and vintage variation.
- Chardonnay, Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Sauvignon are the principal documented varieties.
- A Southern Highlands label identifies regional origin, not one guaranteed flavour profile.

## Where is the Southern Highlands wine region?

The Southern Highlands is inland from the NSW South Coast, on and around the Great Dividing Range. Bowral is the most useful regional centre for general orientation, with Mittagong and Moss Vale nearby. Berrima, Robertson, Bundanoon, Sutton Forest, Exeter, Wingello and Canyonleigh are other familiar highland localities.

From Sydney, Bowral is approximately 90 minutes to 1.5 hours south-west by road in favourable conditions, generally via the M31. That is a practical orientation rather than a precise travel promise: traffic, route and your final destination will affect the journey.

### The Southern Highlands GI and its bottle name

In Australian wine terminology, the Southern Highlands is a **Geographical Indication**, or GI. It is officially classified as a **region** within the **South Coast zone**, in **New South Wales, Australia**.

The legal GI boundary is based mainly on the proclaimed boundaries of the former Shire of Wingecarribee, together with detailed map-grid and cadastral descriptions. That matters because the everyday idea of the Southern Highlands can be broader than the legally defined wine region.

A bottle labelled **Southern Highlands** is using the name of the protected wine GI, subject to Australian labelling rules. Bowral, Moss Vale or Berrima may help you understand where the wine comes from, but a town name is not automatically the applicable legal wine GI.

## Why is the region cool and varied?

The Southern Highlands combines elevation, latitude and a moderately continental climate. Wine Australia describes it as cool, moist, slightly humid and moderately continental, with mild summers and cool winters.

The region’s landscape varies considerably. Wine Australia gives a broad altitude range of approximately 54 to 906 metres, while describing vineyards as reaching about 700 metres. The general highland setting is often described as being between roughly 500 and 900 metres. These figures describe different parts of the region, not a single elevation shared by every vineyard.

For wine drinkers, the important point is simpler: vineyards occupy different slopes, elevations and exposures. One site may ripen fruit differently from another only a short distance away.

### Frost is part of the story

Cool conditions can help grapes retain acidity and aromatic freshness, but they also bring risk. Frost is a significant viticultural hazard, particularly during the growing season. Hail, birds, animal damage and fungal diseases including downy mildew, powdery mildew and botrytis can also affect vineyards.

This is one reason vintage matters in the Southern Highlands. A cool region does not automatically produce better wine; it creates particular opportunities and challenges that growers and winemakers must manage.

### Rainfall and soils vary

Rainfall is not uniform across the GI. Wine Australia describes a substantial difference between wetter eastern areas and drier locations such as Joadja. It records approximately 1,664 millimetres in the east and approximately 832 millimetres at Joadja in its regional description, while a separate regional snapshot gives 569 millimetres of growing-season rainfall.

These figures are different measures and locations, not one regional annual average. They illustrate why it is misleading to treat the Southern Highlands as climatically identical from one end to the other.

Soils include basalt-derived red and brown krasnozems, red earths, shale-derived red and brown podzolics, brown earths and some sandstone-derived yellow earths. In practical terms, soil type affects drainage, fertility and vine growth, but it does not dictate the final taste of a wine by itself. Vineyard decisions and the season remain important.

## How did winemaking develop there?

Wine Australia records early table-grape growing and modest wine production, including approximately 4,300 litres in 1886. Viticulture then remained limited for much of the early twentieth century.

The region’s modern wine history dates from renewed development in the 1980s. This gives the Southern Highlands an interesting character: it has historical links to grape growing, but its contemporary identity is comparatively young and still diverse.

## Which grape varieties are important?

Wine Australia identifies Chardonnay, Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Sauvignon as the principal documented varieties of the Southern Highlands. Pinot Noir and alternative varieties are also relevant to the regional conversation, although the available evidence does not justify ranking them above the principal varieties.

### Chardonnay

Southern Highlands Chardonnay can range from fresh, unwooded expressions to wines given time in oak. Regional descriptions identify melon, lime, citrus and sometimes green-apple characters, supported by good acidity.

That makes Chardonnay a useful starting point if you enjoy wines with freshness but do not want to assume that every cool-climate Chardonnay will be thin or austere. Oak, lees work, harvest timing and texture can make the style broader and more savoury.

### Riesling

Riesling is described as showing promise in the region. Young examples may offer floral and citrus complexity, while bottle age can bring honeyed and toasty notes.

The key lesson is that “fresh” does not necessarily mean “simple” or “only for drinking immediately”. Acidity can help some Rieslings develop over time, although the ageing potential of an individual bottle depends on its wine, vintage and storage.

### Sauvignon Blanc

Southern Highlands Sauvignon Blanc is associated with good acidity and can show light smoky and grassy characters. It is better understood as a cool-area Australian style than as a guaranteed formula of tropical fruit or intense herbaceousness.

### Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon tends to perform better in warmer years, when the fruit has more opportunity to ripen fully. Regional descriptions point towards medium-bodied wines with berry and briar characters, particularly from younger vines.

This is a variety where vintage can be especially useful context. A warmer season may produce a rounder, more generous Cabernet, while a cooler season may result in a lighter and more restrained wine. Neither outcome is automatically preferable.

### Pinot Noir and other varieties

Pinot Noir adds to the region’s cool-climate diversity and can produce lithe, succulent expressions. Alternative varieties, including Arneis, have also been associated with experimentation in the wider regional conversation.

Treat these as evidence that the Southern Highlands is not limited to four grapes, rather than as proof of a dominant regional ranking.

## What do Southern Highlands wines taste like?

A useful broad description is **fresh, cool-climate and varied**, rather than one fixed flavour profile.

You may find:

- noticeable acidity in white wines;
- citrus, floral, grassy or lightly smoky aromatic characters;
- medium-bodied Cabernet with berry and briar notes;
- Chardonnay ranging from crisp and unwooded to lightly oaked and more textured;
- differences between sites, vintages and winemaking approaches.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. A regional label tells you about geographical origin. It does not promise that every bottle will be lean, aromatic, elegant, oaky or powerful.

The best way to interpret the region is to separate four influences:

1. **Region:** the broad cool, elevated and moist highland environment.
2. **Site:** elevation, slope, aspect, soil, drainage and frost exposure.
3. **Vintage:** the conditions of that particular growing season.
4. **Producer choices:** harvest date, fermentation, oak, lees contact and maturation.

That framework is more useful than trying to memorise one “typical” Southern Highlands taste.

## Southern Highlands and Shoalhaven Coast: what is the difference?

Shoalhaven Coast is a **separate GI**, although both regions sit within the South Coast wine zone of New South Wales.

In broad terms, Southern Highlands is the more inland, elevated and moderately continental environment. Shoalhaven Coast has a stronger coastal and maritime context. This is a useful starting comparison, not a rule that every Southern Highlands wine will taste cooler or every Shoalhaven Coast wine warmer.

The distinction also matters when reading a label. The two names represent separate protected geographical indications, not two interchangeable descriptions of one region.

## How should you buy or serve a Southern Highlands wine?

If you are choosing from a shelf, start with the variety and the style you enjoy:

- Choose Chardonnay if you want to compare fresh, citrus-led wines with more textured or oaked examples.
- Choose Riesling for aromatic freshness and the possibility of developing honeyed characters with age.
- Choose Sauvignon Blanc for acidity with grassy or lightly smoky notes.
- Choose Cabernet Sauvignon when you are interested in a medium-bodied red and can check the vintage for additional context.

For a first taste, serve whites cool but not painfully cold, so their aroma and texture remain visible. A medium-bodied Cabernet usually benefits from being served slightly below typical room temperature, especially in a warm Australian home. Tasting two Southern Highlands wines made from different varieties is often more revealing than looking for one definitive regional style.

## Southern Highlands wine region FAQs

### Is Southern Highlands a wine GI?

Yes. Southern Highlands is a protected Australian wine GI classified as a region within the South Coast wine zone of New South Wales.

### What is the Southern Highlands known for?

It is associated with cool-climate wines made from Chardonnay, Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Sauvignon. Pinot Noir and other varieties also contribute to its diversity.

### Is Bowral the Southern Highlands wine region?

No. Bowral is an important regional centre and a useful point of orientation, but the Southern Highlands GI covers a legally defined area that should not be reduced to one town.

### Is Shoalhaven Coast part of Southern Highlands?

No. Shoalhaven Coast is a separate GI, although both regions are within the South Coast wine zone.

### Are Southern Highlands wines always light and crisp?

No. Cool conditions often support freshness and acidity, but wine style also depends on grape variety, site, vintage and winemaking. Cabernet and oaked Chardonnay, for example, can offer more body and texture.
