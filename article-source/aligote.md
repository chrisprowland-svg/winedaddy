# What Is Aligoté? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Aligoté is a distinct white wine grape variety.
- It is not another name for Chardonnay, although the two grapes are genetic siblings.
- Its most recognisable feature is lively, naturally high acidity.
- Wines can show citrus, apple, green pear, white flowers and herbal notes.
- Aligoté is commonly made as a dry white wine, but it can also be used for sparkling wines and blends.
- Burgundy is its historic home, while the grape is also grown in parts of Eastern Europe and Australia.
- Australian examples are uncommon, so the style can vary considerably from one bottle to another.

## Quick answer: what is Aligoté?

Aligoté is a white wine-grape variety, best known for producing fresh, high-acid white wines. It is strongly associated with Burgundy in France, where it is used for Bourgogne Aligoté and the Bouzeron appellation. It is also grown in countries including Moldova, Ukraine, Romania and Russia, as well as in small Australian plantings.

In the glass, Aligoté often feels lively and refreshing rather than broad or heavy. Commonly reported flavours include lemon, apple, green pear, white flowers and herbs. However, these are useful starting points, not a fixed recipe: climate, ripeness, site and winemaking can all change the result.

You may encounter Aligoté as a dry still white wine, a sparkling wine or part of a blend. Its acidity is one reason it works well in sparkling production and can add freshness to a blend.

## Aligoté is not Chardonnay

Aligoté and Chardonnay are separate grape varieties. They are related, however: DNA evidence identifies both as offspring of Pinot Noir and Gouais Blanc. In other words, they are genetic siblings, not different names for the same grape.

That distinction matters when reading a label. **Bourgogne Aligoté** refers to a protected wine appellation made from Aligoté; it does not mean Chardonnay, and it is not an alternative name for the grape.

Aligoté also has recorded synonyms and may appear without its accent as **Aligote**. These spellings refer to the same grape identity. The canonical spelling is Aligoté, but the unaccented form is worth recognising when shopping or reading international wine information.

## Where is Aligoté grown?

Aligoté is an old north-eastern French variety strongly associated with Burgundy. It is believed to have originated in or near the Hautes-Côtes, although that origin is described cautiously rather than as an absolute certainty.

In Burgundy, Bourgogne Aligoté is a recognised appellation. The variety is also important in parts of Eastern Europe, including Moldova, Ukraine, Romania and Russia. Much of the available distribution evidence is historical, so these countries should be understood as established growing contexts rather than a current ranking of the world’s largest producers.

Australia has had Aligoté since the 1960s. It remains a small alternative variety, with industry references identifying plantings or examples in cool-climate areas such as Victoria’s King Valley and Mornington Peninsula. Australian-grown Aligoté should not automatically be assumed to taste like a bottle from Burgundy: local climate, vineyard site and winemaking make a difference.

## What does Aligoté taste like?

The best beginner’s cue is **freshness driven by acidity**. Acidity is the sensation that makes wine feel lively and mouth-watering; you may recognise it from the tang of lemon.

Aligoté is often light- to medium-bodied, with flavours or aromas described as:

- lemon and other citrus;
- apple and green pear;
- white flowers;
- fresh herbs;
- white-fleshed fruit; and
- sometimes saline, mineral or nutty notes.

Not every bottle will show all of these characteristics. A riper or more textural wine may feel rounder, while lees ageing or oak maturation can add weight and texture.

A useful misconception to avoid is that **dry means flavourless**. Dryness describes the amount of residual sugar in the finished wine; it does not prevent a wine from tasting of fruit, flowers or herbs. Bourgogne Aligoté is generally a dry white style, but sweetness is determined by the finished wine and winemaking, not by the grape name alone.

## How might you serve it?

Aligoté is generally approached as a fresh white wine and is best served chilled. Its acidity can work well with lighter foods such as seafood, salads and fresh cheeses, although the right match depends on the dish and the wine’s particular style.

Aligoté is also traditionally associated with **Kir**, a Burgundy drink combining white wine with crème de cassis. That association does not mean Aligoté itself is sweet.

When choosing a bottle, look for **Aligoté** or **Aligote** on the label. If it is Australian-grown, expect the possibility of a distinctive local expression rather than a precise copy of a French or Eastern European style.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Aligoté a white or red grape?

Aligoté is a white wine grape.

### Is Aligoté the same as Chardonnay?

No. They are distinct varieties, although both descend from Pinot Noir and Gouais Blanc.

### Is Aligoté always acidic?

High acidity is its strongest recurring characteristic, but the way that acidity feels can change with climate, ripeness and winemaking.

### Is Aligoté always dry?

Many classic examples, especially Bourgogne Aligoté, are dry. But the grape itself does not determine the sweetness of every finished wine.

### Can Aligoté be sparkling?

Yes. Its acidity makes it suitable for sparkling wine production, including sparkling wines made in Burgundy and Eastern Europe.
