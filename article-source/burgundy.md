# Burgundy: A Beginner’s Guide to the French Wine Region

## Highlights

Burgundy is a wine region in east-central France—not a grape variety. Its French name is **Bourgogne**, and it is best known for wines made principally from **Pinot Noir** and **Chardonnay**.

<!-- VISUAL:VIS-005 -->

The region runs broadly from the Chablis area and Grand Auxerrois in the north, through the Côte de Nuits, Côte de Beaune and Côte Chalonnaise, to the Mâconnais in the south. Burgundy is not one uniform wine style. It is a layered system of places, appellations, vineyard sites, producers, vintages and winemaking decisions.

For an Australian wine learner, the biggest adjustment is simple: Burgundy labels often lead with **where the wine comes from**, while Australian labels commonly lead with **which grape variety was used**.

## The highlights

- Burgundy is a French wine region, also called Bourgogne.
- Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are its principal grape varieties.
- The region is organised around legally defined appellations and place names.
- Burgundy labels may say Chablis or Meursault rather than Chardonnay.
- Premier Cru and Grand Cru describe classified origins, but neither guarantees that every wine will suit every drinker.
- Burgundy stretches north to south, so its climate, geology and wine styles vary.
- Australian varietal labelling and French appellation labelling are related ideas, but they are not interchangeable.

## Where is Burgundy?

Burgundy is a long, north–south wine region in east-central France. Useful orientation points include Auxerre and Chablis in the north; Dijon, Nuits-Saint-Georges and Beaune through the central Côte d’Or; Chalon-sur-Saône farther south; and Mâcon at the southern end.

Dijon is a useful broad reference for the northern and central wine areas, while Beaune is a familiar practical centre for the Côte d’Or corridor. From Lyon, Burgundy’s central areas are roughly two hours north by road, depending on the destination and route. That is only a broad orientation, not a dependable travel estimate.

For wider French context, see our guide to [France](/france/).

### Burgundy’s main wine areas

From north to south, the principal areas are:

- **Chablis and the Grand Auxerrois:** the northern end of the wider Burgundy wine region.
- **Côte de Nuits:** a central Côte d’Or area particularly associated with Pinot Noir, although it is not exclusively red-wine territory.
- **Côte de Beaune:** another Côte d’Or area with both red and white appellations.
- **Côte Chalonnaise:** between the Côte de Beaune and Mâconnais.
- **Mâconnais:** the southernmost major Burgundy wine area, with an important Chardonnay tradition.

Each of these is a destination in its own right. You can learn more in the guides to [Chablis](/chablis/), [Côte de Nuits](/cote-de-nuits/), [Côte de Beaune](/cote-de-beaune/), [Côte Chalonnaise](/cote-chalonnaise/) and the [Mâconnais](/maconnais/).

These names describe geographic areas, but they do not all function in exactly the same way on a label. A town, subregion, appellation and vineyard site can be related without being interchangeable.

## Burgundy is a place-led wine system

Many Australian drinkers first learn wine through labels such as “Pinot Noir”, “Chardonnay” or “Shiraz”. The grape is often the most prominent piece of information.

Burgundy often works in the opposite direction. A bottle may lead with a place or appellation:

- **Bourgogne** is broad regional appellation wording.
- **Chablis** identifies a northern Burgundy appellation.
- **Meursault** identifies a village appellation.
- **Meursault Premier Cru** adds a Premier Cru designation and usually a named climat.
- A **Grand Cru** name identifies a specially classified site and its own appellation.

A Burgundy label may still contain the producer, vintage, grape-related information and other required details. But the place name is often the key to understanding the wine.

This does not mean that “Chablis” is a grape variety. It is a place-based appellation whose wines are principally associated with Chardonnay. Likewise, Meursault is not another name for Chardonnay; it is an appellation where the grape is generally inferred from the appellation rules and tradition.

For the broader idea, read [French AOC and AOP](/french-aoc-and-aop/) and [protected wine names](/protected-wine-names/).

## The Burgundy appellation hierarchy

Burgundy’s hierarchy is a way of expressing increasing geographic specificity and legally recognised classification. It is useful, but it should not be treated as a simple scorecard.

### Regional appellations

Regional appellations cover broader areas. **Bourgogne** is the most familiar example. Other regional names can identify a wider Burgundy territory or a defined regional area, sometimes with additional information about grape or colour.

### Village appellations

Village appellations are associated with a particular commune or village area, such as Chablis or Meursault. The legally defined area is more specific than a broad regional appellation, but the wines can still vary between sites, producers and vintages.

### Premier Cru

A Premier Cru is generally a named **climat** within a village appellation. In Burgundy, a climat is a historically recognised, named vineyard site or parcel—not simply a description of the weather.

Premier Cru therefore adds information about the classified origin of the grapes. It is usually not a separate appellation tier in exactly the same sense as Grand Cru.

### Grand Cru

Grand Cru appellations are associated with specifically classified sites and have their own specifications. The site name is central to the wine’s legal identity.

The important qualification is that hierarchy does not guarantee a particular flavour, quality level or personal enjoyment. A higher classification may indicate a more specific and historically recognised origin, but the finished wine is also shaped by vintage, farming, producer decisions, winemaking and storage.

## Which grapes matter?

### Pinot Noir

Pinot Noir is Burgundy’s principal red grape. Red Burgundy is principally Pinot Noir-based and can show red-fruit character, freshness, acidity and fine or firm tannin. Some wines may feel light on their feet; others can be more structured, concentrated or powerful.

There is no single Burgundy Pinot Noir style. Regional, village, Premier Cru and Grand Cru wines can differ, as can wines from different producers and vintages. For grape fundamentals, see [what Pinot Noir is](/what-is-pinot-noir/).

### Chardonnay

Chardonnay is Burgundy’s principal white grape. It is central to Chablis and important through the Côte de Beaune, Côte Chalonnaise and Mâconnais.

White Burgundy can range from lean and fresh to rounded, textured or oak-influenced. Citrus, orchard-fruit, floral, saline or stony impressions may occur, but no single descriptor applies to every appellation.

Our guide to [what Chardonnay is](/what-is-chardonnay/) provides the broader grape explanation.

### Other varieties

Burgundy is not limited to Pinot Noir and Chardonnay. **Aligoté** has its own regional appellation, Bourgogne Aligoté, and is also relevant to Crémant de Bourgogne. **Gamay** matters in specific southern and regional appellations. **César** has a particularly qualified association with Irancy, while **Sauvignon** is relevant to appellations such as Saint-Bris.

Pinot Blanc, Pinot Gris, Sacy and other varieties also have specific or historical roles. They are useful reminders that Burgundy is more varied than its two famous grapes, but they should not replace Pinot Noir and Chardonnay in a beginner’s first definition.

## Why Burgundy wines vary

Burgundy is often discussed through *terroir*: the interaction of place, climate, geology, soil, vines and human work. It is a useful framework, provided it is not turned into a promise.

Across Burgundy, latitude, continental climate, slope, aspect, frost risk, ripening conditions and geology vary. Farming choices, harvest timing, fermentation, maturation and the producer’s decisions also influence the finished wine.

That means it is safer to say that a site or appellation **can contribute to** a wine’s character than to say that one soil type automatically creates one flavour. A limestone site does not produce a guaranteed taste in isolation, and a Grand Cru does not have one fixed personality.

Read more about [terroir](/what-is-terroir/) and [how climate affects wine](/how-climate-affects-wine/).

## How to compare Burgundy with Australian wine

The most useful comparison is not “which country makes better wine?” It is how the label asks you to think.

An Australian label might make the grape variety prominent:

> Chardonnay — region — producer

A Burgundy label may make the place prominent:

> Meursault — producer — vintage

The Burgundy example does not necessarily state Chardonnay as the main identity. The drinker is expected to recognise that the appellation has rules and traditions associated with particular grapes.

Australian geographical indications and French AOC/AOP systems both connect wine with place, but the terminology and legal structures are not interchangeable. An Australian region name should not be assumed to work exactly like a French village appellation or classified climat.

Australian Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are still useful comparison points. They can help you notice differences in body, acidity, texture, oak and fruit character. But the purpose is understanding labels and styles—not ranking one wine tradition against another.

## A practical way to read a Burgundy label

When you encounter Burgundy in Australia, try this sequence:

<!-- VISUAL:VIS-006 -->

1. **Look for the place or appellation.** Is it Bourgogne, a village name, a Premier Cru designation or a Grand Cru name?
2. **Identify the colour and likely principal grape.** Red Burgundy is principally associated with Pinot Noir; white Burgundy is principally associated with Chardonnay, subject to appellation rules.
3. **Separate the site from the producer.** The place name tells you about origin; the producer’s choices still matter.
4. **Treat the hierarchy as information, not a guarantee.** More specific classification does not automatically mean a wine you will prefer.
5. **Expect variation.** Vintage, producer, farming, winemaking and storage all affect the bottle.

Burgundy becomes less mysterious when you stop trying to memorise every name at once. Start with the north–south map, the two principal grapes and the difference between a grape-led Australian label and a place-led Burgundy label.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Burgundy a grape?

No. Burgundy is a wine region in France. Its French name is Bourgogne.

### Is all Burgundy made from Pinot Noir?

No. Pinot Noir is the principal red grape, but Chardonnay is the principal white grape. Other varieties, including Aligoté, Gamay, César and Sauvignon, are relevant in particular appellations.

### Does Chablis come from Burgundy?

Yes. Chablis is a northern Burgundy wine area with its own appellations. It should not be treated as a grape variety.

### Does Premier Cru always mean better wine?

It indicates a recognised classification and more specific origin, but it does not guarantee quality, style or personal preference.

### Why does a Burgundy label sometimes not name the grape?

Burgundy commonly uses place-led and appellation-led naming. The principal grape may be inferred from the appellation rules and regional convention.

### Is Beaujolais the same as Burgundy?

Beaujolais is treated separately in this guide’s regional structure. It should not be used as a synonym for Burgundy’s appellation system.
