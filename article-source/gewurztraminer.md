# What Is Gewürztraminer? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Gewürztraminer is a grape variety, not a synonym for sweet white wine.
- Its wines are often intensely floral and perfumed.
- Rose, lychee, apricot, tropical fruit, ginger and spice are common reference points.
- The wine may be dry, off-dry or sweet.
- Fruit aromas can make a dry wine seem sweet, even when it contains little residual sugar.
- Gewürztraminer is especially associated with Alsace in France and is also grown in Australia.
- It is a different grape from Riesling, although both are aromatic white varieties.

## Gewürztraminer in a nutshell

Gewürztraminer is an aromatic white wine-grape variety. Its wines are known for pronounced perfume, often showing rose, lychee, ripe fruit and spice.

The important thing to remember is that **aromatic does not mean sweet**. Gewürztraminer can make dry, off-dry or sweet wines, depending on how the wine is made and how much residual sugar remains after fermentation.

In Australia, Wine Australia’s register uses **Gewurztraminer** as the prime name and lists **Gewürztraminer** as a synonym. You may therefore see the name written with or without the umlaut.

## What does Gewürztraminer taste like?

Gewürztraminer is one of the easier wines to recognise by aroma. A glass may suggest rose petals, lychee, apricot, pineapple, passionfruit or other ripe fruit. Ginger, pepper and sweet spice can also appear.

These are typical descriptors, not a checklist that every bottle must match. Climate, ripeness, winemaking, residual sugar, bottle age and serving temperature all influence the finished wine.

The palate is often broad, rounded and richly flavoured rather than sharply lean. Gewürztraminer commonly feels less piercingly acidic than Riesling, but this is a style tendency rather than a rule. Some examples are lighter and fresher; others feel fuller and more textured.

A gently bitter or phenolic note may also appear towards the finish. That can be part of the variety’s character, particularly in strongly flavoured, ripe examples.

## Is Gewürztraminer sweet?

Not necessarily. Gewürztraminer may be:

- **Dry:** little perceptible sweetness from residual sugar.
- **Off-dry:** some noticeable sweetness, but not necessarily dessert-sweet.
- **Sweet:** a clearly sweet style with more residual sugar.

A dry Gewürztraminer can still smell intensely of lychee, ripe pear, flowers or tropical fruit. Those aromas create an impression of sweetness, but they do not prove that the wine contains much sugar.

This is a useful distinction when choosing wine: **fruitiness describes aroma and flavour, while sweetness describes the wine’s sugar and its perception on the palate**. Acidity, alcohol, bitterness, temperature and texture also affect how sweet a wine seems.

If you want to know whether a particular bottle is dry or sweet, look for a sweetness description on the label or ask whoever is serving it. The grape name alone cannot answer that question.

> **Visual opportunity:** VIS-002, “Dry does not mean flavourless”, could support this distinction between aromatic intensity, fruitiness and residual sugar.

## Why is Gewürztraminer associated with Alsace?

Gewürztraminer has a particularly strong association with Alsace, in north-eastern France. The official French origin authority INAO identifies it as one of the principal grape varieties used for Alsace wines.

The grape name may appear on the label of relevant Alsace wines, and Gewürztraminer is also one of the permitted varieties for Alsace Grand Cru wines. That gives Alsace an important place in the variety’s identity, but it does not mean all Gewürztraminer comes from Alsace or that Alsace examples are always sweet.

Alsace is best understood as a major traditional reference point, not as another name for the grape.

## Is Gewürztraminer grown in Australia?

Yes. Gewürztraminer is an established, though relatively small, part of Australian grape production.

Wine Australia’s 2025 variety snapshot records **6,920 tonnes crushed in 2025**. Its reported crush distribution was concentrated in a few regions, including:

- Riverina: 62 per cent
- Padthaway: 24 per cent
- Murray Darling–Swan Hill VIC: 4 per cent
- Riverland: 2 per cent
- Currency Creek: 1 per cent
- Other regions: 6 per cent

These figures describe the reported 2025 crush, not a permanent ranking of Australian regions or a guarantee of flavour. The same snapshot cites **8 hectares** in a 2015 planting dataset, so the planting and crush figures come from different dates and should not be treated as one contemporaneous survey.

For an Australian drinker, the practical lesson is simple: Gewürztraminer can come from different growing environments, and regional origin does not determine one fixed style. A bottle may be dry or sweet, lighter or broader, and more floral or more fruit-driven.

## How should you serve Gewürztraminer?

Serve Gewürztraminer chilled, but not so cold that its perfume disappears. Its aroma is a major part of the experience, so give the wine a moment in the glass and notice what develops as it warms slightly.

A smaller pour can be useful if you are unfamiliar with the variety. First notice the aroma, then taste the wine and ask:

- Does it seem dry, off-dry or sweet?
- Is the acidity crisp or rounded?
- Do the floral and fruit aromas continue onto the palate?
- Is there a spicy or gently bitter note at the finish?

There is no need to identify every aroma correctly. “Floral”, “perfumed”, “ripe-fruited” or “spicy” are already useful observations.

## What food goes with Gewürztraminer?

Dry or off-dry Gewürztraminer can be a useful starting point for aromatic, spicy or salty food. Consider dishes with chilli, ginger, fragrant herbs or warming spices, including many Asian, Indian, North African and Latin American dishes.

Sweeter examples may work well with food that includes chilli heat, fruit, spice or a rich texture. The aim is not to follow a rigid matching rule. Choose a wine with enough freshness for the dish and enough flavour to avoid being overwhelmed by it.

## Is Gewürztraminer the same as Riesling?

No. Gewürztraminer and Riesling are separate grape varieties, although both are aromatic white grapes and are often discussed together.

Gewürztraminer is generally recognised for its floral, lychee and spice-led perfume, while Riesling is commonly associated with a more citrusy, floral and high-acid profile. That is only a brief orientation; the varieties can each produce a range of styles. ## Frequently asked questions

### How do you spell Gewürztraminer in Australia?

Both forms may appear. Wine Australia’s January 2024 register uses **Gewurztraminer** as the prime name and lists **Gewürztraminer** as a synonym. The umlaut spelling is widely recognisable in reader-facing wine education.

### Is Gewürztraminer always low in acidity?

No. It often feels less sharply acidic than Riesling, but acidity varies with climate, ripeness and winemaking.

### Is Gewürztraminer a dessert wine?

No. It can be dry, off-dry or sweet. The grape’s intense fruit and floral aromas should not be confused with residual sugar.

### Are Gewürztraminer and Savagnin the same grape?

Do not treat them as interchangeable. Historical Traminer and Savagnin terminology is complex, and the Australian register does not support simplifying all those names into direct synonyms.
