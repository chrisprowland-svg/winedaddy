# What is Pallagrello Nero?

## Highlights

- Pallagrello Nero is a black-skinned Italian wine grape.
- It is associated mainly with Campania, especially Caserta province.
- It makes red wines, often with deep colour and noticeable structure.
- Fruit, spice and savoury flavours are possible, but no bottle tastes exactly the same.
- Pallagrello Nero is separate from Pallagrello Bianco, despite the similar name.
- On a label, the grape name may appear alongside a geographical designation such as Terre del Volturno IGT.

## Quick answer

Pallagrello Nero is a black-skinned Italian grape variety used to make red wine. It is traditionally associated with Campania in southern Italy, particularly the hills of Caserta province around places such as Caiazzo, Castel Campagnano and Pontelatone.

The variety can produce deeply coloured, structured red wines with red or dark fruit, spice and savoury notes. Some examples may also show tobacco-like, herbal or liquorice characters. These are tendencies rather than guarantees: climate, vineyard site, ripeness, winemaking, maturation and blending all influence the finished wine.

The name matters because Pallagrello Nero is not the same grape as Pallagrello Bianco. The two varieties share part of their name, but Nero refers to the black-skinned red-wine grape and Bianco to a separate white-skinned variety.

## Where does Pallagrello Nero come from?

Pallagrello Nero’s strongest geographic association is with Campania, and particularly Caserta province. You may also see the grape discussed in connection with the wider Terre del Volturno area.

This is geographic context, not a claim that every vineyard or bottle belongs to one particular appellation. Italian wine labels can combine a grape name with a geographical indication. For example, a bottle may refer to Pallagrello Nero and Terre del Volturno IGT: the first identifies the grape, while the second identifies a regulated geographical designation.

The variety also has a historical connection with the royal vineyard at Caserta, known as the Vigna del Ventaglio. That history helps explain why the grape remains linked with the Caserta area, but it does not mean that every modern bottle is a “royal wine”.

## What does it taste like?

Pallagrello Nero can make a full-coloured, structured red wine. Depending on the wine, you might find flavours and aromas reminiscent of:

- red or dark berries;
- pepper and other spices;
- tobacco or liquorice; and
- earthy or savoury notes.

Tannin can be noticeable in some examples, giving the wine a drying, grippy sensation on the gums. Other wines may feel softer, depending on grape ripeness, extraction, ageing and whether the variety is blended with other grapes.

That variation is useful to remember when choosing a bottle. The grape name gives you a starting point, not a precise tasting promise. A younger, fruit-focused wine may feel very different from a more extracted or oak-matured example.

Structured versions can work naturally with hearty food, such as roasted or grilled meats, rich pasta dishes and mature cheeses. If the wine is lighter or softer, you may prefer it with less intense dishes.

## How might it appear on a label?

The clearest modern name is **Pallagrello Nero**. You may sometimes see “Pallagrello” used without the colour qualifier, but that can be ambiguous because Pallagrello may refer to Nero or Bianco.

The grape may appear as a single-variety wine or as part of a blend. A label may also show a designation such as **Terre del Volturno IGT**. Remember the distinction:

- **Pallagrello Nero** = the grape variety.
- **Terre del Volturno IGT** = a geographical indication.

For Australian readers, public evidence of significant Australian plantings or local production is limited. You are more likely to encounter the name through a specialist imported wine or a limited listing than as a familiar Australian variety. Australian wine-labelling guidance also requires grape names used on Australian labels to be recognised by an accepted reference organisation, although the detailed rules depend on the product and label.

## Is Pallagrello Nero a rare grape?

It is not helpful to call it “rare” without a current, reliable measure of vineyard area or production. A safer description is that it is a less familiar grape internationally than varieties such as Shiraz, Pinot Noir or Sangiovese.

If you see Pallagrello Nero on a list or label, expect an Italian red grape from the Campanian tradition—not a wine region, a sweetness level or a general term for any dark-coloured wine.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Pallagrello Nero the same as Pallagrello Bianco?

No. They are separate grape varieties. Pallagrello Nero is black-skinned and used for red wines; Pallagrello Bianco is white-skinned.

### Is Pallagrello Nero a region?

No. It is a grape variety. A bottle may show both the grape name and a regional designation.

### Is Pallagrello Nero always full-bodied and tannic?

No. It can produce structured wines, but body and tannin vary with site, ripeness, extraction, maturation and blending.
