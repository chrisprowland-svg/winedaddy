# Wine awards and medals: what do they mean?

## Highlights

- A wine medal is recognition from a particular competition, judged under that competition’s rules.
- Gold, silver and bronze do not have one universal meaning across every wine show.
- The result usually belongs to a specific wine entry, vintage, blend, class and judging year.
- A trophy is generally a further award selected from eligible class or medal winners.
- An award can be useful buying information, but it does not guarantee quality, value or personal enjoyment.
- Awards, critic scores, reviews, certifications and protected wine names are different things.
- On an Australian label, look for the competition name, award and year.

## Quick answer

Wine awards and medals tell you that a particular wine entry received recognition in a particular competition. The wine was assessed against other entries in a defined class, such as a grape variety, style, region or vintage, using that competition’s judging process.

That makes a medal useful evidence—but only within its proper limits. A Gold medal does not mean the wine is universally the best, that it will suit your taste, or that every later vintage or blend has received the same recognition.

Gold, silver and bronze are also competition-specific. The Australian Society of Viticulture and Oenology’s 2020 best-practice recommendations use Gold for 95 points or more, Silver for 90–94 and Bronze for 85–89. Some Australian shows publish similar bands, but these are not a universal legal or international standard. Always check the rules of the named competition.

## How wine competitions generally work

Wines are entered into classes so that judges compare reasonably similar entries. A class might be organised around variety, style, region, vintage, price band or another category defined by the show.

Judging commonly involves a panel. Judges may assess wines independently before discussing them, reaching a consensus or conducting further tastings. Some Australian shows describe blind or screened judging, while others may use different procedures. It is safer to check the rules of the individual show than to assume every competition works identically.

A medal is then awarded according to the competition’s criteria. A trophy is usually a further selection from eligible class or medal winners. For example, some shows restrict trophy judging to Gold medal wines, while others define eligibility differently. A trophy therefore indicates a higher-level result within that show’s structure, not automatically the best wine in Australia.

## What do Gold, Silver and Bronze mean?

In everyday language, Gold usually signals the highest medal level, followed by Silver and Bronze. But the exact standard belongs to the competition.

One show may use numerical thresholds. Another may rely more heavily on panel consensus, use different score bands or include additional awards. Even where two shows use similar point ranges, their classes, entry rules, judges and procedures may differ.

So read a medal as:

> “This entry met this competition’s criteria for this award.”

Do not automatically read it as:

> “This wine is objectively better than every wine without a medal.”

A medal is also not a guarantee that you will enjoy the wine. Your preferred sweetness, acidity, body, grape variety and occasion still matter.

## What exactly has won?

The award normally relates to the submitted entry—not necessarily every bottle made by the producer. Check the identity of the wine, its vintage, blend and label.

A medal may apply to:

- a particular vintage;
- a particular blend or bottling;
- a particular label identity;
- a defined class;
- the sample submitted for judging;
- a specific competition and judging year.

A later vintage may taste different and may not have been entered. A later re-blend may also be different from the wine that won. Australian industry guidance generally supports using an award only for the wine represented by the entry, unless the relevant show’s rules permit portability.

This is why “Gold medal, [show], [year]” is more informative than a vague “award-winning winery” claim.

## How to read an award claim on an Australian label

A useful award claim should make its provenance clear. Look for:

1. **Competition name** — Which show judged the wine?
2. **Award** — Was it Gold, Silver, Bronze, a trophy or another recognition?
3. **Award year** — When was the result received?
4. **Wine identity** — Does it refer to this wine and label?
5. **Vintage or blend** — Is the current bottle the same as the submitted entry?
6. **Class** — Was it judged among a particular variety, style, region or vintage group?
7. **Recipient** — Did the award belong to the wine, or to a producer, winemaker or exhibitor?

A collective award such as “Winemaker of the Year” should not be presented as though an individual wine won that award. Similarly, a generic medal shape without the show and year gives you less useful information.

Australian Consumer Law principles require advertising and product claims to be truthful, accurate and able to be substantiated. Wine Australia guidance also identifies separate requirements for claims such as vintage, variety and geographical indication. These are general explanations, not legal advice; businesses with a specific label or advertisement should check current official guidance.

## Awards versus scores, reviews and certifications

### Awards versus scores

A score is a numerical assessment. An award is a competition result. A score may help determine a medal, but not every score leads to a medal and different competitions use different scoring systems.

For numerical ratings, see [Wine scores explained](/wine-scores-explained/).

### Awards versus reviews

A review usually combines an assessment with written commentary from a critic, publication or reviewer. A competition medal records a result within a show’s rules. Both can help you learn about a wine, but neither guarantees personal enjoyment.

See [How to understand wine reviews](/how-to-understand-wine-reviews/).

### Awards versus certification

A medal is not certification or accreditation. It does not, by itself, certify organic production, sustainability, authenticity, safety, origin or a particular winemaking method.

### Awards versus protected wine names

A protected geographical or product name concerns the lawful use of a name or description. It is not a competition result. A wine can use a protected regional name without winning a medal, and a medal does not create protection for a wine name.

See [Protected wine names](/protected-wine-names/).

## Are wine medals worth trusting?

They can be worth considering as one piece of evidence. A medal tells you that judges rated a particular entry favourably under a particular system. That may help when you know little else about a bottle.

Use it alongside the wine’s style, vintage, price, storage, occasion and your own preferences. If you dislike full-bodied reds, a Gold medal will not make one a better choice for you. If the award is several years old, check whether the current release is the same wine.

The most confident approach is neither to dismiss medals nor to treat them as guarantees. Ask what was judged, by whom, when and under which rules.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a Gold medal mean a wine is excellent?

It means the wine met the Gold criteria of that particular competition. The criteria and judging process may differ elsewhere, so it is not a universal guarantee of excellence.

### Is a trophy better than a Gold medal?

A trophy is usually a further selection from eligible class or medal winners. Its meaning depends on the competition’s rules and category.

### Do medals apply to every vintage?

Not automatically. Check the vintage, blend and bottling. A later release may not be the same wine that was judged.

### Are medals the same as wine scores?

No. A score is a numerical assessment; a medal is a competition result. They may be connected, but they are not interchangeable.

### Does a medal prove a wine is good value?

No. Value depends on price, quality, suitability and personal priorities—not the medal alone.
