# What is Nerello Mascalese?

## Highlights

- Nerello Mascalese is a red wine grape variety, not a region or a wine style.
- It is best known from Sicily, especially the slopes of Mount Etna.
- Wines are often pale in colour but can still have noticeable acidity and firm tannin.
- Common flavours include red fruit, flowers, herbs, spice and savoury notes.
- Etna Rosso is a regional wine made predominantly from Nerello Mascalese, but not every Nerello Mascalese wine comes from Etna.
- Nerello Cappuccio is a separate grape that may be blended with it.
- Australian drinkers are most likely to encounter the name on Italian labels, wine lists or specialist alternative-variety selections.

## Quick answer

Nerello Mascalese is an Italian red wine grape variety, strongly associated with eastern Sicily and Mount Etna. It is used to make red, rosé and sparkling wines, either on its own or in blends.

The variety commonly produces wines with relatively pale ruby colour, fresh acidity and fine-to-firm tannin. You may notice red cherry and berry flavours, floral notes such as violet, Mediterranean herbs, spice or savoury characters. These are tendencies rather than a fixed tasting checklist: the final wine also depends on where the grapes are grown, the vintage and how the wine is made.

The grape is particularly important in Etna DOC wines. Under the current appellation rules, Etna Rosso and Etna Rosato must contain at least 80 per cent Nerello Mascalese. However, Nerello Mascalese is a grape, while Etna DOC is a regulated place-based appellation. They are related, but they are not the same thing.

## Where is Nerello Mascalese grown?

Nerello Mascalese is most closely associated with Sicily, the large Italian island in the Mediterranean. Its strongest regional connection is Mount Etna, an active volcano in eastern Sicily.

The vineyards around Etna vary considerably in elevation, exposure, climate and soil. That variation is one reason Etna wines can differ from one another. The word “volcanic” is useful geographical context, but it does not mean every wine will taste smoky, mineral or ashy. Local geology interacts with climate, vineyard conditions, vine age, yields and winemaking.

Etna Rosso is the best-known appellation expression of the grape. It is a red wine designation rather than another name for Nerello Mascalese. A bottle labelled Etna Rosso may not display the grape name prominently because Italian wines are often identified by their appellation.

The grape can also appear in broader Sicilian wine designations, including Sicilia DOC. A wine made with Nerello Mascalese is not automatically an Etna wine: it must meet the relevant Etna production rules to use that appellation.

## What does Nerello Mascalese taste like?

A useful way to approach Nerello Mascalese is to expect freshness and structure rather than a heavy, deeply coloured red.

### Colour and body

The wines can be pale to medium ruby in colour. That lighter appearance can be misleading. A wine may look delicate in the glass while still having plenty of acidity and tannin.

Body varies with ripeness, alcohol, extraction and maturation. Some examples are light to medium-bodied and elegant; others can feel more concentrated and substantial. It is better not to assume that every Nerello Mascalese wine will be light-bodied.

### Acidity

Nerello Mascalese is often marked by noticeable acidity. In practical terms, that can make the wine feel fresh, lively and food-friendly rather than soft or flat.

Acidity is not identical in every bottle. Elevation, climate, vintage and picking decisions can all affect the balance.

### Tannin

Tannin provides the drying or gently grippy sensation you may notice on your gums. Nerello Mascalese can have firm, persistent tannin, particularly when young or when the wine has received significant extraction.

That does not necessarily mean harshness. With ripe fruit, careful winemaking and time in maturation, the tannin may feel finer and more integrated.

### Fruit, floral and savoury notes

Commonly reported descriptor families include:

- red cherry and other red berries;
- violet and other floral aromas;
- Mediterranean herbs;
- spice;
- earthy, leathery or savoury notes, particularly in some mature wines.

These descriptions help you explore the wine, but they are not guarantees. A wine’s aromas can come from the grape itself, fermentation, maturation and bottle development. A reusable aroma-origin guide such as VIS-023 could help explain this distinction where available.

Also remember that a dry wine can still taste fruity. “Dry” mainly describes the wine’s residual sugar, not whether it smells or tastes of cherries or berries.

## Why can two Nerello Mascalese wines taste different?

The grape name gives you a useful starting point, not a complete prediction.

Vineyard site and climate affect ripeness, acidity and overall balance. Elevation and exposure can contribute to differences between vineyards, while soil composition is part of a wider combination of place and growing conditions.

Vintage matters too. A warmer or cooler growing season can change fruit ripeness and acidity. Vine age, crop levels and harvest decisions may also influence concentration and structure.

Winemaking adds another layer. Skin and seed contact, fermentation temperature, extraction, oak, vessel choice and maturation can change colour, tannin, texture and aroma. A fresh, lightly handled wine may show bright fruit and delicate structure, while a more extracted or matured example may feel firmer and more savoury.

This is why it is more accurate to say that Nerello Mascalese often has a particular shape—fresh acidity, pale colour and noticeable tannin—than to promise one universal flavour.

## Is Nerello Cappuccio the same grape?

No. Nerello Cappuccio is a separate Sicilian red grape variety.

It may be used alongside Nerello Mascalese in Etna wines. Under the Etna DOC specification, it is identified as Nerello Mantellato and may accompany Nerello Mascalese in permitted blends. The two names should not be treated as interchangeable.

## What food goes with Nerello Mascalese?

Start with foods that welcome freshness and moderate tannin. Good options include:

- tomato-based pasta;
- pasta alla Norma or other aubergine dishes;
- mushrooms;
- savoury legumes;
- grilled or roasted vegetables;
- sausages or moderately rich pork dishes;
- roast poultry;
- medium-aged cheeses.

The logic is straightforward: acidity can work well with tomato and other savoury foods, while tannin tends to feel more comfortable alongside protein and richer textures. These are starting points, not rules. A lighter wine may suit vegetables and poultry, while a more structured example can handle richer dishes.

Serve a red Nerello Mascalese cool rather than warm. A lighter, fresher bottle may benefit from being served slightly below typical room temperature; a fuller or mature example can be served a little warmer. If the wine initially seems tight or tannic, give it time in the glass.

## How might Australians encounter it?

Australian drinkers may see “Nerello Mascalese” on an imported Italian label, on a wine list or in a specialist selection of alternative grape varieties. You may instead encounter the grape indirectly through a regional name such as Etna Rosso.

Wine Australia includes Nerello Mascalese in its grape-variety reference material and has discussed it in the context of alternative varieties suited to warm-climate wine conversations. That confirms industry recognition, but the available evidence does not establish the scale of Australian plantings, production, imports or consumer demand.

So if you see the name, think of a Sicilian red grape with freshness, tannin and potentially pale colour—not a guarantee of one fixed style.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Nerello Mascalese a red wine?

Nerello Mascalese is a red grape variety. It can be used to make red, rosé and sparkling wines.

### Is Nerello Mascalese the same as Etna Rosso?

No. Nerello Mascalese is the grape; Etna Rosso is a regulated wine designation from Mount Etna that must contain at least 80 per cent Nerello Mascalese under the current specification.

### Is Nerello Mascalese always light-bodied?

No. It can look pale while still having noticeable tannin and acidity. Body varies with site, ripeness and winemaking.

### Is Nerello Mascalese always smoky or mineral?

No. Those are possible descriptions, not universal characteristics. Volcanic geography alone does not guarantee a particular flavour.

### What is the simplest way to describe it?

Think of a Sicilian red grape that can combine red-fruit and floral character with fresh acidity and firm structure—sometimes in a surprisingly pale-looking wine.
