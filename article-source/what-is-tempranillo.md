# What Is Tempranillo?

## Highlights

- Tempranillo is a **red-wine grape variety**, not one fixed style of wine.
- It is strongly associated with Spain, especially Rioja and Ribera del Duero.
- Tempranillo wines are usually dry, with red and dark fruit, fresh acidity and noticeable tannin.
- Oak and ageing can add vanilla, spice, leather or savoury flavours, but these are not guaranteed.
- Australian Tempranillo can range from fresh and elegant to dark, full and spice-driven.
- Fruit flavours such as cherry and plum do not mean the wine is sweet.

## Quick answer

Tempranillo is a red grape variety from the Iberian Peninsula, best known through Spanish wines. It commonly produces dry red wines with flavours of cherry, plum, blackberry and other red or dark fruits. Fresh acidity gives the wine lift, while tannin can add a drying or grippy sensation.

The style can change considerably. A fresher example may be medium-bodied, bright and red-fruited. A riper, more extracted or oak-matured wine may be fuller, darker, firmer and more savoury. That is why “Tempranillo” tells you the grape, but not exactly how every bottle will taste.

Tempranillo is also grown and made in Australia. Australian examples may show the same broad grape identity while reflecting their region, climate, vintage and winemaking choices.

## What does Tempranillo taste like?

A useful starting point is **cherry, plum and other red or dark fruit**, supported by acidity and tannin. Depending on the wine, you might also notice blackberry, strawberry, spice or savoury flavours.

Oak maturation can contribute flavours such as vanilla, toast, cedar or spice. With bottle age, some wines develop more earthy, leathery, tobacco-like or dried-fruit characters. These are influences of winemaking and maturation as much as expressions of the grape itself.

Tempranillo can be medium- to full-bodied. “Body” describes how weighty or full the wine feels in your mouth. A lighter-feeling Tempranillo may be fresh and energetic, while a richer example may feel broader and more powerful.

### Is Tempranillo sweet?

Most still Tempranillo is dry. Its ripe fruit flavours can make it seem rich or fruit-sweet, but fruitiness is not the same as residual-sugar sweetness.

If a wine tastes of cherry, plum or blackberry, that does not mean sugar has been added or that the wine is sweet. Dryness describes the amount of perceptible residual sugar; fruit flavour describes what your nose and palate sense.

### Is Tempranillo tannic?

Tempranillo often has noticeable tannin, although the texture varies. Tannin can make your mouth feel slightly dry, grippy or firm, particularly in a young, extracted or oak-matured red.

Ripeness, extraction, blending and bottle age all influence that sensation. Some Tempranillos feel polished and supple; others are more structured and drying.

## Tempranillo in Spain

Spain is central to Tempranillo’s identity. Rioja and Ribera del Duero are two important Spanish contexts for the variety, but Tempranillo is not the same thing as Rioja. Rioja is a wine region; Tempranillo is a grape.

You may also encounter regional names such as **Tinta del País**, **Tinto Fino** and **Tinta de Toro**. Spanish official material also records names including **Cencibel** and **Ull de Llebre**. These names are associated with Tempranillo in particular regional or technical contexts. They should not automatically be treated as completely different grapes, nor as guarantees of one particular flavour profile.

The name Tempranillo is commonly linked to the Spanish word *temprano*, meaning “early”, referring to the variety’s tendency to ripen relatively early. That does not mean every Tempranillo ripens early in every vineyard: climate, site and growing conditions still matter.

## Why Tempranillo wines vary

The grape provides a starting point, but the finished wine is shaped by several decisions and conditions:

- **Climate and site:** warmer conditions can encourage riper fruit and greater body, while cooler conditions or earlier harvesting may preserve freshness and red-fruit character.
- **Ripeness and harvest timing:** riper grapes may produce darker fruit and more weight; earlier-picked grapes may feel brighter and more energetic.
- **Extraction:** more contact with grape skins can increase colour and tannin. Gentler extraction may produce a softer, lighter-feeling wine.
- **Oak:** oak can add vanilla, toast, spice or cedar, as well as influence texture. Oak is a winemaking choice, not an unavoidable flavour of Tempranillo.
- **Blending:** Tempranillo may be bottled as a varietal wine or blended with other grapes. Blending can alter the wine’s colour, body, freshness, tannin and spice.
- **Ageing:** time can soften tannin and allow savoury, earthy, leather-like or dried-fruit notes to emerge. Not every Tempranillo is intended for long ageing.

This is why two wines made from the same grape can feel quite different in the glass.

## Tempranillo in Australia

Tempranillo is grown and made in Australia, although it remains a relatively small variety in the national wine sector.

Wine Australia’s 2025 variety snapshot records **5,263 tonnes crushed in 2025**. It also records **7 hectares planted**, but that planting figure is based on **2015 ABS data**, so it should not be read as a 2025 or 2026 planting total.

The snapshot lists the Riverland, Riverina, Murray–Darling–Swan Hill Victoria, Barossa Valley and McLaren Vale among the leading production contexts for Tempranillo. These figures describe production, not quality rankings.

Australian Tempranillo is not one fixed national style. Wine Australia describes warm-region examples as potentially powerful, dark-fruited and spice-driven while retaining balancing acidity. It also identifies cooler Adelaide Hills examples as potentially fresher and more elegant. In practical terms, an Australian bottle may be bright and medium-bodied, or dark, full and structured, depending on its site and how it was made.

## What should you eat with Tempranillo?

Tempranillo’s acidity, tannin and savoury possibilities can work well with substantial, savoury food. Try it with grilled or roasted meats, roast vegetables, tomato-based pasta, mushrooms, savoury rice or bean dishes, and firm or mature cheeses.

These are starting points rather than rules. A lighter, fresher Tempranillo may suit different food from a fuller, oak-matured example.

For serving, try Tempranillo slightly cool or at a cool room temperature. The best approach depends on the wine’s body and structure. You do not need to know its region or ageing history before enjoying it: taste the wine, notice whether it feels fresh, firm, soft or rich, and serve it in a way that makes those qualities enjoyable.

## Is Tempranillo worth trying?

If you enjoy dry red wine with fruit, freshness and some structure, Tempranillo is a useful variety to explore. Look beyond the grape name and consider the style suggested by the label or wine description: fresh and elegant, or fuller and oak-influenced.

The most important thing to remember is that Tempranillo is a grape, not a flavour checklist. Spain provides its best-known identity, while Australian producers interpret it through a different range of regions and conditions.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Tempranillo a red or white wine?

Tempranillo is a red-wine grape variety and is most commonly used to make dry red wine.

### Is Tempranillo similar to Shiraz?

Both can make full-flavoured red wines, but they are different grape varieties. Tempranillo’s acidity, tannin, fruit profile and oak treatment vary from wine to wine, so a direct comparison should not be assumed.

### Does Tempranillo need to be aged?

No. Some structured wines may develop complexity with age, but many Tempranillos are made to be enjoyed young. Ageing potential depends on the individual wine.

### Can Tempranillo be blended?

Yes. It can be made as a varietal wine or used in a blend. Blending may change the wine’s body, colour, acidity, tannin and flavour.

### Where does Tempranillo grow in Australia?

Wine Australia’s 2025 snapshot identifies production contexts including the Riverland, Riverina, Murray–Darling–Swan Hill Victoria, Barossa Valley and McLaren Vale. Other Australian regions are also exploring the variety.
