# Burgundy village wine labels: how to read them

A Burgundy village wine label usually names a regulated **Village appellation**: a defined place within Burgundy whose wines must follow specific production rules. It tells you more about the wine’s geographic origin than its grape variety. In the practical Burgundy hierarchy, Village wines sit above broad regional appellations and below Premier Cru and Grand Cru designations.

The key idea is simple: a village name tells you where the wine belongs in Burgundy’s appellation system. It does not, by itself, guarantee a particular flavour, producer quality or price.

## Highlights

- “Village” is a formal appellation level, not a synonym for cheap, rustic or simple wine.
- A Village appellation is broader than one vineyard and may include many sites.
- Premier Cru and Grand Cru are not interchangeable with Village.
- Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are central to Burgundy, but the grape may not be prominent on the label.
- A named *Climat* identifies a more specific, historically recognised vineyard site.
- The label establishes regulated origin and appellation context—not a guaranteed taste or quality level.
- For an Australian buyer, the front label and the importer or supplier information on the back serve different purposes.

## Quick answer: what does “village” mean in Burgundy?

In Burgundy, a village wine normally carries the name of a recognised Village appellation, such as Savigny-lès-Beaune, Chassagne-Montrachet or Meursault. The appellation is associated with a legally defined geographic area and production specification.

That makes the village name meaningful: it is not merely saying that the wine was made near a town. It places the wine within a protected French wine-origin system. The rules cover matters such as the appellation name, production area, permitted varieties and other conditions.

However, the name does not tell you everything. Two wines carrying the same Village appellation can differ because of their producer, vineyard site, farming, winemaking, vintage and bottle age. The appellation gives you a framework for understanding the wine, not a complete tasting note.

## The simple Burgundy hierarchy

Burgundy’s classification is detailed, but this four-level version is useful when reading a label:

1. **Regional appellations** — broad names such as Bourgogne.
2. **Village appellations** — names such as Savigny-lès-Beaune or Chassagne-Montrachet.
3. **Premier Cru** — a more specific classified site within a village appellation.
4. **Grand Cru** — a highly specific classified site with its own appellation identity.

This is a reader-friendly model rather than a complete legal directory. The categories describe geographic and appellation status; they should not be treated as a guaranteed ranking of enjoyment. A Grand Cru label will not necessarily be the best personal match for every drinker, and a Village wine is not automatically inferior.

The terms **AOC** and **AOP** are French and European protected-origin designations. You can read more about their broader meaning in [French AOC and AOP](/french-aoc-and-aop/). Burgundy’s system is different from Australia’s Geographic Indications, even though both use geographic names to communicate origin.

## Village name, vineyard and *Climat*: what is the difference?

A village appellation may cover numerous vineyards and parcels. It is therefore not the same as a single vineyard.

Burgundy uses the word ***Climat*** for a precisely named and delimited vineyard site with its own historical and geographic identity. A *Climat* can appear on a label as a more specific indication within a broader appellation. Not every *Climat* is automatically Premier Cru.

A Premier Cru label commonly combines the village appellation with the name of the Premier Cru *Climat*. This is more specific than a Village label alone. Grand Cru wines may foreground the individual Grand Cru site name rather than the village.

One useful way to think about it is:

- **Village appellation:** the wider recognised wine area.
- **Premier Cru *Climat*:** a more specific classified site within that village context.
- **Grand Cru site:** a specific top-level appellation and vineyard identity.

The place names can look similar on a bottle, so take a moment to identify whether you are reading a village name, a *Climat* or a classification term.

## Why is the grape variety sometimes missing?

Burgundy labels are strongly place-led. The appellation name is often the most prominent identifying information, while the grape variety may be absent from the front label or printed less prominently elsewhere.

Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are Burgundy’s two central grape varieties. As a broad guide:

- white Burgundy is commonly associated with Chardonnay;
- red Burgundy is commonly associated with Pinot Noir.

But these are not safe universal rules for every Village appellation. Bouzeron, for example, is a Village appellation associated with Aligoté. That is why the appellation name should be treated as the starting point, rather than assuming the grape from the word “Burgundy” alone.

For more background, see [What is Chardonnay?](/what-is-chardonnay/) and [What is Pinot Noir?](/what-is-pinot-noir/).

## What does the label tell you—and what does it not?

### It can tell you

A qualifying Village appellation can communicate:

- a regulated geographic origin;
- the wine’s place within Burgundy’s appellation hierarchy;
- relevant production rules;
- whether a more specific term such as Premier Cru or Grand Cru is present;
- whether a named *Climat* appears.

### It cannot tell you by itself

The village name does not guarantee:

- producer quality or reputation;
- organic or biodynamic farming;
- particular winemaking choices;
- an exact flavour profile;
- ageworthiness;
- a certain level of price or value;
- that you will personally enjoy the wine.

This distinction is important. A protected place name is evidence about origin and appellation rules—not a promise that every bottle will taste the same.

## How to read a Burgundy bottle in Australia

In an Australian bottle shop, restaurant or wine list, the French appellation may be the most noticeable wording on the front. Start by looking for:

- the Village appellation name;
- “Premier Cru” or “Grand Cru”, if present;
- a named *Climat*;
- the producer or bottler;
- the vintage;
- alcohol percentage and bottle volume;
- importer or supplier information on the back label.

The French appellation and Australian market information have different jobs. “Savigny-lès-Beaune” communicates the wine’s French appellation and geographic identity. An importer or supplier statement identifies the business associated with the product in the Australian market. It does not mean that the importer made the wine.

The existing [How to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/) guide covers general label fields. This page adds the Burgundy-specific question: which place name is doing the explanatory work?

When using an annotated label visual, look for the appellation name, classification wording, *Climat*, producer or bottler, vintage and importer or supplier information. Real labels do not all use the same layout.

## A few useful examples

**Savigny-lès-Beaune** is a verified example of a Côte de Beaune Village appellation. Its wines can include red and white expressions, and the appellation may be followed by the name of a *Climat*.

**Chassagne-Montrachet** shows how different levels can relate to the same commune. The area has a Village appellation, named Premier Cru *Climats* and separate Grand Cru appellations.

**Meursault** is another familiar Burgundy name used in the Village and Premier Cru context. If “Premier Cru” and a specific *Climat* appear, the label is communicating a more specific designation than Meursault Village alone.

These examples are not a complete list. Their purpose is to show how a recognisable place name can sit within a larger hierarchy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does “village wine” mean inexpensive wine?

No. In Burgundy, “Village” describes an appellation level. It is not a synonym for inexpensive, rustic or low-quality wine.

### Is a Burgundy Village wine always Pinot Noir or Chardonnay?

Not always. Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are central to Burgundy, but permitted varieties differ by appellation. Bouzeron is a useful reminder that Aligoté is also relevant in a Village appellation.

### Is a Village wine from one vineyard?

Usually not. A Village appellation can cover multiple sites. A named *Climat* gives more specific site information, but it should not automatically be equated with Premier Cru.

### Does Premier Cru or Grand Cru always taste better?

Those terms communicate a more specific classification and appellation status. They do not guarantee a better personal match, exact flavour or producer quality.
