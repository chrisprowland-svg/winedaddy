# What is Rotgipfler?

## Highlights

- Rotgipfler is a white wine grape variety, not a region or wine style.
- It comes from Austria and is strongly associated with the Thermenregion in Lower Austria.
- It usually makes dry, flavourful white wines with fresh acidity and noticeable texture.
- Typical examples may show pear, melon, mango, spice, baked apple or toast.
- Rotgipfler is different from Zierfandler, although the two grapes are sometimes blended.
- Australian examples are not something to assume you will find widely; imported bottles may be the most likely encounter.

## Quick answer

Rotgipfler is an Austrian white grape variety, grown principally in the Thermenregion south of Vienna. It generally produces dry white wines with good extract, fine or pleasant acidity and a spicy, fruit-led character.

In the glass, Rotgipfler can be more substantial than a very light white wine. Younger examples may suggest melon, mango or juicy pear, while mature bottles can develop baked-apple and toasted notes. These are typical possibilities rather than a fixed tasting formula: site, vintage, ripeness, winemaking and age all make a difference.

The grape is often bottled on its own, but it is also associated with blends with Zierfandler, another Austrian white grape.

## Where does Rotgipfler come from?

Rotgipfler is Austrian and is particularly associated with the Thermenregion in Lower Austria. The variety probably originated in or near this region and is thought to have developed from a natural crossing of Traminer and Roter Veltliner.

That parentage does not make Rotgipfler the same grape as either of them. Nor is it the same as Zierfandler. Gumpoldskirchen is a locality associated with wines from the Thermenregion, not another name for Rotgipfler.

On a bottle, **Rotgipfler** identifies the grape, while **Thermenregion** identifies the relevant wine region. The exact combination of grape and regional wording depends on the wine’s category and labelling.

## What does Rotgipfler wine taste like?

Rotgipfler is commonly made as a dry white wine with:

- fresh, fine or pleasant acidity;
- a fruit-led, spicy bouquet;
- a medium to full sense of substance;
- a textured or extract-rich palate.

The grape’s regional descriptions include melon, mango and pear in youthful wines. With maturity, baked-apple and toasted flavours may appear. Toasted notes do not necessarily mean the wine was heavily oaked; age and development can also change the flavour.

There is no single Rotgipfler template. A warm site, a ripe vintage, oak maturation, residual sugar or bottle age can all alter the result. Reading the label and considering the producer’s approach remains important.

## What other names does Rotgipfler have?

Official Austrian sources record **Reifler, Rotreifler, Rotstreifler** and **Slatzki Zelenac** as names associated with Rotgipfler. You may also encounter historical or regional spelling variations, but Rotgipfler is the clearest name to look for today.

The name is linked to the vine’s reddish or bronze-coloured shoot tips. There is no single authoritative pronunciation guide to rely on, so it is perfectly reasonable to ask for help when ordering it.

## How should you serve it?

A useful starting point is to serve Rotgipfler at about **8–12°C** in a standard white-wine glass. Serve lighter, younger examples towards the cooler end and richer or older wines slightly warmer so their aromas can open.

Its acidity, fruit character and substance make it a flexible partner for poultry, pork, fish, shellfish, creamy vegetables and moderately rich vegetarian dishes. These are starting points, not strict rules. Match the dish to the particular bottle, especially if it is noticeably sweet, rich or oak-influenced.

For Australian drinkers, Rotgipfler is best understood as a specialised Austrian variety. Authoritative evidence supplied for this guide does not establish significant Australian plantings, domestic production or broad local availability, so Australian production should not be assumed.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Rotgipfler a sweet wine?

Not inherently. It is commonly encountered as a dry white wine, but sweetness depends on the wine’s category and how it was made.

### Is Rotgipfler the same as Zierfandler?

No. They are separate grape varieties, although they may be blended together.

### Is Rotgipfler a region?

No. Rotgipfler is the grape. Thermenregion is the Austrian wine region most closely associated with it.
