# Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo: A Beginner’s Guide to This Italian Rosé-Style Wine

Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo is a protected rosé-style wine designation from Abruzzo, in central-eastern Italy. It is made primarily from the Montepulciano grape and is known for its cherry-pink colour, dry palate, freshness and more substantial structure than many pale rosés.

The important point is that Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo is not simply an informal name for a darker rosé. **Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo DOC** is a regulated Italian designation, also recognised by the European Union as a Protected Designation of Origin (PDO).

## Highlights

- It comes from defined municipalities across Abruzzo’s four provinces: Chieti, L’Aquila, Pescara and Teramo.
- The ordinary designation requires at least 85% Montepulciano.
- It is generally dry, although its ripe red-fruit flavours can make it seem generous and juicy.
- Its colour can be deeper than the pale pink associated with many modern rosés.
- Shorter contact with grape skins helps create its colour and moderate structure.
- It is a separate designation from Montepulciano d’Abruzzo, even though both are based principally on Montepulciano.
- It is usually a versatile food wine, particularly with seafood, antipasti, pizza and poultry.

## Where does Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo come from?

Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo follows this geographic hierarchy:

**Italy → Abruzzo → Chieti, L’Aquila, Pescara or Teramo Province → named municipalities → Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo DOC**

Abruzzo is a region on Italy’s Adriatic side, broadly east of Rome. Pescara is a useful major-city reference point for understanding the area, while Chieti, Teramo and L’Aquila help orient the northern, hill-country and inland parts of the region. Localities such as Vasto, Ortona and Loreto Aprutino are useful points of reference in the wider Abruzzo wine landscape, but none of these towns defines the whole designation.

The legal production area covers named municipalities across all four Abruzzo provinces. That makes Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo an Abruzzo-wide designation rather than a wine centred on one single town or valley.

For an Australian traveller, Rome is a practical starting reference: the drive to Pescara is broadly east-north-east and approximately two to two-and-a-half hours, depending on the route, traffic and starting point. Treat that only as orientation. Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo is spread across many municipalities, so it is not a single destination reached by driving to one appellation centre.

## What do DOC and PDO mean?

**DOC**, or *Denominazione di Origine Controllata*, is the Italian term for a protected designation with rules governing origin and production.

**PDO**, or Protected Designation of Origin, is the European Union terminology for a protected place-based food or wine name. You may also see the Italian abbreviation **DOP**.

These systems are comparable in their focus on geographic identity, but they are not legally interchangeable with Australia’s Geographical Indications system. On an imported bottle, the Italian designation tells you that the wine belongs to a protected origin category under Italian and EU rules. Other information on the label may relate separately to Australian import and alcohol requirements.

## Which grape is used?

Montepulciano is the principal grape in Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo. The ordinary designation requires at least **85% Montepulciano**, with up to 15% other suitable non-aromatic red grape varieties grown in Abruzzo.

That means it is not accurate to say that every bottle is 100% Montepulciano. Some individual wines may use only Montepulciano, but that is a producer choice rather than the minimum rule for the ordinary designation.

The grape relationship is one reason Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo can be confused with [Montepulciano d’Abruzzo](/montepulciano-dabruzzo/). They are related, but they are not the same wine or the same designation.

## How is Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo made?

The main production difference from a red wine is the amount of time the grape juice spends with the skins.

Red grape skins contain colour compounds and tannins. Leaving juice with the skins for a shorter period extracts some colour and structure without producing the deep colour and fuller tannin of a typical red wine. The Consorzio Vini d’Abruzzo also describes a white-wine-style approach, known in Italian as *vinificazione in bianco*, where the wine is fermented without extended skin contact.

There is no single universal maceration time for every bottle. Short skin contact is the useful general explanation, but the exact duration and winemaking choices can vary.

## What does it taste like?

The official profile describes Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo as cherry-pink, fruity, dry, soft and harmonious, with a possible almond-like aftertaste.

In the glass, you might find impressions of:

- cherry or sour cherry;
- raspberry and strawberry;
- freshness and lively red-fruit character;
- a moderately substantial texture;
- a little tannic grip in more structured examples.

It is best thought of as a **deeply coloured, dry rosé-style wine**. Some examples are bright and easy-drinking, while others can be more concentrated and textural. Vineyard, producer, vintage and maturation choices all influence the final wine.

The designation gives ordinary Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo a minimum total alcohol of 12% by volume, although individual wines may be higher. Acidity and tannin are not identical in every bottle. Shorter skin contact generally limits tannin compared with red-wine production, but a fuller Cerasuolo can still feel gently grippy.

The wine is officially dry. That does not mean it must taste austere or lack fruit. A wine can smell and taste of cherries or strawberries while still having little residual sugar. Our guides to [dry rosé](/dry-rose/) and [what rosé means](/what-is-rose/) explain this distinction in more detail.

## Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo versus Montepulciano d’Abruzzo

The names are similar because the wines share a principal grape relationship, but the categories are separate.

**Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo** is the cherry-pink, rosé-style designation. It is made with shorter skin contact or a white-wine-style fermentation, producing a wine with freshness, red-fruit character and moderate structure.

**Montepulciano d’Abruzzo** is a separate Abruzzo designation, principally encountered as a red-wine category. Its red versions generally have darker colour and more pronounced tannin and structure.

So the difference is not simply “the same wine in a different colour”. The designations have their own rules, styles and label identities.

## What should you look for on the label?

Look for the complete wording:

- **Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo**
- **Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo DOC**
- **Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo PDO** or **DOP**

The full name matters. “Cerasuolo” on its own can be ambiguous because similar terms may be used elsewhere in Italy.

A bottle may also show a more specific sub-area, such as Terre di Chieti, Terre Aquilane or Terre de L’Aquila, Colline Pescaresi or Colline Teramane. These names have additional regulatory conditions, so they should not be treated as interchangeable with the broad designation.

## How should you serve it?

A practical serving range is **8–12°C**. Serve lighter, brighter examples towards the cooler end and fuller, more structured examples slightly warmer.

Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo’s combination of dryness, freshness, red fruit and moderate structure makes it useful at the table. Try it with:

- grilled seafood or fish soup;
- antipasti and delicate cured meats;
- pizza;
- tomato-based pasta;
- grilled vegetables;
- roast or barbecued poultry;
- fresh or moderately mature cheese.

These are starting points rather than guarantees. A lighter bottle may suit seafood and antipasti particularly well, while a fuller example can stand up to richer poultry, savoury cured meat or strongly flavoured pizza. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo a rosé?

Yes. It is a protected rosé-style wine designation from Abruzzo, although it can be more deeply coloured and structured than very pale rosé styles.

### Is Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo sweet?

The official style is dry. Its red-fruit flavours may taste juicy or generous, but fruitiness is not the same as sweetness.

### Is it made from Montepulciano?

Yes, primarily. The ordinary designation requires at least 85% Montepulciano, but not necessarily 100%.

### Is it the same as Montepulciano d’Abruzzo?

No. They are separate protected designations that share Montepulciano as their principal grape relationship.

### Is Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo a light red wine?

Some examples may feel closer to a light red in colour or structure, but its official category is rosé-style. “Deeply coloured, dry rosé-style wine” is the clearest description.
