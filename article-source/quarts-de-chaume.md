# Quarts de Chaume: A Beginner’s Guide to This Loire Sweet Wine

Quarts de Chaume is a small, protected French appellation for sweet, still white wine made exclusively from Chenin Blanc. It comes from a tightly defined area in Anjou, in the Loire Valley, rather than from the whole surrounding region.

The name can look mysterious on a wine list or imported bottle. The useful starting point is this: **Quarts de Chaume identifies a protected place and production system, while Chenin Blanc identifies the grape.**

## Highlights

- It is a French appellation in the Loire Valley.
- More specifically, it is in Anjou, above the Layon River.
- Its legal geographic area is within Rochefort-sur-Loire, in Maine-et-Loire.
- The wine is always a still white wine under the appellation rules.
- Chenin Blanc is the only permitted grape variety.
- The grapes are naturally overripe and hand-picked in successive selective passes.
- Botrytis, or noble rot, may contribute to concentration, but it is not required in every vintage.
- The finished wine is genuinely sweet, with acidity helping to balance its richness.

## Where is Quarts de Chaume?

The simplest hierarchy is:

**France → Loire Valley or Val de Loire → Anjou → Layon valley → Rochefort-sur-Loire → Quarts de Chaume**

Rochefort-sur-Loire is the legally relevant commune. The actual grape-production area is smaller still: grapes must come from a defined parcel zone known as the *tènement de Chaume*, including local sites called Les Quarts, Les Rouères and Le Veau.

For orientation, Quarts de Chaume is about 20 kilometres south of Angers, the nearest useful regional centre. From Angers, think of it as approximately a 25–35-minute drive south-west, depending on the route and starting point. That is a practical orientation, not a fixed travel promise.

“Loire Valley”, “Anjou” and “Layon valley” help explain where the appellation sits. They are not interchangeable names for Quarts de Chaume itself. This is similar to the way a broad Australian wine region can contain smaller, more specific place names.

## What kind of wine is it?

Quarts de Chaume produces sweet white wine with richness, concentration and freshness. Typical descriptions include floral, citrus, exotic-fruit, dried-fruit, candied, honeyed and spicy notes, although no individual bottle is guaranteed to show every one of them.

The wine’s sweetness is not meant to stand alone. Acidity provides lift and helps stop the palate feeling heavy or flat. A slight bitterness may also add shape and balance. That combination—sweetness, concentration and freshness—is central to understanding the style.

Older bottles may develop more dried-fruit, candied, nutty or spicy characters. Quarts de Chaume has strong ageing potential because sweetness, acidity and concentration can help a wine develop over time. However, the life of any particular bottle depends on its vintage, producer, closure and storage, so there is no single guaranteed ageing timeline.

## Why are the grapes so sweet?

The appellation rules require naturally overripe grapes. They also require hand harvesting by *tries successives*, meaning successive selective passes through the vineyard.

Instead of picking everything at once, pickers return through the vines and select grapes that have reached the required level of concentration. The rules require a minimum grape-must sugar level of 298 grams per litre and a minimum natural alcohol potential of 18 per cent.

After fermentation, the wine must retain more than 85 grams per litre of fermentable sugars. In plain language, this is genuinely sweet wine—not simply a dry wine that happens to smell or taste fruity.

These figures are production requirements, not a prediction that every bottle will have the same alcohol level or taste equally sweet. Acidity, alcohol, temperature and serving context all influence how sweetness feels.

## Is Quarts de Chaume always made with noble rot?

No. Quarts de Chaume is often associated with *botrytis cinerea*, commonly called noble rot. In suitable conditions, this fungus can dehydrate grapes, concentrating their sugars and contributing additional complexity.

But the appellation specification allows natural over-ripening with or without noble rot. Concentration can also occur through *passerillage*, the natural drying or dehydration of grapes on the vine.

That distinction matters. It is accurate to say that Quarts de Chaume may include botrytised grapes or is often associated with noble rot. It is not accurate to say that every vintage or every bottle is made from noble-rot grapes.

You can learn more about the broader ideas behind this in [botrytised wine](/botrytised-wine/) and [botrytis and noble rot](/botrytis-and-noble-rot/).

## How should you read the name on a label?

If you see **Quarts de Chaume**, read it first as the name of a protected French appellation. It is not the name of a grape and it is not a synonym for all dessert wine.

The label may also include permitted complementary wording such as **Val de Loire** or **grand cru**. In this specific appellation, “grand cru” is an authorised mention attached to the name. It is not a grape variety, and it should not be assumed to have exactly the same legal meaning across every French wine region.

A bottle may also mention Chenin Blanc in an importer’s or retailer’s description. That explains the grape, while Quarts de Chaume explains the protected place and rules.

For more on protected place names, see [French AOC and AOP](/french-aoc-and-aop/) and [protected wine names](/protected-wine-names/).

## Quarts de Chaume versus dessert wine

“Dessert wine” is a broad English-language description. It can include sweet wines made in many countries, from different grapes and by different production methods.

Quarts de Chaume is much more specific. It is a defined French appellation with:

- one permitted grape variety, Chenin Blanc;
- a delimited production area;
- naturally overripe grapes;
- successive selective hand harvesting; and
- minimum maturity and finished-sugar requirements.

So the most useful translation for an Australian learner is:

> Dessert wine describes a broad style. Quarts de Chaume identifies a particular French appellation that produces sweet Chenin Blanc.

“Late harvest” or “late-picked” is also a broad production description. It may help explain the wine, but it is not a replacement for the appellation name.

## Is it like Sauternes?

Quarts de Chaume and Sauternes are both French appellations associated with sweet still white wine, but they are separate places with different rules.

Quarts de Chaume is in Anjou in the Loire Valley and uses Chenin Blanc exclusively. Sauternes is in Bordeaux and is generally associated with Sémillon-led blends, often including Sauvignon Blanc and Muscadelle.

Both may be discussed alongside noble rot, but neither name is a synonym for the other. If you see Sauternes, you are looking at a separate Bordeaux appellation—not another level of Quarts de Chaume.

## How to serve it

Serve Quarts de Chaume chilled, but not so cold that its aromas and texture disappear. Its sweetness makes it suitable for a dessert-wine serve, and it can also work with rich, salty or gently sweet foods.

The important thing is to notice the balance: sweetness should be accompanied by freshness rather than feeling merely sugary. If you are tasting it at home, allow the wine a little time in the glass. The aromas and texture may become easier to recognise as it warms slightly.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Quarts de Chaume a grape variety?

No. It is a protected French appellation. The sole permitted grape is Chenin Blanc.

### Is Quarts de Chaume a sweet wine?

Yes. It is a sweet, still white wine, with finished fermentable sugars above 85 grams per litre under the current specification.

### Is every Quarts de Chaume wine botrytised?

No. Noble rot may contribute to concentration, but the rules allow grapes to be naturally overripe with or without botrytis.

### Where does Quarts de Chaume come from?

It comes from a tightly defined parcel area within Rochefort-sur-Loire, in Anjou’s Layon valley in the Loire Valley of France.

### What does “grand cru” mean on the label?

For Quarts de Chaume, “grand cru” is an authorised complementary mention attached to the appellation name. Its use should not be generalised to mean exactly the same thing in every French region.
