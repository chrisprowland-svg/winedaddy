# Concentration in wine explained

## Highlights

- **Concentration** describes how deep, dense and persistent a wine’s aromas and flavours seem.
- It is a **sensory impression**, not one universally accepted chemical measurement.
- A concentrated wine is not automatically full-bodied, high in alcohol, sweet, ripe, heavily extracted or better.
- Concentration can appear in dry red, white, rosé, sparkling and fortified wines.
- Body describes weight in the mouth; concentration describes flavour presence. They can overlap, but they are not the same.
- Australian wines can show many forms of concentration, depending on variety, site, climate, vintage and winemaking.

## Quick answer: what does concentration mean in wine?

When a wine is described as **concentrated**, the taster usually means that its aromas and flavours feel deep, dense and substantial rather than faint or quickly disappearing.

You might notice concentrated fruit, spice, savoury, floral or mineral-associated flavours. The wine may continue to feel flavourful through the middle of the palate—the **mid-palate**—and into the finish.

Concentration is not the same as simply making a strong first impression. A wine can have an intense aroma but lose its flavour quickly. Another wine may be less immediately powerful but have a deep, persistent presence across the palate.

Most importantly, concentration is not a score for quality. A concentrated wine can be balanced and compelling, but it can also feel heavy, hot, overly extracted or out of proportion if its acidity, tannin, alcohol and texture do not work together.

## How do you recognise concentration?

Concentration is easiest to understand by tasting in stages.

### Aroma

A concentrated wine may have aromas that seem clear, deep or packed with detail. These could be dark fruit and spice, citrus and stone fruit, herbs, flowers, savoury notes or oak-related aromas.

A powerful nose alone does not prove that the whole wine is concentrated. Aroma intensity and palate concentration are related, but they are separate observations. For more on this, see [what is wine aroma?](/what-is-wine-aroma/).

### Flavour and the mid-palate

After tasting, ask whether the wine continues to show flavour after its first impression. Does it feel present across the centre of your mouth, or does it become thin and fade quickly?

Concentration can feel like flavour density: not necessarily heaviness, but a sense that there is plenty of flavour packed into each sip. A dry wine can be concentrated in fruit, spice, herbs or savoury character without tasting sweet.

### Finish

A concentrated wine may have a persistent finish, but **persistence** and concentration are not identical. Persistence is about how long aroma or flavour remains. A wine can be concentrated and short, or relatively subtle yet long.

This is why it helps to assess flavour density and length separately when tasting. [How to taste wine](/how-to-taste-wine/) offers a broader tasting sequence.

## Concentration is not the same as these wine terms

### Concentration and intensity

**Intensity** usually means how strongly a particular aroma, flavour or sensation is perceived. Concentration suggests a broader impression of depth or density.

The terms overlap, and wine writers do not always use them consistently. For everyday tasting, ask two questions: “How forceful is this flavour?” and “How much flavour presence does the wine sustain?”

### Concentration and body

**Body** is the wine’s perceived weight, volume or fullness in the mouth. A full-bodied wine may feel broad or substantial, but that does not guarantee concentrated flavour.

Conversely, a light- or medium-bodied wine can have concentrated flavours. A bright, cool-climate wine, for example, may be relatively light on its feet while showing vivid, persistent citrus, red-fruit, herbal or savoury character.

Read more in [what is wine body?](/what-is-wine-body/).

### Concentration and alcohol

Alcohol can contribute warmth, roundness or a sense of weight. It does not prove that a wine has concentrated flavour.

A high-alcohol wine may taste hot or broad but lack depth. A moderate-alcohol wine may be flavourful and persistent without feeling heavy.

### Concentration and sweetness

Sweetness is a taste sensation, while concentration is about perceived flavour depth and presence. A dry wine can still be concentrated. This is the useful distinction behind the idea that **dry does not mean flavourless**.

For the difference between these ideas, see [what is sweetness in wine?](/what-is-sweetness-in-wine/) and [fruitiness vs sweetness](/fruitiness-vs-sweetness/).

### Concentration and ripeness

Ripeness can influence a wine’s fruit profile, alcohol, freshness and texture. In Australian Cabernet Sauvignon research, riper-grape wines were associated with more dark-fruit, hotness and viscosity descriptions, alongside fewer red-fruit and fresh-green descriptions.

That does not mean late-picked grapes always create concentration. Ripeness is one influence among many, and it remains distinct from concentration. See [ripeness in wine](/ripeness-in-wine/) when that related page is available.

### Concentration, tannin and extraction

Tannin can give a wine grip and structure. It may make the wine feel substantial, but tannin is not flavour concentration.

**Extraction** is the transfer of compounds from grape solids into wine during processing. In red winemaking, it can influence colour, phenolics, flavour and texture. More extraction is not automatically more concentration: excessive extraction may bring harsh tannin or bitterness and make the wine feel less balanced.

### Concentration and complexity

**Complexity** is about the range, layering and development of aromas and flavours. Concentration is about the depth or density of their presence.

A wine can be concentrated but simple, with one dominant flavour profile. Another can be complex but subtle, revealing several quieter aromas over time. [What is complexity in wine?](/what-is-complexity-in-wine/) explores that distinction.

## What creates concentration in wine?

No single vineyard or winemaking decision guarantees concentration. Perceived concentration can be influenced by:

- grape variety and berry composition;
- crop load and vine balance;
- ripeness and harvest timing;
- climate, site and the growing season;
- skin contact and extraction;
- fermentation, oak, lees contact and blending.

These factors interact. Crop-load research shows that effects can vary with climate, season and vineyard conditions. Likewise, ripeness may change fruit character and mouthfeel without automatically producing a more concentrated wine.

Balance matters too. A wine’s acidity, tannin, alcohol, sweetness, texture and finish shape how its concentration is experienced. A concentrated wine with lively acidity and supportive tannin may feel energetic and complete. The same level of flavour density may feel tiring if the other elements do not support it. See [what is balance in wine?](/what-is-balance-in-wine/).

## Concentration in Australian wine

Australian wine does not have one national style of concentration. Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay can all show concentration, but the form it takes varies by variety, site, climate, vintage and winemaking.

A warmer-season Shiraz may show dense dark-fruit or spice flavours, while another may be more savoury or lifted. A cool-climate Shiraz can be medium-bodied yet strongly flavoured, with pepper, red fruit or herbal detail.

Cabernet Sauvignon may show concentrated cassis, herbs, cedar or tannin. Chardonnay may be concentrated through citrus, stone fruit, lees, oak or mineral-associated impressions without being sweet or high in alcohol.

These are possibilities, not regional guarantees. Warm climates do not automatically produce concentrated wines, and cool climates do not automatically produce delicate or dilute wines.

## A simple concentration tasting exercise

Choose two wines in contrasting styles, without worrying about particular brands. If possible, use the same or a similar variety.

1. Serve them in similar glasses and at suitable temperatures.
2. Taste each wine separately.
3. Record aroma strength, flavour density and mid-palate presence.
4. Then assess body, texture, acidity, tannin, alcohol warmth, sweetness and finish separately.
5. Ask: **Which wine has more flavour presence?** Do not ask only which wine feels heavier.
6. Taste again after the wines have warmed slightly, or with a neutral food.

Serving temperature, glass shape, palate fatigue, food and expectations can affect perception. The exercise is not about finding the “best” wine. It is about learning to separate concentration from weight, sweetness, alcohol and colour.

## FAQs

### Is concentrated wine always better?

No. Concentration can be attractive, but quality depends on balance, harmony, detail and style. More flavour is not automatically more enjoyable.

### Can white wine be concentrated?

Yes. Concentration is not limited to dark-coloured red wine. A white wine may show deep, persistent citrus, stone fruit, floral, savoury or oak-related flavours.

### Does high alcohol mean high concentration?

No. Alcohol may add warmth or weight, but a wine can be high in alcohol without having dense flavour. The reverse is also possible.

### Is concentration the same as body?

No. Body is perceived weight or volume in the mouth. Concentration is perceived depth and density of aroma and flavour. They often overlap, but should be assessed separately.

### How should I use “concentration” in a wine review?

Describe what you actually notice: flavour density, mid-palate presence, depth and persistence. Then explain whether the wine remains balanced. This is more useful than using “concentrated” as a synonym for powerful or high-quality.
