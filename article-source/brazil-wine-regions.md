# Brazil wine regions

## Highlights

- Brazil’s established wine industry is concentrated in the south of the country.
- Serra Gaúcha in Rio Grande do Sul is its best-known wine region.
- Humidity and rainfall make vineyard management an important part of the regional story.
- Brazil produces sparkling, still and sweet wines from both international and hybrid grape varieties.

## Where Brazilian wine is grown

Brazil spans an enormous range of climates, but most established wine production is in the cooler south. Rio Grande do Sul is the principal state for viticulture, with smaller and emerging areas in other parts of the country.

Unlike many famous dry-climate wine regions, southern Brazil receives substantial rainfall. Producers manage humidity, disease pressure and vintage variation through site selection, canopy work and careful harvest decisions.

## Serra Gaúcha

Serra Gaúcha is a hilly region in Rio Grande do Sul and the historic centre of Brazilian wine. Its elevation provides some temperature moderation, while its humid conditions distinguish it from many other New World regions.

The region makes a wide range of wines. Sparkling wine is an important reference style, alongside still wines from grapes such as Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, Merlot and Moscato. The region also has a long history of table-grape and hybrid-variety production.

## Other Brazilian regions

Wine production also occurs beyond Serra Gaúcha, including higher or drier sites elsewhere in southern Brazil and experimental tropical viticulture farther north. These areas do not all share the same growing cycle or wine style, so “Brazilian wine” should not be treated as a single climatic category.

## Reading a Brazilian wine label

Start with the producer, origin and grape variety. A regional or protected geographic term gives more useful information than the country name alone. Vintage can matter because seasonal rainfall and harvest conditions vary.
