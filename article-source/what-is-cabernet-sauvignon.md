## Highlights
- Cabernet Sauvignon is a red wine grape variety. The name is also used for wines made mainly from that grape.
- It commonly produces deeply coloured, medium- to full-bodied wines.
- Typical Cabernet Sauvignon has noticeable acidity, firm tannin and dark-fruit flavours.
- Blackcurrant, blackberry, mulberry and plum are common descriptors, but every bottle is different.
- Climate, ripeness, oak, blending and bottle age can change the wine considerably.
- Most still Cabernet Sauvignon is dry, even when it tastes richly fruity.
- Australian examples range from elegant, cool-climate wines to fuller, more structured styles.
- Some Cabernet Sauvignon can age for many years, but plenty is made to enjoy now.

## Quick answer: what is Cabernet Sauvignon?

Cabernet Sauvignon is a **red wine grape variety**. It is one of the world’s best-known grapes and is used to make both single-variety wines and blends.

Cabernet Sauvignon wines are often medium to full bodied, with deep colour, noticeable acidity and firm or structured tannins. Tannin is the part of wine that can make your mouth feel dry or grippy. The grape commonly produces dark-fruit flavours such as blackcurrant, blackberry, mulberry and plum.

That is the classic outline, but Cabernet Sauvignon is not one fixed style. A cool-climate example may taste more restrained, herbal or finely structured, while a warmer-climate wine may seem riper, fuller and richer. Oak maturation can add cedar, spice or toast, and bottle age can soften tannin and bring more savoury notes.

Most still Cabernet Sauvignon is dry. In other words, it contains little perceptible residual sugar. A wine can taste of ripe blackberries or plums without being sweet.

## Where did Cabernet Sauvignon come from?

Cabernet Sauvignon originated in south-west France. It is a natural cross between Cabernet Franc and Sauvignon Blanc, although it is a distinct grape variety in its own right.

Today, Cabernet Sauvignon is grown in many wine regions, including Australia. It is made as a single-variety wine and is also commonly blended with varieties such as Merlot, Shiraz or Cabernet Franc. Blending can change the wine’s balance, texture, aroma and softness; it does not make the wine “less Cabernet”.

## What does Cabernet Sauvignon taste like?

Cabernet Sauvignon commonly has a combination of dark fruit, structure and freshness.

### Fruit and aroma

Depending on the region, ripeness and age, you may notice:

- blackcurrant or cassis
- blackberry
- mulberry
- plum
- violet
- mint
- dried herbs
- cedar
- spice or savoury notes

These are possibilities, not a checklist. One Cabernet Sauvignon may smell strongly of blackcurrant, while another may show more herbs, cedar or restrained black fruit.

### Body, acidity and tannin

Cabernet Sauvignon is often medium to full bodied. **Body** is the sense of weight or fullness that a wine has in your mouth.

It commonly has noticeable **acidity**, which gives the wine freshness and helps stop a rich wine from feeling flat. It also often has firm **tannin**, which can feel drying, grippy or slightly rough on the gums.

A young, concentrated Cabernet can feel quite firm. A softer style, a blended wine or a bottle with some age may feel rounder and more approachable. This is why two wines labelled Cabernet Sauvignon can taste noticeably different.

## Why does Cabernet Sauvignon vary so much?

The grape is only one part of the finished wine. Its style is shaped by several decisions and conditions.

### Climate and site

Moderate climates can help Cabernet Sauvignon develop ripe fruit while retaining acidity. Warmer conditions may produce fuller body and riper fruit, while cooler conditions can result in more restrained fruit, firmer acidity or more noticeable herbal character.

These are broad tendencies rather than rules. Vineyard site, vintage, harvest timing and winemaking also matter. Heat and drought stress can make it more difficult to achieve a balanced result.

### Ripeness and extraction

Riper grapes may contribute darker, richer fruit flavours and a fuller texture. Winemakers can also influence colour, flavour and tannin through techniques such as skin contact and extraction during fermentation.

More extraction can contribute to a deeper, more structured wine. The result depends on how that structure sits alongside fruit and acidity.

### Oak and blending

Oak maturation may add cedar, toast, spice, vanilla-like notes and extra texture. Some Cabernet Sauvignon spends significant time in oak, while other examples use less oak so that the fruit remains more prominent.

Blending is another way to shape the final wine. Merlot may contribute softness or mid-palate weight, while Shiraz or Cabernet Franc can alter the fruit, texture and aroma profile. The label or producer’s description may tell you whether a wine is a single-variety Cabernet Sauvignon or a blend.

### Bottle age

Cabernet Sauvignon often has the acidity, tannin, concentration and structure needed to age well. With time, some wines develop softer tannins and more savoury, cedar-like or earthy notes.

However, ageing potential is not a guarantee. Some Cabernet Sauvignon is made for earlier drinking, and not every bottle will improve after years in storage. An affordable, fresh example can be enjoyable now without needing a cellar.

## Australian Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon is an important Australian red grape variety, and Australian examples are not all alike.

### Margaret River

Margaret River Cabernet Sauvignon is often associated with medium- to full-bodied wines showing blackcurrant, cedar, violet and defined tannin. Some examples are blended with Merlot.

### Coonawarra

Coonawarra is commonly associated with Cabernet Sauvignon showing concentrated blackcurrant, mulberry and plum, along with firm tannin. Mint and dried-herb notes may also appear in some examples. The region’s terra rossa soils are an important part of its regional identity, but soil should not be treated as a simple explanation for every flavour in the glass.

### Yarra Valley

The Yarra Valley provides a useful cool-climate example. Its Cabernet Sauvignon can show elegant black fruit, with tannins ranging from soft and approachable to firm and drying.

These regions are useful starting points, not rigid style labels or rankings. Australian Cabernet is made in many other areas too, so the region on the label can offer a clue without telling you everything about the wine.

## How should a beginner choose Cabernet Sauvignon?

Start by considering the style you are likely to enjoy.

Look for clues such as:

- **“Medium bodied”** for a less weighty style.
- **“Full bodied”** for a richer, more powerful style.
- **“Soft tannins”** if you are new to structured red wine.
- **“Firm tannins”** if you enjoy a drying, savoury grip.
- **“Cool climate”** for potentially more restrained fruit and fresher structure.
- **“Oak matured”** if you enjoy cedar, toast or spice.
- **“Unoaked”** or lightly oaked if you want more direct fruit character.

These descriptions are useful guides, not guarantees. The region, vintage and producer’s approach can all change the result.

Serve Cabernet Sauvignon slightly cool rather than warm. If the wine tastes tight or strongly tannic, giving it time in the glass may help it open up. More detailed serving and decanting advice belongs in dedicated WineDaddy guides.

## What food goes with Cabernet Sauvignon?

Cabernet Sauvignon works well with rich, savoury foods because its body and tannin can match protein and substantial textures. Try it with:

- beef
- lamb
- darker game meats
- braised red-meat dishes
- roasted or grilled mushrooms
- firm cheeses

Steak is a familiar match, but it is not the only one. The important idea is balance: tannin can feel smoother alongside protein-rich food, while acidity helps keep a rich pairing fresh.

## Cabernet Sauvignon FAQs

### Is Cabernet Sauvignon sweet?

Most still Cabernet Sauvignon is dry. Dark-fruit flavours such as blackcurrant, blackberry and plum can taste ripe or rich without meaning the wine contains much residual sugar.

### Is Cabernet Sauvignon dry?

Usually, yes. “Dry” refers to the amount of perceptible residual sugar, not whether the wine tastes fruity. A dry Cabernet Sauvignon can still have generous fruit flavours.

### Is Cabernet Sauvignon full bodied?

It is often medium to full bodied, but not every Cabernet Sauvignon is heavy. Climate, ripeness, extraction, blending and oak can make a wine feel lighter, softer or richer.

### Is Cabernet Sauvignon very tannic?

Cabernet Sauvignon is commonly associated with noticeable, sometimes firm tannin. The intensity depends on the grapes, winemaking, blending and age of the wine.

### Can Cabernet Sauvignon age?

Some Cabernet Sauvignon can age for many years because of its acidity, tannin, concentration and structure. Many bottles, however, are made to enjoy earlier. The individual wine and its storage conditions matter.

### What is the simplest way to understand Cabernet Sauvignon?

Think of it as a red grape that often combines **dark fruit, freshness and structure**. Once you know that, the region, oak and age help explain why one Cabernet Sauvignon may feel very different from another.
