# Wine for a wedding: how to choose, pair and serve it

Wedding wine does not need to please every guest with a different bottle. The easiest approach is to choose a small, flexible range that suits the food, season, venue and people—and then plan the service carefully.

## Highlights

A practical wedding selection might include:

- **Sparkling wine** for a welcome drink, toast or celebration.
- **A fresh white or dry rosé** for warm weather, seafood, salads and lighter dishes.
- **A light- to medium-bodied red** for roasted food, richer dishes and guests who prefer red wine.

That is a starting point, not a rule. The best choices depend on how the food is prepared, how long the event runs, whether beer or cocktails are also available, and what the venue allows.

Choose by **style**, not just colour or grape variety. A wine label can suggest a starting point, but it cannot guarantee exactly how the wine will taste.

## Start with the wedding, not the wine label

Before choosing bottles, answer five practical questions:

1. **What is being served?** A seafood lunch needs a different range from a winter dinner built around roast meat.
2. **What will the weather and venue be like?** Outdoor summer weddings need reliable chilling and shade for the bottles.
3. **Who is drinking wine?** Consider wine drinkers, non-drinkers, under-18 guests and designated drivers.
4. **What other drinks are available?** Beer, spirits, cocktails and non-alcoholic drinks all affect wine demand.
5. **How will wine be served?** Table service, a staffed bar and self-service create different pouring patterns.

A short list is usually easier to buy, chill, explain and serve consistently than a collection of many different wines.

## A manageable wedding wine range

### Sparkling wine

Sparkling wine works naturally for arrival drinks and toasts. It can also be served with food, particularly lighter dishes and seafood. Keep it properly chilled and make sure the venue has a safe system for opening and serving it.

### White wine or rosé

A fresh white or dry rosé is versatile for warm conditions, salads, seafood and lighter poultry dishes. Crisp, dry whites can work well with oysters, prawns, shellfish and simply prepared fish.

The preparation matters. Seafood with butter, cream or strong seasoning may suit a fuller white than delicate raw or lightly dressed seafood.

### Red wine

A light- or medium-bodied red can cover many wedding dishes without overwhelming them. It may work with roast poultry, pork, grilled vegetables, pasta and moderately rich food, as well as some red-meat dishes.

There is no need to follow the rigid rule that white wine belongs only with white meat and red wine only with red meat. Sauce, cooking method, richness and seasoning matter just as much as the main ingredient.

### An optional dessert wine

A small dessert-wine service can be enjoyable when dessert is a defined course and the venue can serve it appropriately. Its suitability depends on the dessert itself, so it is often simpler to finish with sparkling, coffee or a non-alcoholic option instead.

## Matching wine with common wedding food

Use these as starting points rather than promises:

- **Seafood:** fresh, dry whites and some sparkling wines are useful starting points. Lemon, herbs, butter, cream and spices can change the best direction.
- **Poultry:** fresh white, rosé, lighter red or fuller white can all work, depending on whether the dish is grilled, roasted, creamy or strongly seasoned.
- **Red meat:** medium- to full-bodied reds often suit roasted or grilled flavours, but a powerful, tannic red is not compulsory.
- **Vegetarian food:** look at the preparation. Salads and herbs may suit fresh whites or rosé; mushrooms, roasted vegetables, pulses, tomato and smoky flavours may support a fuller white or red.
- **Spicy food:** fresh, lighter-bodied or slightly sweet wines can be more comfortable with chilli heat than highly alcoholic or strongly tannic reds. Remember that Australian Riesling is often dry; the variety name alone does not tell you the sweetness.
- **Dessert:** match the wine to the sweetness and intensity of the dish. A very sweet dessert can make a dry wine taste thin.

If the menu includes several different courses, choose wines that can bridge them rather than trying to find a separate bottle for every plate.

## How much wine do you need?

There is no reliable universal formula such as “one bottle per guest”. A 750 millilitre bottle contains a varying number of Australian standard drinks depending on its alcohol strength, but standard drinks are not the same thing as wedding servings.

Build your estimate from your event:

1. Count the guests.
2. Estimate how many are likely to drink wine.
3. Separate welcome and toast pours from meal service.
4. Consider the event’s length.
5. Note whether beer, cocktails, spirits and non-alcoholic drinks are available.
6. Consider whether wine is poured at the table, served by staff or freely available.
7. Ask whether unopened bottles can be returned, stored or taken home.
8. Add a modest contingency only after these assumptions are clear.

A long outdoor reception in hot weather may create different demand from a short lunch where wine is served only with food. Small toast pours also produce a different estimate from generous, continuous table pours.

Treat the result as a planning estimate, not a guarantee of what guests will drink.

## Chilling, storage and service

Approximate serving temperatures are useful:

- Sparkling: **5–10°C**
- White and rosé: **7–14°C**
- Light-bodied red: **12–17°C**
- Full-bodied red: **17–21°C**

For an Australian summer wedding, chill sparkling, white and rosé in advance and confirm that the venue has enough refrigeration or ice. Keep bottles out of direct sun and do not leave them in hot vehicles.

Most wine is sold in **750 mL bottles**, although half-bottles, magnums, cans and other formats exist. Larger bottles may reduce handling, but they can require different storage and pouring arrangements. Choose a format that suits the venue rather than assuming it is automatically more efficient.

You do not need elaborate glassware for every wine. Confirm with the venue or caterer what glasses are supplied, how they will be washed or replaced, and who is responsible for opening and pouring.

## Australian venue and responsible-service checks

Alcohol arrangements vary between states and territories, and venue policies can be more restrictive than the general legal position. In Australia, the legal drinking age is **18** for buying alcohol or drinking in a licensed venue.

Before buying anything, ask the venue or caterer:

- Is outside alcohol permitted?
- Is corkage charged?
- Must alcohol be supplied through the venue?
- Who buys, chills, stores and serves it?
- Are there delivery or pack-in time limits?
- Are particular bottle or can formats excluded?
- What happens to unopened bottles?
- Is a licensed bar or responsible-service team required?
- Are free water and non-alcoholic choices provided?

A standard drink contains **10 grams of pure alcohol**, and packaged alcohol labels show standard-drink information. This is useful context, but it is not a consumption calculator. Responsible service, water, food, non-alcoholic choices and safe transport should be part of the plan. Nobody should be encouraged to drive after drinking.

## A simple wedding wine checklist

- Taste a short list before committing.
- Match wine to the preparation, not only the main ingredient.
- Choose a manageable range.
- Estimate quantities from real event assumptions.
- Confirm BYO, corkage, storage and service rules.
- Arrange chilling before the day.
- Keep wine away from heat and direct sun.
- Provide water and appealing non-alcoholic drinks.
- Confirm who is responsible for service.
- Make a safe transport plan.

The goal is not to find one perfect wedding wine. It is to make the wine easy to enjoy, easy to serve and appropriate for the people and food sharing the day.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is sparkling wine necessary at a wedding?

No. It is useful for welcomes and toasts, but still wine, beer, non-alcoholic drinks or another celebration format may suit the event better.

### Should I serve both red and white wine?

Often, a small selection that includes a white or rosé and a red gives guests useful choice. The decision depends on the menu, season, guest preferences and other drinks available.

### Can I serve one wine with the whole meal?

Yes, if it is flexible and suits the menu. A fresh white, dry rosé or moderate-bodied red may cover several courses better than a highly specialised style.

### How early should wedding wine be chilled?

Confirm the venue’s refrigeration and ice arrangements early. The exact timing depends on the quantity, bottle format, storage space and service plan.

### Is BYO wine allowed?

Only if the venue permits it. Corkage, storage, approved formats and service conditions are venue-specific, so confirm them before purchasing.
