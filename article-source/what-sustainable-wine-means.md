# What sustainable wine means

## Highlights

- Sustainable wine considers environmental, social and economic impacts.
- It can include grape growing, winemaking, packaging, transport, workers and long-term business health.
- Sustainable is broader than organic, biodynamic or natural.
- A certification shows that a defined standard and scope have been assessed; it does not mean a wine has zero impact.
- A general claim such as “sustainable” needs evidence and clear explanation.
- The most useful question is: **what exactly is covered, and how has it been checked?**

## Quick answer

Sustainable wine means wine made through an ongoing effort to manage and improve its environmental, social and economic impacts.

That can include how grapes are grown, how the wine is made, how workers are treated, how water and energy are managed, and how the wine is packaged and transported. It can also include the long-term viability of the wine business and its relationship with local communities.

In Australia, **Sustainable Winegrowing Australia** is a voluntary industry programme that covers environmental, social and economic aspects of grapegrowing and winemaking. Businesses seeking certification are independently audited against relevant Australian Wine Industry Standards of Sustainable Practice.

The important qualification is **scope**. A certification or claim may apply to a vineyard, winery, wine business or particular practice. It does not automatically prove that every part of a bottle’s complete lifecycle has been assessed, or that the wine is impact-free.

## What does sustainability cover?

### Environmental impacts

Environmental sustainability may involve:

- water use and irrigation management;
- soil health and erosion;
- biodiversity and habitat;
- chemical and spray management;
- energy use and emissions;
- waste and wastewater;
- packaging materials and weight; and
- transport and distribution.

Australian wine production takes place in a country with highly variable rainfall, as well as recurring drought and heat risks. That makes water management and long-term resilience relevant questions. But an Australian origin label, by itself, does not prove that a particular vineyard has used water sustainably or is prepared for every climate risk.

Packaging is another piece of the picture. The Australian Wine Research Institute notes that lightweight packaging may reduce transport costs and associated emissions. That does not make every lightweight bottle the best option in every circumstance: material, manufacture, reuse, recycling, transport and disposal all matter.

### Social impacts

Social sustainability can include worker health and safety, lawful and respectful employment, training, supply-chain conduct and relationships with local communities.

This is one reason sustainability is broader than a farming method. A wine may be made from organically grown grapes, for example, without that claim telling you how the business manages worker welfare, packaging or long-term finances.

### Economic and business impacts

A wine business also needs to remain viable over time. Sustainability can therefore include record keeping, risk management, measurement, continual improvement and resilience.

This does not mean that a profitable business is automatically sustainable. It means that environmental and social improvements need to be managed in a way that can continue, rather than being treated as a one-off project or attractive label phrase.

## How does Australian sustainability certification work?

Sustainable Winegrowing Australia provides a recognised Australian industry framework. Participation is voluntary. Members seeking certification undergo an independent audit against the relevant standards for viticulture and/or winery operations. Certification is maintained through reassessment, with the programme’s certification process stating that successful audits are required every three years.

A certification is best understood as evidence that a defined operation has met a defined standard at the time and within the scope of the assessment. It is not a universal score for the wine, a guarantee of zero environmental impact or proof that one wine is “better” than another.

When you see a certification mark or named programme, ask:

1. Is this a recognised programme or simply a producer’s own wording?
2. What is covered: the vineyard, winery, wine business, bottle or one practice?
3. What is measured, documented or audited?
4. Was the assessment independent?
5. Are important conditions or limitations explained?

## Sustainable, organic, biodynamic and natural: what is the difference?

These terms overlap in conversation, but they do not mean the same thing.

**Organic wine** relates to organic production and processing requirements, including permitted materials and methods. In Australia, organic certification is not generally mandatory for products sold domestically, although claims still need to be truthful and supported. Organic claims for export are subject to certification requirements.

**Biodynamic wine** refers to a particular agricultural or production approach with its own standards or practices. Like organic wine, it may tell you useful information about production, but it does not automatically cover worker welfare, business resilience, packaging or transport.

**Natural wine** is generally used to describe a low-intervention or minimally manipulated style of winemaking. However, the term is used inconsistently and does not, by itself, establish certification or a comprehensive sustainability framework.

So a wine can be organic, biodynamic or made with minimal intervention without automatically demonstrating every dimension of sustainability. Conversely, certification under a sustainability programme does not necessarily mean that every aspect of the wine’s full lifecycle has the lowest possible impact.

## What does “sustainable” on a label mean?

The word may describe a certified programme, a documented practice or a broad producer statement. Those are not equivalent.

A specific claim such as “made using renewable electricity” or “packed in a lightweight bottle” should be read as a claim about that particular practice. It should not automatically be expanded into a conclusion about the entire wine.

Australian Competition and Consumer Commission guidance says environmental and sustainability claims should be accurate, based on reasonable grounds and supported by evidence. Broad words such as “sustainable” can mislead if they create an exaggerated impression or leave out important qualifications.

A green logo, leaf image or environmental phrase is not proof of certification. Look for the name of the programme, the relevant standard, the certified scope or a clear explanation of what the producer has actually measured.

## Why trade-offs matter

Sustainability is rarely a single yes-or-no verdict. Reducing one impact may create a different consideration elsewhere.

For example, lighter packaging may reduce the weight transported, while the overall result also depends on the material and what happens to it after use. A water-saving measure may be valuable in one setting, while its effectiveness depends on local conditions and how it is managed.

This does not make sustainability meaningless. It means that responsible claims should be specific. “This practice reduces one identified impact” is more useful than “this wine is completely green”.

## A practical checklist for choosing with confidence

When reading a label or producer website, ask:

- Is there a named certification or programme?
- What part of production does it cover?
- Is the claim independently audited or self-described?
- Is it about organic grapes, energy, water, packaging, biodiversity or another specific practice?
- Can you find evidence or a standard behind the wording?
- Are the limits of the claim explained?
- Does the language suggest that one practice represents the whole wine?

You do not need to become an auditor before buying a bottle. The simple habit is to treat sustainability as a question of **scope and evidence**, not as a magic word.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is sustainable wine the same as organic wine?

No. Organic wine concerns organic production requirements. Sustainability is broader and may include environmental, social and economic performance across the vineyard, winery and business.

### Is natural wine automatically sustainable?

No. “Natural wine” is used inconsistently and does not by itself prove certification, low environmental impact or responsible social and economic practices.

### Does certification mean a wine has no environmental impact?

No. Certification means the relevant operation has met a defined standard within a defined scope. It does not mean the wine has zero impact or that every lifecycle stage has been assessed.

### Is sustainable wine better for the planet?

A supported sustainability claim can provide useful evidence about particular practices or a defined programme. It should not be treated as proof that every impact is lower than every alternative.

### What is the best question to ask?

Ask: **“What exactly is covered, and what evidence supports the claim?”**
