# What causes earthy flavours in wine?

“Earthy” is a broad tasting description, not a diagnosis. It can describe attractive savoury aromas such as forest floor, dried leaves, mushroom and undergrowth. It can also describe unwanted characters such as damp earth, mould, beetroot, barnyard or medicinal notes.

Several things may contribute: the grapes and their condition, fermentation organisms, maturation, bottle age, storage, or compounds associated with taints and faults. A single earthy impression cannot tell you which cause is responsible.

## Highlights

Earthy flavours in wine can come from different combinations of grape chemistry, microorganisms and wine development.

A mature wine may develop savoury, dried-leaf or forest-floor aromas as its fruit character changes. Fermentation organisms can also influence aroma. For example, some yeast strains produce more savoury or earthy impressions than others, although that does not mean wild yeast always makes earthy wine.

Other earthy smells may signal a taint. Geosmin is one compound associated with damp-earth, musty, muddy, mushroom-like and beetroot-like aromas. It can be linked with fungal contamination of grapes. The yeast *Brettanomyces bruxellensis* can produce phenolic characters described as leather, barnyard, horsey, medicinal, spicy or sometimes earthy.

That does not mean every earthy wine contains geosmin or Brett. Whether the character seems complex or faulty depends on its intensity, balance, persistence, the wine’s style, its age and storage, and your own sensitivity.

## What does “earthy” mean?

Wine tasters use “earthy” for a family of overlapping impressions, including:

- forest floor and damp leaves
- mushroom or truffle
- roots, beetroot or wet soil
- leather, barnyard or horse
- medicinal or phenolic aromas
- mouldy, musty or damp-cellar smells

These are not interchangeable. “Mushroom” in a mature, savoury wine may be quite different from a persistent mouldy smell. The word is useful as a starting point, but better tasting notes describe the specific impression as well.

It is also important to separate **flavour perception** from **cause**. When you say a wine smells earthy, you are reporting what you perceive. You are not necessarily identifying a particular compound, vineyard soil or winemaking decision.

## Four possible sources of earthy character

### Grape condition and vineyard context

Grape variety, growing conditions, grape condition and the microorganisms present on fruit can all influence the wine’s final aroma. Fungal contamination is one possible pathway: research has linked geosmin and other fungal volatiles with earthy or musty aromas in grape products.

This is not the same as saying wine literally tastes of the soil. An earthy note does not prove that soil particles have transferred into the wine, nor does it automatically prove a particular terroir. Wine aroma results from interactions between grapes, microorganisms, processing and the finished wine’s chemistry.

### Fermentation and microbial activity

Yeasts and other microorganisms transform grape juice during fermentation and can create many different aroma compounds. The choice and activity of microbial strains can shift a wine towards fruity, floral, savoury, sulphurous or earthy impressions.

One important example is *Brettanomyces*, often shortened to “Brett”. It can produce volatile phenols associated with leather, barnyard, horse, medicinal, spicy and sometimes earthy characters. In some wines, a small amount may be perceived as savoury complexity. At higher or more dominant levels, the character may overwhelm the fruit and be judged unpleasant.

So “earthy” does not equal “Brett”. Leather, barnyard or medicinal notes may provide additional clues, but sensory description alone cannot confirm the cause.

### Maturation and bottle development

As wine develops, its original fruit aromas can become less prominent while savoury, dried, leafy, tobacco-like or forest-floor impressions become more noticeable. These are sometimes called tertiary aromas.

Age does not guarantee earthy character, however. Bottle development depends on the wine, its closure, storage conditions and many other factors. A mature wine showing attractive savoury detail is not automatically faulty, while an unpleasant earthy smell in an older wine is not automatically a normal sign of age.

### Musty or tainted aromas

Some earthy impressions are more likely to make a wine seem tainted: damp mould, stagnant water, wet cardboard-like mustiness or an intense beetroot smell. Geosmin is a well-known example of a compound associated with earthy and musty odours, and it can be noticeable at very low concentrations.

There are other possible musty and mould-related compounds too. They do not all smell identical, which is another reason not to diagnose a wine from the word “earthy” alone.

If a bottle smells clearly tainted or unpleasant, you do not need to keep drinking it to prove what happened. Set it aside and, where appropriate, speak with the retailer, producer or a qualified wine professional. The descriptor alone does not establish a health risk.

## Complexity or fault?

There is no universal point at which earthy becomes “good” or “bad”. Consider four questions:

1. **How intense is it?** A subtle savoury note may sit behind the fruit; a dominant musty smell may take over the wine.
2. **Is it balanced?** Does it work with the wine’s fruit, acidity, tannin and texture?
3. **How long does it persist?** A brief forest-floor note is different from a persistent mouldy or medicinal aroma.
4. **Does it fit the style?** Some mature or savoury wines are expected to show less obvious primary fruit.

Your own sensitivity matters, too. The same compound may seem integrated to one taster and overwhelming to another. The wine’s alcohol, oak, acidity and other aromas can also mask or amplify particular characters.

## A practical way to taste an earthy wine

Try describing the wine in stages rather than guessing its cause.

### 1. Be more specific

Ask whether the aroma is closer to:

- dried leaves or forest floor
- mushroom or truffle
- beetroot or damp earth
- leather, barnyard or medicinal notes
- mould or a musty cellar

### 2. Consider context

Is the wine young or mature? Is it intended to be fruity, savoury or developed? Has it been stored in conditions you trust? Storage and age can change how aromas appear.

### 3. Check balance and persistence

Does the earthy note support the wine, or dominate it? Does it become more obvious as the wine sits in the glass? Does the wine remain fresh and pleasant, or does the character seem stale, mouldy or medicinal?

### 4. Avoid a single-cause conclusion

A useful tasting note might be: “Savoury, leafy and earthy, with dried-fruit character.” Or: “Earthy and musty, with a persistent medicinal note.” Both describe the experience without claiming certainty about the chemistry.

The most confident tasters are not those who name a fault instantly. They are the ones who can describe what they notice, recognise uncertainty and decide whether the wine is enjoyable.

## The main takeaway

Earthy wine character can come from development, fermentation, grape condition, microbial activity or taint-related compounds. It is not automatically terroir, Brett, age or poor winemaking. Start with the precise aroma, then consider its intensity, balance, persistence and context.

In Australian wine conversations, “savoury”, “mouldy”, “musty”, “forest floor” and “barnyard” are more useful descriptions than treating every non-fruit aroma as simply earthy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does earthy wine mean it tastes of soil?

No. “Earthy” is a sensory comparison. It does not prove that soil itself has transferred into the wine or that the wine’s character directly reflects its vineyard soil.

### Is earthy wine faulty?

Not necessarily. Savoury, leafy or forest-floor notes can be part of a wine’s style or development. A dominant mouldy, musty, medicinal or otherwise unpleasant character may indicate a fault or taint.

### Does earthy wine always mean Brett?

No. *Brettanomyces* can contribute earthy and related phenolic aromas, but earthy character has many possible causes. A technical assessment may be needed to identify the cause.

### What is geosmin in wine?

Geosmin is a highly odour-active compound associated with earthy, damp, musty, muddy and beetroot-like aromas. It is one possible cause of earthy taint, not an explanation for every earthy wine.

### Can you identify the cause just by tasting?

Usually not with confidence. Tasting can help describe the character and suggest possibilities, but one descriptor does not establish a chemical cause or fault diagnosis.
