# Amarone della Valpolicella: A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Amarone della Valpolicella is a protected Italian DOCG red wine from Veneto.
- It comes from the Valpolicella area, in the province of Verona.
- The grapes are dried before fermentation in a process called *appassimento*.
- Drying concentrates the grapes, helping create a rich, full-bodied and high-alcohol wine.
- Amarone can taste intensely fruity and almost sweet while still being technically dry.
- Recioto della Valpolicella is the related sweet wine; Amarone is fermented further.
- “Appassimento” is a technique, not another name for Amarone.
- On an Australian bottle, look for “Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG”.

## Quick answer: what is Amarone della Valpolicella?

Amarone della Valpolicella is a protected Italian red-wine denomination from the Valpolicella area of Veneto, near Verona. It is made principally from Corvina and other authorised red grapes. Before fermentation, the harvested grapes undergo *appassimento*: controlled drying that removes water and concentrates the fruit.

The resulting wine is usually full-bodied, powerful and high in alcohol, with flavours that may suggest ripe or dried red fruit, spice, chocolate, tobacco and balsamic notes. Despite that richness, Amarone is made as a dry wine rather than a sweet dessert wine.

That distinction is the key to understanding it. **Dryness concerns residual sugar; it does not mean a wine must be light, low in alcohol or lacking in ripe fruit flavour.**

## Where does Amarone come from?

Amarone’s geographic hierarchy is:

**Italy → Veneto → province of Verona → Valpolicella production area → Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG**

Valpolicella lies in the foothills north-west of Verona, between Lake Garda and the Lessini Mountains. From central Verona, the area is roughly 20–30 minutes by car, depending on the particular locality, route and traffic. Treat that as orientation rather than a precise travel promise.

The wider Valpolicella area includes the historic **Valpolicella Classica** area and the **Valpantena** valley, as well as other defined parts of the production zone. These are not simply informal town names: where they appear on a bottle, they are regulated geographic references within the denomination framework.

The names can be easy to confuse:

- **Veneto** is the Italian region.
- **Valpolicella** is the broader wine area and a separate DOC denomination.
- **Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG** is the protected denomination for this particular wine.
- **Classico** and **Valpantena** may identify regulated geographic references within the denomination.
- **Verona** is the nearest useful city for orientation, but it does not define the whole denomination.

## What does DOCG mean?

DOCG is an Italian denomination category. It indicates that the wine must meet defined requirements for origin, grape varieties, production and ageing. It is not an Australian legal classification, and it does not mean every bottle will taste identical or suit every drinker.

The protected wording to look for is:

**Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG**

A label may also include terms such as **Classico**, **Valpantena** or **Riserva**, where the applicable requirements are met. A wine labelled simply *appassimento* is not automatically Amarone. Appassimento describes a drying technique that can be used for wines from other places and under different rules.

## How is Amarone made?

The distinctive step is drying the grapes before fermentation. In the Valpolicella tradition, grapes are dried in suitable rooms known as *fruttai*. The Consortium describes a drying period of approximately 100–120 days, although this should be understood as a regional description rather than an immutable experience for every wine.

As the grapes lose water, the remaining juice and berry compounds become more concentrated. This can contribute to Amarone’s intense fruit character, weight and texture. Drying does not, by itself, determine the final sweetness of the wine: fermentation still affects how much sugar remains.

After drying, the grapes are fermented. The reviewed production requirements support a minimum finished alcohol level of 14% alcohol by volume, or ABV. This is a legal minimum, not a promise that every bottle will have the same strength.

The principal grape is **Corvina**, with **Rondinella** and other authorised red varieties also permitted under the production specification. Exact proportions are regulated and should not be confused with a claim that every Amarone uses the same blend.

## Amarone versus Recioto

Amarone and Recioto della Valpolicella share the idea of making wine from dried grapes, but their fermentation outcomes differ.

- **Amarone** is fermented to a dry, high-alcohol style.
- **Recioto** retains substantial sweetness because fermentation is stopped or managed before all the fermentable sugar is converted.

So dried grapes do not automatically produce sweet wine. They provide a concentrated starting point; the winemaking determines the finished balance.

For a fuller explanation of dryness and perceived sweetness, see [what sweetness means in wine](/what-is-sweetness-in-wine/).

## What does Amarone taste like?

Amarone is typically:

- deep red, sometimes developing garnet tones with age;
- full-bodied and concentrated;
- high in alcohol;
- rich in ripe or dried red-fruit flavours;
- warm, velvety and textured;
- supported by acidity and tannin.

You may also notice spice, tobacco, balsamic or chocolate-like characters. These are tendencies, not guarantees. The final style varies with the site, season, grape blend, drying conditions, fermentation, maturation and bottle age.

Its richness can make it seem sweet even when it is technically dry. Think of the difference between a dry wine with very ripe plum and raisin-like flavours and a genuinely sweet wine with noticeable residual sugar. They may overlap in flavour, but they are not the same thing.

## Amarone versus other Valpolicella wines

**Valpolicella DOC** is a separate denomination and is generally made from fresh grapes rather than the extended dried-grape process associated with Amarone. It is often lighter, fresher and lower in alcohol, although styles vary.

**Recioto della Valpolicella DOCG** is the sweet counterpart made from dried grapes.

**Generic appassimento wines** are wines made using grape drying. They may come from other Italian regions, use different grapes and follow different production and ageing rules. The word *appassimento* alone does not establish Amarone origin.

These wines should not be arranged as a simple quality ladder. They are distinct categories with different methods and styles.

## How should Australians read an Amarone label?

On an imported bottle, the Italian protected name may appear alongside Australian label information. Look for:

- **Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG**;
- **Classico**, **Valpantena** or **Riserva**, if included;
- alcohol content shown as ABV;
- standard-drink information;
- the Australian pregnancy health warning where required;
- country-of-origin information.

DOCG should be read as an Italian denomination term, not as an Australian equivalent. For more help, see [how to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/).

## Serving Amarone

A practical serving range is approximately **16–18°C**. The Valpolicella Consortium gives a broader suggestion of 16–20°C, but serving a powerful red too warm can make its alcohol feel more prominent.

Use a generous red-wine glass. Decanting can help a young, concentrated or reductive bottle open up, but it is not compulsory for every Amarone. Older bottles may need careful handling rather than lengthy aeration. See [when wine should be decanted](/when-to-decant-wine/).

Food starting points include braised beef, slow-cooked meats, game, rich mushroom dishes and mature hard cheeses. Because Amarone must reach at least 14% ABV, pour modestly and treat it as a strong wine rather than a casual dessert drink.

## FAQs

### Is Amarone a sweet wine?

Usually not. Amarone is made in a dry style, although its concentrated fruit and alcohol can make it taste rich or almost sweet.

### Is Amarone fortified?

No. Amarone is not a fortified wine. Its high alcohol comes principally from fermentation of concentrated grape sugars, rather than the addition of spirit.

### Does Amarone always contain the same grapes?

No. Corvina is the principal grape, but authorised proportions and supporting varieties are defined by the production specification.

### Does every Amarone improve with age?

Not necessarily. Some can develop complexity in bottle, but cellaring depends on the individual wine, vintage, producer, storage and balance.

### Is every appassimento wine Amarone?

No. *Appassimento* is a production technique. Amarone della Valpolicella is a specific protected denomination with its own geographic and production requirements.
