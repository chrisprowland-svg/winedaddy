# What is heat damage in wine?

## Highlights

- Heat damage is deterioration caused or accelerated by excessive heat exposure during storage or transport.
- It is different from simply serving a wine warm.
- Heat can make wine develop too quickly, reducing freshness and changing its aroma, flavour, colour and balance.
- Cooked or stewed fruit, muted freshness, premature ageing, leakage or a pushed cork may be warning signs—but none proves heat damage alone.
- A bottle can look normal and still taste affected.
- Chilling can improve the presentation of a warm, sound wine, but it cannot reverse chemical changes caused by heat.
- In Australia, hot cars, direct sun, garages, sheds and exposed delivery areas are practical risks to avoid.

## Quick answer

Heat damage in wine is a loss or alteration of quality caused or accelerated by excessive heat exposure, usually during storage or transport. Heat can speed up the chemical reactions involved in ageing, causing a wine to develop faster than intended.

The result might be less freshness, muted fruit, changed colour, altered texture or flavours that seem cooked, stewed, jammy, nutty or caramelised. Some heat-affected wines may also show premature oxidation. The exact result depends on the temperature reached, how long the exposure lasted, the wine’s style and composition, its closure, oxygen exposure and its previous storage history.

This is not the same as serving a wine warm. A warm but sound wine may taste heavier or more alcoholic and seem less refreshing. Chilling it to an appropriate serving temperature can improve its balance. If the wine has already been damaged by heat, chilling may make it more pleasant to assess, but it cannot undo the chemical changes.

## How can you recognise it?

Heat damage is usually suspected rather than proven from one clue. After opening, possible signs include:

- cooked, stewed or unusually jammy fruit;
- muted or tired aromas;
- a flat impression or reduced freshness;
- premature dried-fruit, nutty or caramelised notes;
- browning or an unexpectedly aged colour;
- less clear varietal character.

These descriptions can overlap with normal bottle age, oxidation unrelated to heat, closure problems, winemaking choices or bottle variation. An older wine may naturally develop dried-fruit or nutty aromas, for example, so those characters do not automatically indicate poor storage.

Packaging can provide clues. Heat causes the liquid and air space in a sealed bottle to expand, which can place stress on the closure. Leakage, staining beneath the capsule, seepage around the cork or a cork that appears to have moved may be consistent with heat exposure.

But appearance is not a verdict. A leaked bottle may have a packaging problem without major sensory damage, while a bottle that looks completely intact may still taste affected. If you can, compare it with another bottle of the same wine that has a known storage history. That gives you a better basis for judging whether the wine has developed unusually.

## What affects the risk?

There is no single temperature that proves every wine is ruined. Risk generally increases with greater heat and longer exposure, while repeated heating and cooling can also stress closures and encourage air ingress.

Wine style, composition, closure, oxygen exposure and previous storage all matter. A short warm spell is not automatically equivalent to prolonged exposure in a hot vehicle. Similarly, a wine’s colour or grape variety cannot by itself tell you whether it has been heat damaged.

Technical storage guidance commonly describes around 10–16°C as a long-term storage range and warns against much higher temperatures. These figures are general guidance, not a pass-or-fail test for an individual bottle.

Heat can contribute to oxidation, but the two terms are not interchangeable. A wine can oxidise without excessive heat, and heat can affect a wine in ways that go beyond oxidation, including accelerated ageing, aroma loss and closure stress.

## How can you prevent heat damage?

Treat wine as something that should be kept cool, shaded and relatively stable:

- do not leave bottles in a parked car, especially in direct sun;
- bring deliveries indoors promptly during hot weather;
- avoid exposed verandas, balconies and windowsills;
- do not use garages, garden sheds, roof spaces or corrugated-metal areas for long-term storage;
- keep wine away from ovens, heaters and other heat sources;
- where practical, avoid transporting or receiving wine during the hottest part of an Australian summer.

If a delivery arrives visibly compromised, keep your description factual and contact the retailer or producer. You may not be able to prove the cause from the bottle alone.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can chilling fix heat-damaged wine?

No. Chilling can improve the presentation of a warm but sound wine. It cannot reverse chemical changes or restore freshness lost through heat exposure.

### Is a pushed cork proof of heat damage?

No. A moved cork or leakage can indicate thermal expansion or closure stress, but it does not prove that the wine’s sensory quality has been damaged.

### Is heat-damaged wine undrinkable?

Not necessarily. The severity varies. Open it, smell and taste a small amount at a suitable serving temperature, and compare it with a known-good bottle if possible.

### Is heat damage the same as oxidation?

No. Heat can accelerate oxidation and premature ageing, but oxidation has other causes, and heat damage can involve changes beyond oxidation.
