# What is Rossese?

Rossese is a wine-grape name, especially associated with a red grape from Liguria in north-west Italy. Its best-known red-wine expression is Rossese di Dolceacqua, a protected regional designation from western Liguria.

There is one important complication: **Rossese is not always a single, unambiguous grape identity**. Italian grape references record several distinct varieties carrying the name, including red Rossese nero and white Rossese bianco. In most discussions of Ligurian red wine, however, Rossese means the red grape associated with western Liguria.

## Highlights

- Rossese is a grape name, not a generic term for red wine.
- The principal red-wine context is Liguria, particularly the province of Imperia.
- Rossese di Dolceacqua is a protected wine designation, not another name for every Rossese wine.
- Rossese wines are often perfumed, floral and red-fruited.
- They can be medium-bodied, supple and relatively light in tannin, although examples vary.
- Site, ripeness, vineyard work and winemaking can all change the final style.
- On a bottle, distinguish the grape name from the regional designation.

## Where does Rossese come from?

Rossese is strongly associated with Liguria, the coastal region in north-west Italy. The red-wine heartland is western Liguria, particularly the inland valleys and hills around Dolceacqua in the province of Imperia.

Dolceacqua is an inland town near Ventimiglia and the western Ligurian coast. Imperia is a useful provincial reference point, while Genoa provides a broader orientation: Dolceacqua is roughly 2½–3 hours west of Genoa by car, depending on the route and traffic. That is travel orientation, not a precise promise.

The protected designation **Rossese di Dolceacqua DOC**, also permitted to appear as **Dolceacqua DOC**, covers a legally defined area around Dolceacqua and nearby municipalities including Apricale, Baiardo, Camporosso, Isolabona, Perinaldo, Pigna, Rocchetta Nervina, San Biagio della Cima and Soldano, along with specified parts of other localities.

The place name and the grape name are different things. A town may be associated with an appellation without every wine made there qualifying for that appellation.

## Rossese versus Rossese di Dolceacqua

This is the distinction most likely to help when reading a label:

- **Rossese** is a grape name or, depending on the label and designation, part of a wine category.
- **Rossese di Dolceacqua DOC** is a protected regional designation for wine made principally from Rossese in a defined area.
- **Dolceacqua DOC** is the accepted shortened form of that designation in the relevant specification.

So, a bottle labelled Rossese di Dolceacqua tells you about both grape and place. A bottle labelled simply Rossese does not automatically qualify for the Dolceacqua DOC.

Another name to watch is **Riviera Ligure di Ponente DOC**. This is a separate Ligurian designation that also includes a Rossese category. It is not the same geographical indication as Rossese di Dolceacqua.

If an unfamiliar bottle makes the distinction seem confusing, start with the wording that identifies the protected designation. A grape tells you what was grown; a regional designation tells you where and under which production rules the wine qualifies.

## What does Rossese wine taste like?

Rossese is often described as a fragrant, red-fruited red wine. Depending on the example, you may find floral aromas and flavours associated with rose, strawberry, raspberry or blueberry. Some descriptions also point towards savoury Mediterranean vegetation.

In the glass, Rossese di Dolceacqua can be ruby-coloured and relatively transparent. It may feel medium-bodied, soft or supple, with light or restrained tannin rather than the firm, drying grip associated with some fuller-bodied red varieties.

Those are useful starting points, not rules. Rossese is not guaranteed to be pale, light-bodied or low in tannin. Acidity, colour, alcohol, savouriness and structure can differ between wines.

That variation is part of what makes the variety interesting. A vineyard’s altitude, exposure and local climate may influence ripening. Vineyard work and harvest timing also matter, as do extraction, ageing and the producer’s winemaking choices. Two wines made from Rossese can therefore share a name while offering noticeably different experiences.

A lighter colour does not automatically mean a wine is thin or lacking concentration. Some varieties naturally produce wines with colour transparency while still offering strong aroma and flavour.

## How should a newcomer approach Rossese?

Treat Rossese as a chance to compare **grape and place**, rather than expecting one fixed flavour profile.

When looking at a bottle, ask:

1. Does the label name Rossese as the grape, or does it name a protected designation?
2. Does it say Rossese di Dolceacqua or Dolceacqua?
3. Is it from the separate Riviera Ligure di Ponente DOC?
4. What does the wine’s colour, aroma and texture actually tell you once it is in the glass?

Expect perfume and red-fruit character as possible signposts, but leave room for savoury notes, freshness, warmth and differences in texture. Rossese can be a useful reminder that grape variety is only part of a wine’s identity.

Australian drinkers may encounter Rossese as an imported or specialist Italian variety. Its unfamiliarity is not a reason to assume anything about its quality, price or availability. If you see it, read the grape name and regional wording together.

## Is Rossese the same as Rossese bianco?

No. Rossese bianco is a white-berried grape that also carries the Rossese name. It should not be silently treated as the same variety as the red Rossese context associated with Rossese di Dolceacqua.

This naming history is why a careful explanation says “Rossese is a name used for several Italian grape varieties” before focusing on the red Ligurian example. The related Provençal name Tibouren is sometimes discussed in wine references, but it should not be treated as a simple synonym here.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Rossese a red wine?

Rossese is primarily a grape name, not a wine colour. In the Ligurian context most relevant to wine drinkers, it usually refers to the red grape Rossese nero. The name has also been used for other distinct varieties, including Rossese bianco.

### Is Rossese di Dolceacqua a grape?

No. Rossese di Dolceacqua is a protected regional wine designation made principally from the Rossese grape in western Liguria.

### Is every Rossese wine from Dolceacqua?

No. Rossese can appear in different regional or varietal contexts. A bottle labelled Rossese di Dolceacqua or Dolceacqua belongs to that protected designation; a bottle labelled simply Rossese does not automatically do so.

### Is Rossese similar to Pinot Noir?

Both can produce fragrant red wines with relatively moderate structure, but this broad comparison is not a reliable identification test. Rossese varies with site and winemaking, so it is better understood on its own terms.

### What should I look for in a Rossese?

Begin with floral and red-fruit aromas, ruby colour and a potentially supple, lightly tannic palate. Then notice how the particular wine balances freshness, savouriness, warmth and structure.

### Where is Rossese grown?

The best-known red-wine association is western Liguria in north-west Italy, especially the area around Dolceacqua and the province of Imperia. The name itself has been used for several distinct Italian grape varieties, so it is safer not to treat every Rossese reference as identical.
