# What Is Dealcoholised Wine Production? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Dealcoholised wine usually starts as fermented wine, not grape juice.
- Alcohol is then reduced or removed using controlled separation technology.
- Common approaches include membrane processes, vacuum-based separation and distillation.
- Reverse osmosis is one possible technique, not a synonym for dealcoholisation.
- Removing alcohol can change aroma, body, warmth, sweetness perception and balance.
- “Low-alcohol”, “no-alcohol”, “alcohol-free” and “dealcoholised” are not automatically interchangeable terms.
- In Australia, the declared alcohol by volume (ABV) is an important label detail to check.

## Quick answer: how is dealcoholised wine made?

Dealcoholised wine production is the processing of fermented wine to reduce some or almost all of its ethanol. In broad terms, grapes are made into wine through fermentation first. The wine is then prepared for alcohol removal, treated using membrane, vacuum or distillation-based technology, and adjusted before packaging.

A typical pathway looks like this:

**Grapes → fermentation → wine preparation → alcohol separation → possible aroma recovery → final adjustment → packaging**

That is a guide to the general process, not a compulsory recipe. Producers may use different technologies, combine methods or adjust the sequence depending on the wine and the desired final alcohol level.

The important distinction is that dealcoholised wine normally begins with wine. It is not simply grape juice with alcohol added or removed. Fermentation creates the original wine’s alcohol, acidity, aroma and structure. Dealcoholisation is a later processing stage designed to remove ethanol while retaining as much of the wine’s character as possible.

For the usual starting point, see [how wine is made](/how-wine-is-made/), [what fermentation does in wine](/what-is-fermentation/) and [alcoholic fermentation](/alcoholic-fermentation/).

## The main stages of dealcoholised wine production

### 1. Making a fermented wine base

Grapes are harvested and processed into must or juice. Yeast then converts grape sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide, creating a fermented wine base.

The base may be clarified, stabilised, blended or otherwise prepared before alcohol removal. The treatment depends on the wine’s composition and the intended finished style.

### 2. Separating alcohol

The wine is passed through one or more controlled separation processes. These are intended to reduce ethanol, but they can also affect other components, particularly volatile aroma compounds.

Some systems separate and retain aroma fractions before or during alcohol removal. Those fractions may later be returned to the wine.

### 3. Rebalancing and packaging

After alcohol reduction, the wine may be blended or adjusted for balance. Aroma, acidity, sweetness perception, texture, carbonation, colour and other sensory properties can all be considered.

Not every product uses every adjustment, and the exact additions or sequence vary. Final alcohol concentration and stability are checked before packaging and labelling.

## How is alcohol removed from wine?

### Membrane processes

Membrane techniques use selective barriers to separate parts of the wine. The wine is divided into fractions, which may then be treated and recombined.

[Reverse osmosis](/reverse-osmosis-in-winemaking/) is one membrane-based option. Pressure pushes wine against a selective membrane, allowing some components to pass through more readily than others. The resulting fractions can be processed and brought back together.

Reverse osmosis is therefore one possible tool within the broader dealcoholisation workflow. It is not used in every dealcoholised wine, and it does not define the whole category.

Other membrane approaches include techniques such as nanofiltration, pervaporation and osmotic distillation. They differ in how they separate alcohol and other components, so they should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Vacuum-based separation and distillation

Lower pressure changes the conditions under which volatile components and ethanol separate. Vacuum-based processing can reduce the need for the high temperatures associated with ordinary atmospheric boiling, but it does not guarantee that aroma will be perfectly preserved.

Pressure, temperature, processing time and the wine itself all influence the result.

### Spinning-cone technology

A spinning-cone column uses rotating cones and controlled conditions to separate volatile fractions and ethanol in stages. Aroma compounds may be captured separately and later used in the treated wine.

It is one commercial approach among several. The finished result depends on the number of processing stages, the target alcohol level and how aroma recovery and blending are managed.

## Why does removing alcohol change wine?

Alcohol is more than a number on a label. In wine, ethanol contributes to:

- the release and perception of aromas;
- warmth or “hotness”;
- body and fullness;
- bitterness and sweetness balance;
- the perception of acidity and astringency.

That means removing alcohol can change more than the drink’s strength. A dealcoholised wine may seem less aromatic, lighter-bodied or less warm. Acidity or astringency may seem more prominent, while sweetness perception and overall balance may also change.

These are possibilities, not universal outcomes. The result depends on the original wine, grape variety, processing method, pressure and temperature, the amount of alcohol removed and the final ABV. Partial alcohol reduction may produce a different result from treatment designed to reach below 0.5% ABV.

Some producers may recover aroma fractions, blend wines or make other final adjustments to improve balance. The aim is generally to retain a recognisable wine character, but dealcoholised wine should not be assumed to taste identical to the original wine.

For more background, see [alcohol in wine](/alcohol-in-wine/).

## Dealcoholised, no-alcohol and low-alcohol: what is the difference?

These expressions are related, but they are not automatically synonyms.

- **Dealcoholised** describes a process: alcohol has been reduced or removed from wine.
- **Low-alcohol** describes a product with less alcohol than many conventional wines, but its precise meaning can depend on the jurisdiction and label context.
- **No-alcohol** and **alcohol-free** are consumer-facing expressions whose meaning may also vary according to the applicable rules.

A useful international reference point is **0.5% ABV**. The OIV distinguishes a wine-derived beverage below 0.5% ABV from a partially dealcoholised beverage at or above 0.5% ABV but below the applicable minimum strength for wine.

That distinction should not be treated as a complete Australian legal dictionary for every front-label phrase. In Australia, check the declared ABV and the wording required on the label rather than assuming that “no-alcohol” or “alcohol-free” always means exactly 0.0% ABV.

The broader category is covered in [no-alcohol and dealcoholised wine](/no-alcohol-and-dealcoholised-wine/). This article focuses specifically on how the product is made.

## What should Australian drinkers look for on the label?

FSANZ states that beverages containing **0.5% ABV or more** must provide alcohol-content information.

For beverages containing at least 0.5% ABV but no more than 1.15% ABV, the label must use wording to the effect of “contains not more than X% alcohol by volume”. Above 1.15% ABV, alcohol can be declared as ABV or millilitres per 100 millilitres.

Beverages containing more than 0.5% ABV must also include a standard-drinks statement.

This makes the declared ABV an important first check. A product described as dealcoholised may still contain residual alcohol. Imported products sold in Australia may have been produced under different overseas conventions, but they must meet applicable Australian requirements for sale here.

Terminology and labelling rules can change, so current official guidance and the product’s own label should take priority.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is dealcoholised wine still wine?

It is wine-derived and normally begins as fermented wine. However, whether the finished product may legally be called “wine” depends on the applicable jurisdiction and product rules. The legal description on a particular Australian bottle should not be assumed from the production process alone.

### Does dealcoholised wine contain any alcohol?

It can. Some products retain residual alcohol, including products below 0.5% ABV. Check the declared ABV rather than assuming that “dealcoholised”, “no-alcohol” or “alcohol-free” means zero alcohol.

### Does dealcoholised wine taste like regular wine?

It may retain wine-derived aromas and flavours, but removing alcohol can affect aroma intensity, body, warmth, sweetness perception, acidity and balance. Similarity varies with the original wine and the technology used.
