# How wine medals work

## Highlights

- A wine medal is an award from a particular competition—not a universal score for every wine.
- Gold, silver and bronze can mean different things in different competitions.
- A medal usually recognises a judged entry in a particular category and competition year.
- A trophy is a separate, higher-level award in many competitions, not simply another medal colour.
- A medal does not guarantee that you will enjoy the wine, that it is good value or that it is better than every unmedalled wine.
- On an Australian bottle or shelf card, check the competition name, award year, medal level, category and vintage.

## Quick answer: what does a wine medal mean?

A wine medal means that a particular wine entry was assessed favourably in a particular competition, under that competition’s rules and judging system.

That is useful information—but only within its context. Competitions differ in their categories, entry rules, scoring methods, judging panels, medal thresholds and trophy structures. There is no single global meaning for “gold”, “silver” or “bronze”.

So, when you see a medal on a bottle, shelf card or retailer page, treat it as one clue rather than a final verdict. Check which competition awarded it, when the award was made, which category the wine entered and whether the claim applies to the exact wine and vintage being sold.

A medal cannot guarantee that you will like the wine. It does not, by itself, prove that the wine is the best, offers good value, will improve with age or is better than every wine that did not receive a medal.

## What happens in a wine competition?

Every competition has its own rules, but the process commonly follows these stages.

### 1. A wine is entered

A producer or exhibitor submits a wine according to the competition’s entry requirements. Those requirements can cover matters such as the wine’s vintage, variety, region, labelling or eligibility.

A wine generally needs to be entered to be judged. That means an unmedalled wine may simply not have entered that particular competition; the absence of a medal is not proof of poor quality.

### 2. The wine is placed in a class or category

The entry is assigned to a judging group, often based on factors such as grape variety, style, region or vintage.

This context matters. A wine may be assessed alongside wines with similar characteristics, rather than against every wine in the marketplace. A medal therefore describes how the entry performed in its class under that show’s system.

### 3. Judges assess the wine

Judges taste and assess the wine using the competition’s stated method. Depending on the show, this might involve individual scores, panel discussion, consensus, reassessment or more than one judging stage.

Some competitions use blind tasting, but you should not assume that all competitions use exactly the same format, number of judges or scoring scale.

The Australian Wine Research Institute describes wine-show judging in terms of qualities such as style definition, complexity, intensity, balance, persistence and freedom from faults. These are useful examples of qualities judges may consider, not a universal definition of every competition’s medal standard.

### 4. A medal is awarded—or withheld

A competition may award medals when entries reach published points thresholds, satisfy a panel or meet another stated standard. Some shows can award medals to every wine that reaches the threshold. Others limit the number or proportion of awards, or withhold awards where entries do not meet the required standard.

For example, the 2026 Australian Small Winemakers Show lists Gold at 95 points or above, Silver at 90–94 and Bronze at 85–89. Those are that show’s published thresholds—not a universal Australian or international rule.

### 5. Eligible wines may compete for trophies

A trophy is usually selected from a smaller group of qualifying wines, such as class winners or medal recipients. The exact process varies.

The Australian Small Winemakers Show requires qualifying Gold results for its trophy categories. The 2026 Victorian Wine Show also states that only Gold Medal wines are eligible for trophy consideration. These examples show how trophy rules can differ between competitions.

A trophy is therefore not simply “a stronger gold”. It is a separate award decided under its own rules.

## Gold, silver and bronze are not universal grades

It is tempting to read medal colours as if they were fixed grades: gold equals excellent, silver equals good and bronze equals average. In practice, that shortcut can mislead.

Different competitions may use:

- different points thresholds;
- panel discussion or consensus;
- separate judging stages;
- limits on the proportion of wines receiving medals; or
- additional awards such as double gold, platinum or grand gold.

The International Organisation of Vine and Wine’s standards for international wine competitions include several medal levels and limits on the total proportion of medals awarded. The Decanter World Wine Awards provides another example of a multi-stage system, with regional panels and later reassessment for higher awards.

These systems should be understood as attributed examples, not as a universal template. A Gold from one competition may not represent the same process as a Gold from another.

## Medal, trophy, score and review: what is the difference?

These terms are related, but they are not interchangeable.

- **Medal:** an award given to an entered wine within a competition.
- **Trophy:** a higher-level or champion award selected under a competition’s rules, often from qualifying wines.
- **Points score:** a numerical assessment, sometimes used to determine a medal.
- **Review:** a published evaluator’s written or numerical opinion, usually presented with the reviewer’s or publication’s viewpoint.

A competition might use a points score to decide whether a wine receives a medal, but the score and medal still communicate different things. A review may discuss style, quality or value in a way that a medal does not.

Always check the source and judging context instead of treating a medal, score, trophy and review as four versions of the same claim.

## What should Australian shoppers check?

Wine Australia says medals and awards may be used in the description and presentation of wine in Australia, provided care is taken to avoid misleading presentation. Its summary of the Display of Awards Code of Practice advises that, where a wine has received an award, the competition or show name and award year should be displayed.

That makes the details around a medal worth reading. On a bottle, shelf card or online listing, look for:

1. **Competition or show name** — who awarded the medal?
2. **Award year** — when was it awarded?
3. **Medal or trophy level** — what was actually won?
4. **Category or class** — what kind of wine was judged?
5. **Vintage** — does the claim identify a particular vintage?
6. **Exact wine** — does the award apply to the bottle being sold?
7. **Competition rules** — what did that show’s medal level mean?
8. **Your own preferences** — does the wine’s variety, region, style and price suit you?

The award year and vintage are not necessarily the same fact. A wine might carry a medal from one competition year while naming a different vintage on the label. Check that the award claim relates to the product in front of you.

Wine Australia’s Label Integrity Program concerns the truthfulness of claims such as vintage, variety and geographical indication. It should not be read as a guarantee of a wine’s sensory quality or as verification of every implication a medal might create.

## What a medal cannot tell you

A medal is a snapshot of a judged entry in a defined setting. It cannot establish, by itself, that:

- everyone will enjoy the wine;
- the wine is objectively the best;
- it offers better value than an unmedalled wine;
- it will improve with age;
- every vintage of the same label has won the award;
- the current bottle is identical to the sample judged; or
- the wine was compared with every other wine available.

Wine can also change with storage, transport and bottle age. The most useful interpretation is modest: judges liked what they assessed, under that competition’s rules.

## FAQs

### Is a gold medal always better than a silver medal?

Usually, a gold represents a higher award within that particular competition. But the thresholds and judging structure vary, so colours should not be compared casually between different shows.

### Does a medal mean the wine is worth buying?

It may be a helpful signal, but it is not a buying guarantee. Consider it alongside grape variety, region, vintage, producer, price and your own taste.

### Is a trophy better than a medal?

A trophy often recognises a higher-level winner selected from qualifying entries, but the structure differs between competitions. Read the show’s rules.

### Why does an unmedalled wine sometimes taste better to me?

Judges and consumers can value different styles. Personal preference is not a mistake, and a wine must generally be entered before it can win a medal.

### Can a medal apply to every vintage?

Not automatically. Check the award year, stated vintage and exact product. A medal awarded to one entry should not be assumed to cover every later release.

## The simplest way to use a medal

A medal can answer one narrow question: **how did this entered wine perform in this competition, category and judging system?**

It cannot answer the larger question: **will I enjoy this bottle?**

Use the award as a starting point, then read the rest of the label and choose with your own palate in mind.
