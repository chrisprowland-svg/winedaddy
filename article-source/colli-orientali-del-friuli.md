# Colli Orientali del Friuli: A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Colli Orientali del Friuli is a defined wine area in north-eastern Italy, not another name for all of Friuli-Venezia Giulia.
- Its formal Italian designation is **“Friuli” Colli Orientali DOC**.
- The area lies in the hills of the Province of Udine, around Cividale del Friuli and east of Udine.
- Friulano, Ribolla Gialla, Schioppettino, Picolit and Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso are among its important regional associations.
- The area makes white, red and sweet wines. Its name does not guarantee one flavour profile.
- Collio, Friuli DOC, Ramandolo DOCG and Rosazzo DOCG are related or nearby designations, but they are not interchangeable with Colli Orientali del Friuli.
- For Australian drinkers, the simplest comparison is with a regulated place-of-origin designation such as an Australian GI—but the legal systems are not identical.

## Quick answer: what is Colli Orientali del Friuli?

Colli Orientali del Friuli is a hilly wine-growing area and DOC in **Friuli-Venezia Giulia**, in north-eastern Italy. Its formal protected-designation wording is **“Friuli” Colli Orientali**, while *Colli Orientali del Friuli* is the familiar name used by the region, wine trade and consumers.

The area sits in the Province of Udine, with Cividale del Friuli as the most useful local orientation point. It extends through parts of municipalities from the northern hills around Tarcento and Nimis to the southern area around Corno di Rosazzo and San Giovanni al Natisone. Udine is the nearest major regional centre, west of the appellation.

From Trieste, Cividale is roughly an hour to an hour and a quarter by road north-west, depending on the route and traffic. Treat that as a broad orientation, not a precise travel promise.

The wines associated with the area include aromatic and structured whites, distinctive local reds and special sweet-wine designations. The grape, subzone, production category and winemaking choices all matter, so the name alone cannot tell you exactly how a bottle will taste.

## Where is it, exactly?

The hierarchy is:

**Italy → Friuli-Venezia Giulia → Province of Udine → “Friuli” Colli Orientali DOC**

Cividale del Friuli is a practical geographic and cultural centre, but it does not define the whole DOC. Other useful place names include Prepotto, Faedis, Rosazzo, Corno di Rosazzo, Manzano, Tarcento and Nimis.

The legal production area includes all or part of 19 municipalities. That wording matters: a municipality appearing in the official list is not necessarily wholly inside the wine area.

The vineyards occupy hills rather than the flat Friulian plain. The area is commonly described as having vineyards at roughly 100 to 350 metres above sea level, with geology influenced by ancient marine sedimentation and marl- and sandstone-derived formations. These details help explain why the area supports varied wine styles, but they do not dictate the flavour of every bottle. For a broader explanation, see our guides to [terroir](/what-is-terroir/) and [how climate affects wine](/how-climate-affects-wine/).

## What does DOC mean on the label?

DOC stands for *Denominazione di origine controllata*, or controlled designation of origin. In practical terms, it is a regulated Italian designation covering a defined place and production rules, including eligible grapes and wine categories. It sits within the wider European protected-origin framework.

A bottle using the formal designation may show **“Friuli” Colli Orientali**. You may also encounter a grape name such as Friulano, Ribolla Gialla or Schioppettino, depending on the applicable category and labelling rules.

For an Australian drinker, an Italian DOC is broadly comparable to the role an Australian GI can play in identifying geographic origin. Both connect a wine with a defined place. They are not legal equivalents, however: Italian DOC rules combine geographic origin with denomination-specific production requirements, while Australian GIs primarily define geographic origin within Australia’s labelling system. Our guide to [Australian Geographic Indications](/australian-geographic-indications/) explains the Australian side.

Most importantly, a DOC is not a flavour guarantee or a quality score. It does not automatically tell you whether the wine is sweet or dry, oaked or unoaked, light-bodied or full-bodied.

## Which grapes and wine styles matter?

### White wines

Colli Orientali del Friuli is strongly associated with white grapes including:

- **Friulano**
- **Ribolla Gialla**
- **Malvasia Istriana**
- **Pinot Grigio**
- **Chardonnay**
- **Sauvignon**, where permitted in the relevant category

Friulano and Ribolla Gialla are especially useful names to recognise. The region’s whites may be fresh, aromatic or more structured, but those are broad tendencies rather than rules. A grape variety, vineyard site, harvest timing and winemaking approach can all change the result.

### Red wines

Local red varieties give the area a particularly distinctive identity. Look for associations with:

- **Schioppettino**, especially around Prepotto
- **Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso**
- **Pignolo**
- **Tazzelenghe**

Merlot and Cabernet varieties are also grown and used where permitted. Schioppettino di Prepotto is a more specific subzone reference within the area. It is now associated with a dry still red wine, despite historical explanations of the name involving slight effervescence in bottle.

### Sweet and special-designation wines

**Picolit** is one of the area’s best-known historical associations and has its own related designation, **Colli Orientali del Friuli Picolit DOCG**.

The nearby **Ramandolo DOCG** is associated with Verduzzo Friulano. **Rosazzo DOCG** is another related designation in the broader Colli Orientali landscape. These DOCGs should not be treated as alternative names for the principal “Friuli” Colli Orientali DOC.

## How is it different from nearby regions?

The easiest confusion is between **Colli Orientali del Friuli** and **Friuli-Venezia Giulia**.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia is the larger Italian region. Colli Orientali del Friuli is one more specific wine area within it. A broader regional designation can cover wine from a much wider territory and should not automatically be described as Colli Orientali del Friuli. You can read more about the parent region in our guide to [Friuli-Venezia Giulia](/friuli-venezia-giulia/).

**Collio**, also called **Collio Goriziano**, is a separate DOC, mainly associated with the Province of Gorizia. It is nearby and shares some grapes and hillside characteristics, but it is not the same geographical indication.

Other separate or broader designations in the surrounding area include Friuli DOC, Friuli Grave DOC, Friuli Isonzo DOC, Ramandolo DOCG and Rosazzo DOCG. Similar geography does not make their labels interchangeable.

## What should you expect in the glass?

A useful starting expectation is a region with:

- aromatic and structured white wines;
- distinctive wines from local red varieties;
- sweet-wine styles connected with Picolit and nearby DOCG designations.

That is a map, not a tasting guarantee. Do not assume every bottle will be mineral, high-acid, spicy, sweet, oak-aged or built for long ageing. If you are choosing a bottle, the grape name and the specific wine category will usually tell you more about likely style than the regional name alone.

## FAQs

### Is Colli Orientali del Friuli a DOC or DOCG?

The principal designation is **“Friuli” Colli Orientali DOC**. Related DOCG designations, including Ramandolo, Rosazzo and Colli Orientali del Friuli Picolit, are separate categories.

### Is Colli Orientali del Friuli the same as Friuli-Venezia Giulia?

No. Friuli-Venezia Giulia is the larger region; Colli Orientali del Friuli is a specific hilly DOC area within it.

### Is Collio the same as Colli Orientali?

No. Collio, or Collio Goriziano, is a separate DOC in the nearby Province of Gorizia.

### Which grapes is the area known for?

Important regional associations include Friulano, Ribolla Gialla, Schioppettino, Picolit, Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso, Verduzzo Friulano, Pignolo and Tazzelenghe. The list is not a ranking, and not every wine uses one of these grapes.
