# How to choose wine for ageing

## Highlights

- Ageing potential is about balance, structure and flavour—not simply price, colour or prestige.
- Acidity and tannin can help a wine develop, while sweetness, alcohol, concentration and fruit intensity also matter.
- No grape variety, region, closure or bottle price guarantees that a wine will improve.
- “Can age” and “will improve with age” are different questions.
- Many wines are made to drink young, and ageing cannot repair poor wine or bad storage.
- In Australia’s varied wine landscape, judge the specific wine, producer, vintage and provenance.
- If possible, buy two or more bottles and taste one before committing the rest to the cellar.

## The quick answer

To choose a wine for ageing, look for a wine with enough **structure, flavour intensity and balance** to remain interesting as it develops. Acidity and tannin are important supports, while sweetness, alcohol, concentration, fruit integrity and winemaking style also play a part.

Then look beyond the liquid itself. Consider the producer, vintage, intended style, closure, bottle format and provenance—the wine’s storage and ownership history. These factors increase or reduce your confidence, but none guarantees a particular result.

The most useful distinction is this:

- **A wine that can age** may remain sound or develop in bottle.
- **A wine that will improve with age** is likely to become more enjoyable for you as it changes.

A wine may survive several years without becoming better. Another may be delicious now but lose freshness if held too long. Ageing is not a reward that every bottle earns automatically.

## What makes a wine suitable for ageing?

### Acidity brings freshness

Acidity gives wine lift and freshness. It can help a wine remain lively as it develops and is especially important in sweet wines, where it balances the sugar.

High acidity alone is not enough. The wine still needs fruit, flavour and overall balance. Think of acidity as part of the frame, not the entire picture.

### Tannin provides structure

Tannin is the drying, grippy sensation found particularly in red wine. During bottle development, tannin can become softer and more integrated.

That does not mean the most aggressively astringent wine is the best choice for ageing. Harsh tannin without enough fruit or balance can remain harsh. Look for structure that feels supported rather than punishing.

### Sweetness, alcohol and concentration matter together

Sweetness can help preserve some wines when it is balanced by acidity and flavour intensity. This is why certain sweet wines can develop over time. Fortified wines may also have ageing potential, but alcohol alone is not a guarantee.

Concentration matters because youthful fruit flavours can change with age. A wine needs enough substance to remain balanced as those fresh flavours evolve. Concentration does not necessarily mean heaviness or high alcohol.

The guiding question is not “Which component is highest?” It is “Do the components work together?”

## Different wines age in different ways

Ageing is not only for big red wines.

- **Structured Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon** may develop when acidity, tannin, fruit concentration and balance support the style.
- **Riesling** can be a white-wine example where acidity and fruit integrity support bottle development.
- **Traditional-method sparkling wine** can gain more developed, savoury or toasty characters as it matures.
- **Sweet and fortified wines** may have useful preservation factors, but their sugar, acidity, alcohol, concentration and style still need to be considered together.

These are broad tendencies, not guarantees. Australian wine is made across diverse climates and styles, so a grape name or regional name cannot tell you everything about one particular bottle.

## Check the story behind the bottle

Before buying a bottle to cellar, ask:

1. **Who made it?** Is the producer known for this particular style, rather than simply being prestigious?
2. **What is the intended style?** Was the wine made for early drinking or for development?
3. **What is the vintage?** Vintage conditions can influence the balance and structure of the finished wine.
4. **Is the provenance credible?** Has the bottle been stored carefully since release?
5. **Is the bottle in good condition?** Look for a sound label, capsule, cork and fill level where these can be assessed.
6. **What does the technical information suggest?** Producer notes may explain the wine’s structure and intended drinking approach.

Australian wine labels can help identify details such as vintage, variety and origin. Those details establish what the wine is, but they do not promise that it will improve with age.

A screwcap does not automatically prevent ageing, and cork is not automatically superior. Closure type affects the bottle’s oxygen environment, but it is one part of a much larger system. Bottle size can also influence development, although it should remain a secondary consideration rather than a reason to buy a wine you otherwise do not want.

## Store potential carefully

Storage can protect a wine’s potential; it cannot create potential that was never there.

For an Australian household, choose the coolest, darkest and most stable place available. Avoid kitchens, garages, cars, roof spaces, sunny windows and areas that become hot during the day. Heat, light and repeated disturbance can accelerate unwanted changes.

A controlled cellar is not the same as a warm cupboard. If you do not have a suitable place to store wine, buying fewer bottles and drinking them sooner may be the better decision.

Bottle position depends partly on the closure and storage conditions. Do not treat upright or sideways storage as a universal quality rule. The more important principles are stable conditions, protection from heat and light, and a credible storage history.

## A simple cellaring plan

If you are trying a wine for ageing:

- Buy two or more bottles if possible.
- Record the vintage, purchase date and storage location.
- Open one bottle relatively early to establish a reference point.
- Note the fruit, acidity, tannin, sweetness, alcohol, aroma and overall balance.
- Taste another bottle periodically rather than waiting blindly for a predicted “peak”.
- Stop ageing when the wine is most enjoyable to you.

There is no prize for keeping a bottle unopened beyond its best moment. Older does not automatically mean better.

## Common mistakes to avoid

Do not assume that:

- an expensive wine will age better;
- every Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon or Riesling is suitable for cellaring;
- high alcohol or heavy oak guarantees longevity;
- natural wine is automatically ageworthy;
- a particular region guarantees development;
- every wine benefits from extended ageing;
- ageing can repair faults or poor storage.

The best cellar decision is often a modest one: choose a balanced wine with a credible style, store it well, and give yourself the chance to taste its progress.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every wine improve with age?

No. Many wines are made to be enjoyed young, and some may lose freshness or fruit before they become more enjoyable. A wine’s ability to remain sound is not the same as a guarantee that it will improve.

### Is red wine better for ageing than white wine?

Not automatically. Red, white, sweet, fortified and sparkling wines can all develop, but they do so in different ways. Acidity, fruit integrity, sweetness, tannin, style and balance matter more than colour alone.

### Is a screwcap bad for ageing?

No. A screwcap does not automatically prevent bottle development, just as a cork does not guarantee superior ageing. Closure is one factor among several that influence the bottle’s oxygen environment.

### How long should I age a wine?

There is no universal number that works for every bottle. The producer, vintage, style, storage history and the wine’s balance all matter. Tasting periodically is more reliable than following a fixed timeline.

### Can storage improve a wine?

Good storage can help preserve a wine’s potential. It cannot turn a wine that lacks balance into an ageworthy one, and it cannot reverse damage caused by excessive heat, light or poor handling.
