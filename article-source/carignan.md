# What Is Carignan? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Carignan is a red wine grape variety, not a wine style or region.
- It is a black-skinned variety of *Vitis vinifera*.
- It probably originated in Aragón in north-east Spain.
- In different countries, you may see names including Carignane, Carignano, Cariñena, Mazuela and Mazuelo.
- Carignan is generally late-ripening, vigorous, fertile and capable of high yields.
- Its wines can have lively acidity, strong colour and firm tannin.
- Australian Carignan exists, particularly among alternative-variety plantings, but it does not have one fixed national style.

## Quick answer: what is Carignan?

Carignan is a red wine grape variety with a long Mediterranean history. It is generally considered to have originated in Aragón, north-east Spain, before becoming strongly associated with southern France, particularly Languedoc-Roussillon.

In the vineyard, Carignan is usually late-ripening, vigorous and highly fertile. That means it can produce large crops, and crop level matters: wines from heavily cropped vines may be less concentrated, while mature vines and limited production can produce more generous and structured results when the site and season are suitable.

In the glass, Carignan commonly contributes acidity, colour and tannin. Depending on ripeness, vine age, yields, region and winemaking, it may taste fresh and red-fruited, darker and savoury, herbal, rustic or firmly structured. It is made as a single-variety wine and used in blends, where it is often associated with Grenache, Shiraz and Mourvèdre.

Carignan is also grown in Australia. Australian Wine Research Institute evidence recorded producers using or claiming to use it in the Barossa Valley, McLaren Vale, Victoria and Western Australia. It remains a minor or alternative variety in the Australian context, and current national planting figures are not established in the supplied evidence.

## Where does Carignan come from?

Carignan’s strongest historical identity is Mediterranean. Its accepted origin is probably Aragón, near the Spanish town and wine region of Cariñena. The grape is also known in Spain as Mazuela or Mazuelo.

Over time, Carignan became especially important in southern France. It was widely planted in Languedoc-Roussillon, where its ability to produce substantial crops suited the region’s historic wine industry. French plantings have since declined considerably, as growers and wineries shifted towards other varieties and different quality and market priorities.

This history helps explain why Carignan can appear in discussions about Mediterranean red wines. However, its origin does not determine how every bottle tastes. A Carignan from one region may differ noticeably from another because of climate, soil, crop level, vine age, vintage and winemaking.

## Other names for Carignan

The label name depends partly on where the wine comes from:

- **Carignan** is the standard French and widely used international name.
- **Carignane** is used in the United States and appears in some technical and historical sources.
- **Carignano** is associated with Italy, particularly Sardinia.
- **Cariñena** is a Spanish name linked with Aragón.
- **Mazuela** and **Mazuelo** are Spanish names for the variety.

Historical grape names are not always interchangeable. For example, “Mataro” is commonly used in Australia and internationally for Mourvèdre, even though some historical synonym lists associate the name with Carignan. It is therefore safer not to assume that a bottle labelled Mataro contains Carignan.

In Australia, **Carignan** is the clearest name to look for. If another historical synonym appears, consider its regional context rather than treating it as automatically identical.

## What is Carignan like to grow?

Carignan is a late-ripening grape. Its fruit needs a relatively long growing season to mature, which helps explain its long association with warm Mediterranean and dry-climate regions.

It is also vigorous and fertile, producing substantial crops when conditions allow. That productivity can be useful, but it creates an important quality question: how much fruit is the vine carrying, and how ripe is it when harvested?

High yields can produce wines with less concentration and more pronounced tannin or acidity. By contrast, suitable sites, mature vines and limited production may help produce wines with greater concentration and generosity. These are possibilities, not guarantees. “Old vine” is not a synonym for Carignan, and not every old-vine wine is automatically better.

Carignan’s main vineyard weakness is its strong susceptibility to powdery mildew, also called oidium. Vineyard management, site conditions and the growing season all influence its performance.

## What does Carignan taste like?

There is no single Carignan flavour profile. A useful starting expectation is a dry red wine with noticeable acidity, colour and tannin.

Depending on the wine, you might find:

- red-fruit character and freshness;
- darker fruit;
- savoury or herbal notes;
- firm, drying or grippy tannin;
- a more rustic impression in high-yielding or less favourable examples.

Acidity gives the wine lift and liveliness, while tannin can create firmness or a drying sensation on the gums. If you are unfamiliar with tannin, our guide to [what tannins are](/what-are-tannins/) explains the feeling in more detail. You can also explore [acidity in wine](/what-is-acidity-in-wine/) and [wine body](/what-is-wine-body/) to understand why two Carignan wines may feel so different.

Fruit flavour does not mean a wine is sweet. A Carignan can smell or taste of ripe red or dark fruit while still being completely dry. This is the same distinction shown in the reusable **VIS-002 — Dry does not mean flavourless** visual: fruitiness describes flavour, while dryness describes the amount of residual sugar.

## Is Carignan used in blends?

Yes. Carignan is made as a varietal wine, but it is also used in blends. Its acidity, colour and structure can contribute useful shape to a wine alongside varieties such as Grenache, Shiraz and Mourvèdre.

Blending can soften or balance Carignan’s more forceful characteristics. Winemaking can do the same. Carbonic maceration, a fermentation approach used in some French Carignan wines, may help soften tannin and bring out more fruit expression.

A blended wine will not necessarily taste like a single-variety Carignan. When reading a label or wine description, check whether Carignan is the only named grape or one part of a blend. The region, vintage and winemaking choices matter just as much as the variety name.

## Is Carignan grown in Australia?

Yes. Carignan is grown and made in Australia, although it is a minor variety and current national planting figures are not established by the evidence available for this article.

An Australian Wine Research Institute profile published in 2020 recorded at least 22 producers claiming to use Carignan. Twelve were in the Barossa Valley, with other examples recorded in McLaren Vale, Victoria and Western Australia. These figures describe documented producer use at that time; they are not a current national vineyard census or a ranking of Australian regions.

There is also an important identification caution. Some older South Australian vines labelled Carignan before 1966 may actually be other varieties, including Paraletta, Bonvedro or Mourvèdre. That means claims about historic Australian Carignan plantings should be made carefully unless the vine identity has been confirmed.

Australian Carignan therefore does not have one national style. A bottle may be fresh and bright, structured and savoury, or richer and more concentrated depending on where it was grown and how it was made.

## How should you recognise a Carignan wine?

Start with the grape name, but do not stop there. When choosing or discussing a bottle, consider:

1. **Is it varietal or blended?** A single-variety wine will show the grape more directly.
2. **Where was it grown?** Region and site influence ripeness, acidity and texture.
3. **What is the vintage?** Weather changes the balance of fruit, acidity and tannin.
4. **How was it made?** Winemaking can alter the fruit expression and soften or emphasise tannin.
5. **What style is described?** Words such as fresh, savoury, structured or fruit-driven provide useful clues, but they are not guarantees.

Carignan is worth approaching with an open mind. It can be a firm, energetic red rather than a simple “big” wine, and its combination of acidity, colour and tannin gives it a distinctive structural personality.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Carignan a red wine?

Carignan is a red wine grape variety, not a finished wine style. It produces red wines, either on its own or as part of a blend.

### What does Carignan taste like?

Carignan often shows lively acidity, noticeable colour and firm tannin. Its fruit may be red or dark, with savoury or herbal notes in some examples. The final style depends on region, crop level, vine age, vintage, blending and winemaking.

### Is Carignan grown in Australia?

Yes. It is grown in Australia, with documented producer use in the Barossa Valley, McLaren Vale, Victoria and Western Australia. It remains a minor or alternative variety, and the available evidence does not establish a current national planting total.

### Is Carignan the same as Mataro?

Not reliably. Although some historical synonym lists connect the names, Mataro is commonly used for Mourvèdre in Australia and internationally. Treat the names as region-dependent rather than assuming they identify the same grape.
