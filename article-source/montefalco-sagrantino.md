# Montefalco Sagrantino: A Beginner’s Guide to the Umbrian Appellation

Montefalco Sagrantino is a protected Italian wine appellation in Umbria, central Italy. Its red wines are made exclusively from the Sagrantino grape and come in two regulated categories: **Secco**, the dry style, and **Passito**, the sweet style made from dried grapes.

The most important distinction is simple:

- **Montefalco Sagrantino** is the protected place and appellation.
- **Sagrantino** is the grape variety.
- **Umbria** is the broader Italian region.
- **Montefalco** is the central town and municipality named in the appellation.

## Highlights

- It is officially known as **Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG** and is also recognised as a European PDO/DOP.
- It lies in Umbria, in the Province of Perugia.
- The legal production area includes all of Montefalco and defined parts of four neighbouring municipalities.
- Both Secco and Passito must be made from 100% Sagrantino.
- Secco is dry, deeply coloured and often notably tannic.
- Passito is sweet and made from dried Sagrantino grapes.
- The appellation has substantial minimum ageing requirements, but that does not mean every bottle will taste mature when opened.

## Where is Montefalco Sagrantino?

Montefalco Sagrantino is in central Italy, in the Umbrian province of Perugia. Montefalco lies south-east of the city of Perugia and roughly north-east of Rome. From Rome, the drive is commonly around two hours, depending on the route, traffic and starting point. That is useful orientation rather than a precise travel promise.

The legal production area is larger than the town of Montefalco but much smaller than Umbria as a whole. It includes:

- the entire municipality of Montefalco; and
- defined parts of Bevagna, Gualdo Cattaneo, Castel Ritaldi and Giano dell’Umbria.

Foligno is a useful larger regional centre for orientation. Spoleto is another nearby Umbrian reference point, but neither city defines the appellation.

Italy does not use exactly the same state–zone–region structure familiar from Australian wine geography. For an Australian learner, the helpful hierarchy is:

**Italy → Umbria → Province of Perugia → Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG**

A map such as **VIS-024**, if it shows Umbria and Montefalco only as broad orientation points, can help place the appellation without implying a precise legal boundary.

## What does DOCG mean?

DOCG stands for *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*, or Controlled and Guaranteed Designation of Origin. At European Union level, Montefalco Sagrantino is recognised as a PDO, or DOP in Italian administrative language.

These terms indicate protected geographic and production rules. They do not mean DOCG is a straightforward ranking in which every DOCG wine must be better than every wine with another designation. For a plain-English explanation of the system, see [Italian DOC and DOCG](/italian-doc-and-docg/).

In an Australian bottle shop, look for wording such as **Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG**, **Montefalco Sagrantino**, **Montefalco Sagrantino Secco** or **Montefalco Sagrantino Passito**. A bottle labelled only **Sagrantino** does not automatically qualify as Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG.

## Secco and Passito: the two styles

### Montefalco Sagrantino Secco

Secco means dry. It is a red wine with deep ruby colour that can develop garnet tones with age. The appellation’s specification describes characteristic blackberry-like aromas, dryness and harmony.

In the glass, Secco is often substantial, dark-fruited and structured, with firm **tannin**. Tannin is the drying or grippy sensation that can make your gums and cheeks feel slightly rough; you can learn more in [What are tannins?](/what-are-tannins/).

The wines can also show balancing acidity and the influence of oak, extraction, vintage and bottle age. “Full-bodied” is a useful broad description, but it is a tendency rather than a guarantee for every bottle.

### Montefalco Sagrantino Passito

Passito is the sweet category. The grapes are dried before they are made into wine, concentrating their sugars and changing the shape of the finished wine. Passito is not the same thing as a fortified wine: the sweetness comes from the winemaking process and residual sugar, not from adding spirits.

Passito is also made exclusively from Sagrantino. It can combine sweetness with dark fruit, acidity and a substantial texture. The Consortium suggests serving it with aged or salty cheese, biscuits, pastries and dark chocolate.

## Why are these wines often tannic?

Sagrantino is a thick-skinned grape with a strong tendency towards firm tannin. In Montefalco Sagrantino, producers manage that structure through choices such as extraction, fermentation and oak maturation. Vintage conditions also affect the relationship between fruit ripeness, alcohol, acidity and tannin maturity.

That means two bottles from the same appellation can feel quite different. A young Secco may taste tightly wound or drying, while a bottle with more time in bottle may seem more integrated. There is no universal rule that every bottle must be cellared for a particular number of years.

The current specification requires at least 33 months of compulsory ageing for both categories. For Secco, at least 12 months must be in oak casks, and both styles require at least four months of bottle ageing before release. These rules help explain why Montefalco Sagrantino is not generally built around immediate release, but they do not guarantee that every released bottle will suit every drinker’s idea of maturity. See [Does wine improve with age?](/does-wine-improve-with-age/) for the broader principle.

## Montefalco Sagrantino versus Sagrantino

The existing [Sagrantino guide](/sagrantino/) concerns the grape as a broader wine entity. This page concerns the place and the protected wines made under its rules.

Not all wine made from Sagrantino is necessarily Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG. The DOCG designation requires the defined origin, permitted production conditions and 100% Sagrantino composition.

Montefalco Sagrantino is also different from **Montepulciano d’Abruzzo**. They come from separate Italian geographic designations and involve different place and grape relationships. The similar-looking names are a common label-reading trap.

## Serving and food pairing

The Consortium’s approximate serving guidance is:

- **Secco:** around 18°C
- **Passito:** around 12°C

For an Australian table, serve Secco cool rather than warm, especially in a heated room. Passito is generally served cooler and in smaller pours. Temperature is a starting point, not a rule carved in stone.

Secco’s structure can work well with lamb shoulder, roast or braised red meat, game and aged hard cheese. Passito’s sweetness suits aged or salty cheese, dark chocolate and some desserts. A young Secco may benefit from careful decanting to open its aromas, but decanting is optional and depends on the particular wine. See [When should wine be decanted?](/when-to-decant-wine/).

As always, drink responsibly and follow Australian alcohol guidelines.

## Common questions

### Is Montefalco Sagrantino a grape or a region?

It is the name of a protected appellation and its wines. Sagrantino is the grape. The appellation is in Umbria, centred on Montefalco and extending into defined parts of four neighbouring municipalities.

### Is Montefalco Sagrantino always dry?

No. **Secco** is dry, while **Passito** is sweet and made from dried grapes. The label should identify Passito when that category is presented.

### Is Passito fortified?

Not on the evidence used for this guide. Passito is made from dried grapes and is sweet; it should not automatically be described as fortified.

### Does DOCG mean the wine is better?

DOCG means the wine follows protected origin and production rules. It is not a simple guarantee of personal preference or universal superiority.
