# Fiano di Avellino: an Australian beginner’s guide

Fiano di Avellino is a protected Italian white-wine appellation from the Province of Avellino in Campania. It is not a separate grape variety. Instead, it is a regulated place name for wine made principally from the Fiano grape within a legally defined area.

That distinction is the key to understanding the name on a bottle: **Fiano is the grape; Fiano di Avellino is the protected geographic designation.**

## Highlights

- Fiano di Avellino is a protected Italian appellation, not another grape variety.
- It comes from the inland Province of Avellino in Campania, southern Italy.
- The legal production area includes all or part of 26 named municipalities.
- Fiano is the principal permitted grape, with limited use of other approved white varieties.
- The ordinary wine is officially described as dry, fresh and harmonious.
- **Riserva** is a regulated category with a minimum ageing requirement.
- The name tells you about protected place and production rules; producer, vintage and winemaking still influence the finished wine.
- Fiano made elsewhere cannot automatically use the name Fiano di Avellino.

## Quick answer: what is Fiano di Avellino?

Fiano di Avellino is a protected designation of origin for white wine made in a defined area of Campania, around Avellino. In Italian classification language it is **Fiano di Avellino DOCG**. In European Union geographical-indication language, it is a **DOP/PDO**: a Protected Designation of Origin.

The wine is principally made from Fiano. The Campania regional authority states that Fiano must account for at least 85 per cent, with limited proportions of Greco, Coda di Volpe Bianca and Trebbiano Toscano permitted under the rules.

The official specification describes the ordinary wine as dry, fresh and harmonious, with a pale straw-yellow colour. Those are useful style markers, but they are not a promise that every bottle will taste identical. Site, vintage, producer decisions and winemaking can all affect the result.

For the broader grape variety, see our guide to [Fiano](/fiano/).

## Where is Fiano di Avellino?

The geographic hierarchy is:

**Italy → Campania → Province of Avellino → Fiano di Avellino production area**

Avellino is both the provincial capital and the most useful local reference point. The area lies inland in Campania, east to north-east of Naples, among hilly country associated with Irpinia.

From Rome, Avellino is roughly 243 kilometres away, or about two and a half hours by car under the route conditions assessed by the source. Treat that only as orientation: traffic, roadworks, route choice and your starting point can change the journey considerably.

Familiar names within the legally defined area include Avellino, Lapio, Atripalda, Montefalcione, Montefredane, Mercogliano, Candida and Sorbo Serpico. These towns can help you picture the landscape, but no single town defines the entire appellation.

The legal production area covers the administrative territories of 26 municipalities:

Avellino, Lapio, Atripalda, Cesinali, Aiello del Sabato, Santo Stefano del Sole, Sorbo Serpico, Salza Irpina, Parolise, San Potito Ultra, Candida, Manocalzati, Pratola Serra, Montefredane, Grottolella, Capriglia Irpina, Sant’Angelo a Scala, Summonte, Mercogliano, Forino, Contrada, Monteforte Irpino, Ospedaletto d’Alpinolo, Montefalcione, Santa Lucia di Serino and San Michele di Serino.

## Fiano versus Fiano di Avellino

Think of the difference as similar to the difference between a grape name and a place name.

**Fiano** is a white grape variety. It can be grown in different places, including outside Italy.

**Fiano di Avellino** is a protected appellation. To use that name, the wine must come from the defined area and comply with the appellation’s production requirements.

The name does not mean that there is a second grape called “Fiano di Avellino”. Nor does every wine made from Fiano qualify for the designation. A Fiano produced elsewhere may be labelled with the grape name, another regional designation or a producer-specific description, but not automatically with the protected name Fiano di Avellino.

## What does DOCG mean?

DOCG stands for *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*, usually translated as Controlled and Guaranteed Designation of Origin. In plain English, it is an Italian regulated designation with rules covering geographic origin, permitted varieties and production.

At EU level, the same protected-origin framework may be described as DOP or PDO. These terms can appear differently in official and commercial contexts, but they refer to the protected geographic identity of the product.

For an Australian reader, an Australian Geographic Indication is a useful comparison because both systems connect wine names with defined places. They are not identical legal systems, however. DOCG and DOP/PDO belong to the Italian and European geographical-indication framework, while Australian GIs operate under Australian law.

DOCG is not a simple ranking that guarantees every DOCG wine will be better than every other wine. It tells you that the wine belongs to a regulated origin and production category. Our guide to [Italian DOC and DOCG](/italian-doc-and-docg/) explains the terminology in more detail.

## What grapes and styles are permitted?

Fiano is the principal grape. Under the regional summary, it must make up at least 85 per cent of the wine. Greco, Coda di Volpe Bianca and Trebbiano Toscano may be used in limited proportions under the specification.

The ordinary Fiano di Avellino style is officially described as:

- pale straw-yellow in colour;
- intense, pleasant and characteristic in aroma;
- dry, fresh and harmonious on the palate.

The specification also sets minimum levels for alcohol, acidity and non-reducing extract. These are production requirements, not tasting scores.

Fiano di Avellino also has a regulated **Riserva** category. Riserva requires at least 12 months of ageing, calculated from 1 November of the harvest year. It also has higher minimum alcohol and extract requirements than the ordinary category.

Riserva does not automatically mean every bottle will be richer, oakier or more ageworthy. Those characteristics depend on the producer, vintage, site, storage and winemaking choices.

## Why does the place matter?

The appellation covers a varied inland, hilly area rather than one uniform vineyard site. The official description refers to ventilated, well-lit slopes and a mixture of geological conditions, including clay, limestone, volcanic elements and harder rock in different locations.

That variation matters because a geographic designation does not make every vineyard identical. It provides a shared place and set of rules, while allowing differences between sites and producers.

It is safer to treat soil and landscape as contributors to variation than as a recipe for one guaranteed flavour. The appellation does not mean that every wine will taste smoky, mineral, nutty or floral.

## How to read Fiano di Avellino on a label

When you see **Fiano di Avellino DOCG** in an Australian bottle shop, restaurant or wine list, you can take several things from the name:

1. The wine is associated with the protected area in the Province of Avellino, Campania.
2. Fiano is the principal grape variety.
3. The wine must comply with the appellation’s production rules.
4. The name does not, by itself, tell you the producer, vineyard, vintage or winemaking method.
5. The official style is dry, fresh and harmonious.
6. If the label says **Riserva**, it refers to a regulated category with a minimum ageing requirement.

A label can therefore tell you where the wine belongs, but not everything about how it will taste. For more label-reading help, see [How to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/) and our guide to [protected wine names](/protected-wine-names/).

## What should you remember?

Fiano di Avellino is best understood as **Fiano from a protected place, made under protected rules**. It is a Campanian appellation centred on the Province of Avellino, not a separate grape variety and not a guarantee of one universal flavour profile.

That makes it a useful example of how European wine names work. The grape matters, but so do place, legal boundaries and production rules. When you encounter the name on a bottle, you know more than simply “this contains Fiano”: you also know the wine is presenting itself through the regulated identity of Avellino.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Fiano di Avellino the same as Fiano?

No. Fiano is the grape variety. Fiano di Avellino is a protected appellation for wine made principally from Fiano in a defined area of Campania.

### Is Fiano di Avellino always 100 per cent Fiano?

Not necessarily. The Campania regional authority states a minimum of 85 per cent Fiano, with limited use of permitted varieties including Greco, Coda di Volpe Bianca and Trebbiano Toscano.

### Is Fiano di Avellino sweet?

The official description of the ordinary wine is dry. Dry describes the level of residual sugar, not whether the wine is aromatic or flavourless.

### What is Fiano di Avellino Riserva?

Riserva is a regulated category requiring at least 12 months of ageing from 1 November of the harvest year, along with higher minimum alcohol and extract requirements.

### Is Fiano di Avellino from all of Campania?

No. Campania is the wider Italian region. Fiano di Avellino comes from a legally defined area within the Province of Avellino.
