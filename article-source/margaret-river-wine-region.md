# Margaret River Wine Region: What It Is Known For

## Highlights

- Margaret River is a recognised wine region and Geographical Indication (GI) in the far south-west of Western Australia.
- The region is best known for Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay.
- Sauvignon Blanc–Semillon blends are another important Margaret River style.
- Its Mediterranean climate has a strong maritime influence, with wet winters, dry summers and ocean-moderated conditions.
- Margaret River is not one uniform vineyard: site, soil, variety, vintage and winemaking all affect the final wine.
- A Margaret River label tells you where the grapes come from. It does not guarantee one particular flavour profile or quality level.

## Quick answer: what is Margaret River known for?

Margaret River is especially known for Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay, along with fresh Sauvignon Blanc–Semillon blends. The region’s coastal position in Western Australia, Mediterranean climate, winter rainfall, sea breezes and varied soils help create wines that often combine ripe fruit with freshness, structure and texture.

That description is a useful starting point, not a tasting guarantee. A Margaret River Cabernet can be medium- to full-bodied, but producer, site, vintage and winemaking influence its final shape. A Margaret River Chardonnay may be concentrated and citrusy, or richer and more textural, depending on choices in the vineyard and winery.

The region is also more than a town or tourism destination. Margaret River is a legally recognised Australian Geographical Indication, with a defined geographic origin for wine. You can learn more about how wine regions fit into the wider world of wine in our guide to [what wine is](/what-is-wine/).

## Where is the Margaret River wine region?

Margaret River lies in the far south-west corner of Western Australia, between Cape Naturaliste and Cape Leeuwin. It sits between the Indian and Southern oceans and is one of Australia’s geographically isolated wine regions.

The Margaret River GI covers approximately 2,137 square kilometres. Wine Australia’s current regional profile records about 5,725 hectares of vineyards, with vineyards ranging from sea level to approximately 234 metres in altitude. These figures are a regional snapshot rather than permanent constants: vineyard area and climate statistics can change with reporting periods and data sources.

The GI is not identical to the town of Margaret River, the Shire of Augusta-Margaret River or the broader tourism region. Those places overlap in everyday conversation, but they are not interchangeable wine terms.

## What does Margaret River GI mean?

A Geographical Indication identifies the geographic origin of wine. Wine Australia entered the Margaret River GI in the Register of Protected Names on 30 October 1996.

In practical terms, “Margaret River” on a wine label gives you useful information about where the grapes were grown. It does not, by itself, tell you:

- which grape variety is in the bottle;
- whether the wine is red, white or a blend;
- how much oak was used;
- whether the wine is dry or sweet;
- how the wine will taste;
- or whether it is a high-quality wine.

The legal GI boundary is defined by a written description. A map can help people understand the region’s location, but a promotional or orientation map should not automatically be treated as the legal boundary.

## Does Margaret River have subregions?

Not officially. Margaret River does not currently have recognised GI subregions.

Names including Wilyabrup, Karridale, Yallingup, Carbunup, Treeton and Wallcliffe may be used by producers and wine writers to discuss local site or climate differences. They are useful points of reference, but they should not be described as six official Margaret River subregions.

This distinction matters when reading a label. A locality name may tell you more about a vineyard’s place within the region, but it does not replace the official Margaret River GI.

## How does the climate shape Margaret River wine?

Margaret River has a Mediterranean climate with strong maritime influence. In broad terms, that means:

- winters are generally mild and wet;
- summers are warmer and comparatively dry;
- much of the rainfall arrives during the cooler months;
- ocean influence helps moderate temperature variation;
- afternoon sea breezes can cool vineyards;
- conditions vary between sites and from north to south.

Wine Australia’s current regional snapshot records 202 millimetres of growing-season rainfall and a mean January temperature of 20.9°C. The Margaret River Wine Association publishes different long-term reference figures, partly because climate statistics can use different periods and methods. It is better to treat any number as a dated measurement than as a permanent description of every vintage.

The important idea is balance. Maritime influence can moderate temperature extremes and support a relatively even ripening season. Warm, dry conditions may help grapes reach physiological ripeness, while coastal exposure and sea breezes can help preserve freshness. Variety, vineyard site, crop level, harvest timing, vintage and winemaking still determine the finished wine.

That is why it is too simple to say that the ocean “makes” Margaret River wines elegant, or that every vintage produces the same result.

## Why do site and soil matter?

Margaret River is not one uniform vineyard. Soil depth, gravel content, drainage, slope, rainfall and coastal exposure vary across the GI.

Wine Australia broadly describes the region’s ridge as gravelly loam over granite and gneiss. Western Australian soil and geology research shows a more varied collection of soil-landscape environments, while regional sources describe differences between local areas and changes over relatively short distances.

These conditions can affect how vines access water, how quickly sites warm and how grapes ripen. They help explain why two wines carrying the same Margaret River GI can differ in fruit character, texture, freshness and structure.

However, soil should not be treated as a flavour machine. A gravelly site does not guarantee a particular taste, and ancient geology does not automatically make a wine better. Vineyard decisions and winemaking are part of the story too.

## Margaret River Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Sauvignon is one of Margaret River’s defining varieties. Regional examples are often associated with ripe cassis or other dark-fruit flavours, freshness, structure and firm but polished tannins.

Wine Australia describes Margaret River Cabernet as ranging from medium- to full-bodied, with possible earthy or gravelly notes alongside ripe cassis and violet aromas. That gives a useful expectation without suggesting that every bottle will taste the same.

When choosing a Margaret River Cabernet, consider the style described on the label and the producer’s approach. Some wines may feel more restrained and savoury; others may be richer, more powerful or more oak-influenced.

## Margaret River Chardonnay

Chardonnay is the other major variety associated with Margaret River. The region’s Chardonnay is often linked with concentrated fruit, complexity and pronounced freshness, including lime- or citrus-like acidity.

That combination can make the wines feel both generous and energetic. But Chardonnay is particularly responsive to winemaking. Oak, lees contact, malolactic fermentation, harvest timing and texture-building techniques can all change the result.

One Chardonnay may taste bright and citrusy; another may be creamier, richer or more savoury. The region’s association with Mendoza or Gin Gin Chardonnay material is useful historical and viticultural context, but not every Margaret River Chardonnay comes from one clone, and clone identity alone does not determine quality.

Our guide to [what Chardonnay is](/what-is-chardonnay/) explains the variety in more detail. You may also find our guide to [what oak does to wine](/winemaking/oak.html) useful when comparing richer and more lightly oaked examples.

## Sauvignon Blanc–Semillon and other varieties

Sauvignon Blanc and Semillon are frequently blended in Margaret River. These wines may be crisp, lively, aromatic and juicy, although the balance changes with the proportions of each variety, grape ripeness and the use of oak.

Some examples are designed to be bright and refreshing. Others may have more texture or complexity from oak and winemaking. The blend is therefore more flexible than a single fixed style.

Margaret River also grows Shiraz, Chenin Blanc, Malbec, Merlot and other varieties. Shiraz is a notable part of the region’s broader identity, although Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay deserve the greatest attention in a beginner’s introduction. ## How did Margaret River become a wine region?

The modern commercial wine industry is relatively young, although the land has a much older cultural history. Margaret River is on Wadandi Country, and Wadandi people have longstanding connections with the region.

Modern viticulture was strongly influenced by scientific research. Harold Olmo investigated Western Australian viticultural potential in 1955. In the 1960s, John Gladstones assessed Margaret River’s climate and geography and helped identify its suitability for grape growing.

The first modern commercial plantings followed this research. Vasse Felix was planted in 1967, and Moss Wood was established in 1969. These early developments helped establish the region’s wine identity.

During the 1980s, Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay from Margaret River gained wider attention through international recognition associated with early producers. The region’s GI was formally entered in the Register of Protected Names in 1996.

This history helps explain why Margaret River has a strong fine-wine reputation despite its relatively short modern commercial history: its development was shaped by deliberate site and climate research rather than by chance alone.

## How to choose a Margaret River wine

Start with the grape variety and the style you want:

- **Cabernet Sauvignon:** look for dark fruit, structure and freshness.
- **Chardonnay:** expect the possibility of citrus-like freshness, concentration and varying degrees of oak or texture.
- **Sauvignon Blanc–Semillon:** consider this if you want a lively white, while remembering that oak can create a broader style.
- **Shiraz:** a useful choice when you want to explore a less central but still relevant regional variety.

Then consider the producer, vintage, alcohol level, oak information and any named vineyard or locality. The Margaret River name gives geographic context, but it is not a complete tasting prediction.

A bottle from Wilyabrup or Karridale may offer useful site information, but those names are not official GI subregions. Similarly, a Margaret River label does not guarantee that the wine is full-bodied, heavily oaked, ageworthy or made from Cabernet Sauvignon.

## Frequently asked questions

### What wines is Margaret River best known for?

Margaret River is especially known for Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay. Sauvignon Blanc–Semillon blends are also an important regional style, while Shiraz, Chenin Blanc, Malbec, Merlot and other varieties add diversity.

### Is Margaret River a cool-climate wine region?

It is more useful to describe Margaret River as a moderate Mediterranean region with strong maritime influence. A simple “cool-climate” label does not explain its wet winters, dry summers, ocean moderation and relatively even heat accumulation.

### What makes Margaret River wine distinctive?

Its distinctive identity comes from the interaction of coastal geography, maritime climate, rainfall, sea breezes, soils, varieties and winemaking. No single factor explains every bottle.

### Does Margaret River have official subregions?

No. Localities such as Wilyabrup and Karridale are used to discuss place and site, but they are not currently recognised as official GI subregions.

### What does Margaret River on a wine label mean?

It indicates geographic origin under Australia’s GI system. It does not guarantee a particular variety, flavour profile, oak treatment or quality level.
