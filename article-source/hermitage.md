# Hermitage: A Beginner’s Guide to the Northern Rhône Appellation

## Highlights

- Hermitage is a protected French wine appellation, not a grape variety.
- It sits in the Northern Rhône, within the wider Rhône Valley.
- The appellation is on the Rhône’s left bank, principally around Tain-l’Hermitage.
- Red Hermitage is based on Syrah; white Hermitage uses Marsanne and Roussanne.
- Hermitage and Crozes-Hermitage are separate appellations, despite their similar names.
- Older Australian wine language sometimes used “Hermitage” as a name for Shiraz.
- “Cru” describes Hermitage’s place in the Rhône appellation system; it is not a guarantee that every bottle is the world’s best.

## Quick answer: what is Hermitage?

Hermitage is a protected French AOC/AOP—roughly, a legally protected place-name for wine—in the Northern Rhône. It is also one of the Rhône’s named crus, meaning a specific wine-growing appellation with its own defined area and production rules.

The appellation’s vineyards are on the Rhône’s left bank in the Drôme département. Its legal production area includes delimited vineyard parcels in the communes of Tain-l’Hermitage, Crozes-Hermitage and Larnage. The vineyard is principally associated with the steep Hermitage hill above Tain-l’Hermitage.

Hermitage makes still red and white wines. Red Hermitage is Syrah-led, while white Hermitage is made from Marsanne and Roussanne. A small, distinctive white style called *vin de paille* is also permitted when the wine meets additional production conditions.

The most useful Australian takeaway is simple: **Syrah is the grape; Hermitage is the protected French place.**

## Where is Hermitage?

Hermitage sits within this hierarchy:

**France → Rhône Valley → Northern Rhône → Hermitage**

Tain-l’Hermitage is the main orientation point. Across the Rhône is Tournon-sur-Rhône, while Crozes-Hermitage and Larnage are nearby Drôme communes connected to the appellation’s legally defined area. These towns help locate Hermitage, but no single town defines the whole appellation.

The nearest useful regional centre is the paired area of Tain-l’Hermitage and Tournon-sur-Rhône. From Lyon, Hermitage is roughly 90 kilometres south by road—about an hour in favourable conditions. Treat that as a general orientation, not a precise travel promise.

## What grapes and wines are allowed?

### Red Hermitage

Syrah is the principal red grape. Under the current appellation rules, Syrah must account for at least 85 per cent of the relevant vineyard planting and at least 85 per cent of the finished red wine. Marsanne and Roussanne may be used as accessory varieties within the permitted framework.

That means “Hermitage red” is not necessarily a legal synonym for “100 per cent Syrah”, even though Syrah overwhelmingly defines the style.

Red Hermitage is commonly described as deeply coloured, rich and complex, with possible flavours and aromas of ripe fruit, spice and forest-floor notes. It can be powerful and tannic, and some examples are capable of ageing. These are broad tendencies, not promises made by the appellation.

### White Hermitage

White Hermitage is made from Marsanne, Roussanne or a combination of the two. It is not automatically a single-variety wine.

Official descriptions associate white Hermitage with powerful aromas and flavours, including white flowers, spice and honey, alongside acidity and a rounded or soft impression. “White Hermitage” should not automatically be read as sweet: the separate *vin de paille* mention identifies a particular traditional style made from dried grapes.

## Why does Hermitage taste distinctive?

Hermitage is principally planted on a warm, south-facing hill with steep terraces and retaining walls. The appellation includes a mixture of granitic and metamorphic materials, sandy-clay granitic soils, older alluvial terraces and some calcareous loess deposits.

These conditions help explain the appellation’s association with structured Syrah reds and substantial, aromatic whites. They do not make every wine identical. Vineyard position, vintage, vine material, winemaking and maturation can all influence the finished wine.

This is a useful example of [terroir](/what-is-terroir/) in practice: place matters, but it does not work like a flavour guarantee. For more background, see how [climate affects wine](/how-climate-affects-wine/).

## Hermitage versus Northern Rhône and Crozes-Hermitage

The **Northern Rhône** is a broad regional category containing several separate appellations. Hermitage is one of them.

The **Rhône Valley** is broader again, covering the northern and southern parts of the Rhône wine region. Hermitage is therefore a specific place within a much larger geographic hierarchy. Our guides to the [Rhône Valley](/rhone-valley/) and [Northern Rhône](/northern-rhone/) provide that wider context.

**Crozes-Hermitage is separate from Hermitage.** The names are geographically related, but Crozes-Hermitage is its own AOC/AOP with a much broader legal area. It is not Hermitage’s second wine, a cheaper spelling or a subcategory of Hermitage.

Other separate Northern Rhône crus include Saint-Joseph, Cornas, Saint-Péray, Côte-Rôtie, Condrieu and Château-Grillet.

## What does Hermitage mean on an Australian label?

Australian wine history adds a potential source of confusion. Older Australian wines made from Shiraz were sometimes labelled “Hermitage”. In that historical context, the word was being used as an older name associated with the grape now generally called Shiraz in Australia.

That is different from French Hermitage. On a genuine French label, Hermitage identifies a protected geographic appellation in the Northern Rhône. It does not simply mean “French Shiraz”.

Australian wine descriptions must comply with Wine Australia’s protected-GI rules and must not use protected geographic names falsely or misleadingly. So the safe modern distinction is:

- **Shiraz:** the grape variety name commonly used in Australia.
- **Hermitage:** a protected French place-name and appellation.
- **Historical Australian “Hermitage”:** older terminology for Shiraz, not evidence of French origin.

## Common questions

### Is Hermitage a grape?

No. French Hermitage is a protected place-based appellation. Its red wines are based on the Syrah grape.

### Is Hermitage always red?

No. Hermitage produces both red and white wines. A separate white *vin de paille* style is also permitted.

### Is Hermitage the same as Shiraz?

Not exactly. Syrah and Shiraz are names for the same grape variety, but Hermitage is a French geographic appellation. Older Australian wine language sometimes used “Hermitage” for Shiraz.

### Is Crozes-Hermitage the same as Hermitage?

No. Crozes-Hermitage is a separate appellation with its own larger legal area and production rules.

### Does “cru” mean Hermitage is the best wine?

No. In the Rhône, “cru” identifies a named, specific appellation. It is a classification and geographic term, not a universal quality ranking.
