# What is Mondeuse?

## Highlights

- Mondeuse is a black-skinned wine grape, usually meaning **Mondeuse noire**.
- It is strongly associated with **Savoie and Bugey in eastern France**.
- It generally makes deeply coloured, aromatic red wines with lively acidity and firm tannin.
- Mondeuse noire is different from **Mondeuse blanche**, which is a separate white grape.
- It is also not simply the same grape as Italy’s **Refosco**, despite historical naming confusion.
- In Australia, Mondeuse has a small specialist presence, particularly in central and north-east Victoria.
- The wine can vary considerably with climate, site, vintage, ripeness and winemaking.

## Quick answer

Mondeuse is a black-skinned grape variety used mainly to make red wine. More precisely, the name usually refers to **Mondeuse noire**, an old French variety associated with eastern France, especially Savoie and Bugey.

Wines made from Mondeuse are typically dark in colour, aromatic, fresh in acidity and noticeably tannic. They may show flavours such as bitter cherry, dark plum and other dark-fruit characters. That is a broad guide rather than a guarantee: a cooler site, a warmer vintage, different ripeness levels or a particular winemaking approach can all change the result.

Mondeuse is a useful name to recognise if you enjoy structured, savoury reds but want to explore beyond the familiar varieties. In Australia, it is generally labelled simply **Mondeuse**, although “Mondeuse noire” is the more precise variety name.

## Where does Mondeuse come from?

Mondeuse noire is historically rooted in eastern France. It is particularly important in **Savoie**, where it is one of the main red varieties used for the region’s wines, and in nearby **Bugey**.

These are separate French wine regions, not alternative names for the grape. Savoie and Bugey help explain where Mondeuse is traditionally found, while Mondeuse is the variety itself.

The grape is late-ripening and performs best in suitable warm sites. This helps explain why place matters: a site that allows the grapes to ripen fully can produce a different wine from one where the season is cooler or the fruit struggles to reach maturity.

## What does Mondeuse wine taste like?

A typical Mondeuse red may offer:

- deep colour;
- lively or high acidity;
- firm, sometimes assertive tannin;
- aromatic dark-fruit character;
- bitter-cherry and dark-plum descriptors;
- medium to fuller weight, depending on ripeness, alcohol, extraction and blending.

Acidity gives the wine freshness, while tannin creates a drying, grippy sensation on the gums. Together, they can make Mondeuse feel more structured than soft, immediately approachable reds.

That does not mean every bottle will be heavy or austere. Some examples may be more fragrant and supple, while others may be darker, denser and more tannic. Mondeuse can be bottled on its own or used in blends, and blending can change its texture and balance.

The variety also has ageing potential, although that does not mean every bottle needs years in a cellar. Vintage, fruit quality, structure and winemaking all matter.

## Mondeuse noire, Mondeuse blanche and Refosco: what is the difference?

The names can be confusing.

**Mondeuse noire** is the black-skinned grape that makes red wine. When a bottle simply says “Mondeuse”, this is usually the grape being discussed, particularly in Australia.

**Mondeuse blanche** is a separate white grape variety. The two varieties are genetically related, but Mondeuse blanche is not simply the white-berried version of Mondeuse noire.

**Refosco** needs similar care. Historical plantings in California were sometimes identified as Refosco but were later associated with Mondeuse noire. Italian Refosco varieties, however, are distinct. So “Mondeuse is Refosco” is an unsafe shortcut unless the historical and geographical context is made clear.

This is one reason a grape’s name can tell only part of the story. Regional history, old plant material and changing naming practices can leave the same or similar names attached to different vines.

## Is Mondeuse grown in Australia?

Yes, but the evidence points to a small specialist presence rather than a mainstream Australian variety.

An Australian Wine Research Institute profile published in 2017 reported Mondeuse plantings and production mainly in **central and north-east Victoria**. It also noted that Australian examples could be made as varietal wines or blended with varieties such as Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon.

Those figures are historical evidence, not a current count of Australian vineyards or producers. The variety may therefore be encountered less often than familiar Australian grapes, and availability can vary.

For Australian shoppers, the practical point is simple: a bottle labelled **Mondeuse** will generally be referring to Mondeuse noire. Do not assume that naming conventions used in France, California or Italy transfer automatically to Australian labelling.

## How can the style change?

Mondeuse has a recognisable structural profile, but grape variety is not a fixed recipe.

Climate and site influence how successfully the late-ripening grapes mature. Vintage conditions can change the balance between fruit, acidity and tannin. Ripeness can affect whether the wine feels sharper and more angular or broader and more concentrated.

Winemaking matters too. Extraction, maturation and blending can alter the wine’s colour, aroma, body and tannin. The Australian Wine Research Institute has also linked controlled yields with the possibility of achieving good colour and tannin when the fruit ripens well.

Think of the variety as a starting point, not a promise. “Mondeuse” suggests a fresh, structured red, but the bottle in front of you still reflects its place, season and cellar decisions.

## What food suits Mondeuse?

Its acidity and tannin can make Mondeuse a useful partner for savoury food. You might try it with grilled meat, mushrooms, lentils, rich vegetable dishes or firm cheeses.

These are starting points rather than rules. A lighter, fruitier Mondeuse may work differently from a concentrated, tannic example, and blending or oak can also affect the match. If the wine feels firm on its own, food with savoury richness can help make the combination feel more balanced.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Mondeuse a red or white grape?

Usually, Mondeuse refers to **Mondeuse noire**, a black-skinned grape used mainly for red wine. Mondeuse blanche is a separate white grape.

### Is Mondeuse the same as Syrah or Shiraz?

No. Mondeuse is a different grape. Mondeuse blanche is genetically related to Syrah, but that does not make Mondeuse noire the same as Shiraz or guarantee a similar taste.

### Is Mondeuse the same as Refosco?

Not generally. Historical naming linked Mondeuse noire with Refosco in some Californian contexts, but Italian Refosco varieties are distinct.

### Is Mondeuse always tannic?

It is commonly described as tannic, but the amount and texture of tannin vary with site, ripeness, yield, extraction, maturation and blending.

### Should I cellar Mondeuse?

Some examples have ageing potential, but not every bottle needs long-term cellaring. The wine’s structure, vintage and producer’s style are better guides than the grape name alone.
