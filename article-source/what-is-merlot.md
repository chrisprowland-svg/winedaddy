# What Is Merlot?

## Highlights

- Merlot is a **red wine grape variety**, as well as the name commonly used for wines made from it.
- Most still Merlot is **dry**, even when it tastes of ripe plum or blackberry.
- Merlot often has a soft, rounded texture, moderate acidity and relatively supple tannins.
- It can range from lighter and fresher to fuller, richer and more structured.
- Australian Merlot is made both as a single-variety wine and in blends, often with Cabernet Sauvignon.
- Merlot can suit everyday foods such as burgers, sausages, roast chicken, pizza and mushroom dishes.

## Quick answer: what is Merlot wine?

Merlot is a red grape variety used to make red wine. A bottle labelled “Merlot” is usually a wine made primarily from that grape, although Merlot is also used in blends.

Merlot commonly produces wines with plum, blackberry, black cherry and other ripe-fruit flavours. Depending on where it is grown and how it is made, it may also show chocolate, spice, herbs, cedar or savoury notes.

Most Merlot is dry. Its ripe fruit flavours can make it seem rich or sweet-fruited, but fruity flavour is not the same as sweetness from residual sugar.

In the glass, Merlot is often medium to full bodied, with moderate acidity and tannins that feel softer or rounder than those in many Cabernet Sauvignon wines. That is a broad tendency rather than a rule. Some Merlots are lighter, fresher or more structured.

## What does Merlot taste like?

Merlot often tastes of ripe red and dark fruit. Plum is a common description, but you might also notice blackberry, black cherry or other berry flavours.

Some examples have chocolate or cocoa-like notes. Others may show spice, herbs, cedar or savoury flavours, especially when the wine’s climate, oak maturation, blending or age contributes to its character.

These descriptions are useful signposts, not promises. Merlot does not always taste the same because the grape responds to its growing conditions and winemaking choices.

A warmer site and riper grapes may contribute darker fruit, fuller body and a richer texture. A cooler or maritime site may produce a fresher, more restrained wine. Oak can add flavours such as toast, vanilla, spice or cedar, while unoaked Merlot may taste more directly of fruit.

## Is Merlot sweet or dry?

Most still Merlot is dry.

This is one of the most useful things to understand about the variety. A wine can taste strongly of ripe plum or blackberry without containing much residual sugar. Fruit flavour is an aroma and flavour impression; dryness describes the wine’s sweetness level.

So, if you enjoy rich, ripe fruit flavours but do not want a sweet wine, a dry Merlot may still suit you.

## Is Merlot full bodied?

Merlot can be medium to full bodied. Body is the sense of weight or fullness a wine has in your mouth.

A lighter Merlot may feel fresh and relatively delicate. A riper, warmer-climate or oak-matured example may feel broader and richer. Alcohol, extraction, blending and the wine’s overall balance also influence how full it seems.

Merlot is often described as smooth, but it is worth treating that word carefully. Many examples have supple, rounded tannins, while others can be firm or structured.

**Tannins** are compounds that create grip or dryness in the mouth. Merlot is commonly associated with softer tannins than Cabernet Sauvignon, but that does not mean every Merlot is low in tannin.

## Why does Merlot vary so much?

Merlot’s final style is shaped by a chain of decisions and conditions:

**Climate and site → grape ripeness and acidity → winemaking → oak and blending → finished wine.**

Climate affects how quickly grapes ripen and how much freshness they retain. Vineyard site matters too: Merlot performs poorly in wet conditions and benefits from good drainage.

Winemaking also changes the result. The amount of contact with grape skins can influence colour, tannin and texture. Oak maturation may add flavour and structure, while blending can change the balance between fruit, body and tannin.

Merlot may be bottled as a varietal wine or combined with another grape. In Australia, it is often blended with Cabernet Sauvignon. A blend is not automatically better or worse than a varietal Merlot; it is simply another way to create a particular style.

## Australian Merlot

Australian Merlot is not one uniform style. It is grown in warm inland areas as well as cooler or more maritime regions, so bottles from different parts of the country can feel quite different.

Wine Australia’s 2024 Merlot snapshot identifies the Riverland, Riverina, Murray–Darling/Swan Hill and Coonawarra among important production contexts. Those figures describe production, not a ranking of quality.

Regional examples also show Merlot’s range. Wine Australia describes Merlot in Cowra alongside dark berry, chocolate and eucalypt characters. Southern Fleurieu and Pemberton provide examples of Merlot made both as a varietal wine and in blends with Cabernet Sauvignon.

The useful takeaway is simple: an Australian Merlot label gives you a starting point, not a guarantee of one fixed taste. Region, producer, vintage and winemaking all matter.

## How should you choose a bottle of Merlot?

If you are choosing Merlot for tonight, start with the label and the style description rather than assuming every bottle will taste alike.

Look for:

- **The grape:** “Merlot” indicates the principal variety named on the bottle.
- **The region:** this provides geographic context and may hint at the climate, but does not guarantee a particular style.
- **The body:** descriptions such as “medium bodied” or “full bodied” can help set expectations.
- **The texture:** words such as “soft”, “supple” or “rounded” suggest a gentler tannin impression.
- **Oak:** “oaked” or “unoaked” may indicate whether oak character is likely to be part of the style.
- **Blending:** a label naming Merlot with Cabernet Sauvignon signals a blend rather than a single-variety wine.

You do not need specialist knowledge to enjoy Merlot. The [WineDaddy grape-variety hub](/grapes/) is a useful place to compare it with other familiar varieties, while [what wine is](/what-is-wine/) provides broader context about wine itself.

## What food goes with Merlot?

Merlot’s fruit, body and generally supple tannins make it a flexible match for everyday savoury food.

Try it with:

- burgers or sausages;
- roast chicken;
- pizza;
- pasta with tomato-based sauce;
- meatloaf;
- grilled vegetables;
- mushroom dishes;
- mild to medium cheeses.

These are suggestions, not rules. A lighter Merlot may suit lighter dishes, while a fuller, richer example can stand up to more substantial food.

Serve Merlot slightly cool or at a cool room temperature. The ideal approach depends on the individual wine, but avoiding an overly warm serving temperature can help preserve its freshness.

## Merlot in one sentence

Merlot is a usually dry red wine made from a versatile grape variety, commonly showing ripe fruit and a supple texture while ranging from fresh and medium bodied to rich and structured.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Merlot always smooth?

No. Many Merlots have soft or rounded tannins, but climate, ripeness, extraction, oak and blending can produce firmer, more structured wines.

### Is Merlot an easy-drinking wine?

It can be approachable because of its fruit and supple texture, but “easy drinking” is not a guarantee. Some Merlots are more powerful, savoury or tannic.

### Is Merlot sweeter than Shiraz?

Not necessarily. Merlot and Shiraz can both be made as dry red wines. Perceived sweetness depends on fruit ripeness, alcohol, acidity, tannin and residual sugar, not simply the grape name.

### Is Merlot the same as Cabernet Sauvignon?

No. They are different red grape varieties, although they are sometimes blended together. A detailed comparison belongs in a separate guide.

### Can Merlot be blended?

Yes. Merlot can be bottled on its own or blended with Cabernet Sauvignon and other varieties. Blending can change the wine’s fruit, texture, body and structure.
