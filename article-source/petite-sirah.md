# What Is Petite Sirah? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Petite Sirah is a name commonly used for the red wine grape **Durif**.
- Durif was created in nineteenth-century France from a natural cross between **Syrah and Peloursin**.
- It usually makes deeply coloured, full-bodied and firmly tannic red wines.
- Dark fruit, plum, spice, pepper and floral notes are common descriptions, but no bottle is guaranteed to taste the same.
- In Australia, you are more likely to see **Durif** on a label than Petite Sirah.
- Petite Sirah is not simply another name for Syrah. Syrah is one of its parents, but the varieties are distinct.

## Quick answer: what is Petite Sirah?

Petite Sirah is a red wine grape name most commonly associated with **Durif**, a distinct *Vitis vinifera* variety. Durif originated in France from a natural cross between Syrah and Peloursin.

The grape is known for making wines with deep colour, substantial body and noticeable tannin. Depending on where it is grown and how the wine is made, you may find dark berry and plum flavours, spice, pepper, floral notes or earthy characters.

For Australian wine drinkers, the most useful label lesson is simple: **Petite Sirah and Durif generally refer to the same modern grape variety, but Australian naming favours Durif**. Wine Australia’s OIV-derived variety list identifies Durif as the prime name and Petite Sirah as a synonym.

The name has a more complicated history in California. DNA testing confirmed that Californian material labelled Petite Sirah matched Durif, but older vineyards using the name could also involve Syrah or Peloursin. So it is safest to understand Petite Sirah as the modern international name commonly used for Durif, while remembering that historical labels were not always botanically consistent.

## What does Petite Sirah taste like?

A typical Petite Sirah or Durif is a **deeply coloured, full-bodied red wine with firm tannin**. Tannin is the compound that can create a drying, grippy feeling on your gums. If you want to explore the sensation more confidently, see our guide to [what tannins are](/what-are-tannins/).

Common descriptions include:

- blackberry and other dark berries;
- plum;
- blueberry;
- pepper and spice;
- violet or other floral notes;
- earthy or savoury flavours.

These are tendencies, not a checklist. A warm growing season and ripe grapes may produce a richer, more concentrated wine, while earlier picking or a cooler site may preserve more freshness and create a less dense style.

Oak can contribute vanilla, toast, cedar or spice. It is a winemaking influence rather than an inherent flavour of the grape. Bottle age may also soften the impression of tannin and allow the fruit and savoury characters to become more integrated.

The grape name does not guarantee a particular alcohol level, sweetness, acidity or ageing potential. A wine’s final character also depends on climate, crop level, ripeness, extraction, maturation and storage.

## Why is Petite Sirah so dark and tannic?

The variety is commonly associated with intense colour and firm structure. During red winemaking, contact between grape skins and juice extracts colour and tannin. More intensive extraction can make a wine feel darker, denser and more grippy; gentler extraction can result in a more approachable texture.

Growing conditions matter too. Warm conditions can encourage ripe, concentrated fruit and higher alcohol. Cooler conditions or earlier harvesting may retain more acidity and create a fresher expression. Lower crop levels can contribute to concentration, although yield alone does not determine quality.

This is why two wines made from the same grape can feel quite different. Grape variety gives you a starting point, not a complete tasting forecast. The same principle applies to other familiar grapes such as [Shiraz](/what-is-shiraz/) and [Cabernet Sauvignon](/what-is-cabernet-sauvignon/).

## Is Petite Sirah the same as Durif?

For modern wine identification, **yes, Petite Sirah is commonly treated as a synonym for Durif**.

In Australia, Wine Australia’s official OIV-derived list gives Durif as the prime name and Petite Sirah as a synonym. That is why an Australian bottle is more likely to say Durif.

However, the names are not interchangeable with **Syrah**. Durif was created from Syrah and Peloursin, making Syrah a parent rather than an alternative name. “Petite” does not mean that it is simply a smaller version of Syrah.

Historical Californian use of Petite Sirah also included some confusion between Durif, Syrah and Peloursin. That history explains why a careful explanation is more useful than an absolute statement that every historical bottle labelled Petite Sirah contained exactly the same material.

## Where is Petite Sirah grown in Australia?

Australia grows and uses the variety under the name **Durif**. Wine Australia’s 2020 Durif snapshot recorded 625 hectares planted in its 2015 dataset and 12,796 tonnes crushed in 2020. Those figures are historical and should not be read as current production or market-share statistics.

The same snapshot documented Durif in several Australian wine-growing areas, including:

- Heathcote and Rutherglen in Victoria;
- the Riverina in New South Wales;
- the Riverland in South Australia;
- the Murray–Darling–Swan Hill area.

These are documented growing and crush locations, not exclusive origins. Australian Durif does not have one fixed regional style, and the grape can be encountered in other parts of the country as well.

When reading an Australian label, look first for **Durif**. Petite Sirah may appear as an alternative or international name, but Australian naming practice should not be assumed to apply everywhere in the world.

## How should you serve Petite Sirah?

A full-bodied, tannic example is often more enjoyable **slightly cool rather than warm**. If a young bottle feels especially tight or grippy, decanting may help it seem more open, although decanting is not essential for every wine.

Try it with food that has enough flavour and texture to meet the wine’s structure, such as:

- grilled or roasted red meat;
- braised dishes;
- smoky barbecue-style foods;
- mushrooms;
- firm, savoury cheeses.

These are starting points, not rules. A lighter or more mature example may work with less substantial food. The best pairing is the one that makes both the wine and the meal more enjoyable.

## Does Petite Sirah age well?

Some Petite Sirah or Durif wines can develop with bottle age because tannin, acidity, fruit concentration and alcohol can provide structure. But the grape name alone does not guarantee that a wine will improve or last for a particular number of years.

Storage, winemaking, balance and the individual bottle matter. If you are tasting an older example, look for changes from fresh dark fruit towards softer fruit, savoury flavours and more integrated tannin.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Petite Sirah a sweet wine?

Not necessarily. Petite Sirah usually refers to a red grape variety, not a sweetness level. Many wines made from it are dry, although ripe fruit flavours can make a dry wine seem sweet-fruited.

### Is Petite Sirah stronger than Shiraz?

There is no universal answer. Petite Sirah often has more obvious tannin, colour and structural weight, but alcohol and body vary from wine to wine. It is a different variety from Shiraz, even though Syrah is one of Durif’s parents.

### What is the Australian name for Petite Sirah?

The Australian prime name is **Durif**. Petite Sirah is listed as a synonym in Wine Australia’s OIV-derived variety list.

### What should a beginner notice when tasting it?

Start with colour, then notice how full the wine feels, how drying the tannin is, and whether the fruit tastes dark and ripe or fresher and brighter. You can use our guide to [how to taste wine](/how-to-taste-wine/) for a simple approach.

### What is the best food with Petite Sirah?

Begin with rich, savoury dishes such as grilled meat, braises, mushrooms or hard cheeses. For a broader explanation of matching wine and food, see [how wine pairing works](/how-wine-pairing-works/).
