# Pedro Ximénez sherry: a beginner’s guide to PX

Pedro Ximénez, usually shortened to **PX**, is both a white grape variety and the name commonly used for an intensely sweet style of Sherry made from it.

PX Sherry is usually rich, dark, raisiny and viscous, with flavours that may suggest dried figs, dates, prunes, treacle, coffee or spice. It is not another name for all Sherry. Rather, it is one distinctive style within the broader protected Sherry category.

## Highlights

- **Pedro Ximénez** refers to a grape variety and a wine style.
- **PX Sherry** is naturally sweet, rather than simply a dry Sherry sweetened after production.
- Traditional production may include sun-drying the grapes to concentrate their sugars.
- PX is generally fortified and matured oxidatively.
- It is often served in a small pour alongside dessert, blue cheese or mature cheese.
- “PX” on a label does not automatically tell you the exact sweetness, ageing period or production method.

## What do “Pedro Ximénez”, “PX” and “Sherry” mean?

The name can be confusing because it does several jobs.

**Pedro Ximénez** is the full name of a white grape variety. It is also used for a naturally sweet wine style made principally from that grape.

**PX** is the familiar abbreviation. You may see it on a bottle, wine list or restaurant menu.

**Sherry** is the English form of the protected Jerez-Xérès-Sherry / Jerez / Xérès / Sherry designation. PX Sherry belongs to this broader family, but not every Sherry is PX. Fino, Manzanilla, Amontillado and Oloroso are different styles with different production and ageing characteristics.

In the Jerez classification, PX is a **dulce natural** wine: a naturally sweet category. The published specification describes PX as having at least 160 grams per litre of reducing sugar, although the latest operative specification should be checked before relying on precise regulatory figures.

## Where is PX Sherry made?

The relevant protected designation is in southern Spain. Its legally defined area covers ten municipalities across the Province of Cádiz and part of Seville, including:

- Jerez de la Frontera
- El Puerto de Santa María
- Sanlúcar de Barrameda
- Trebujena
- Chipiona
- Rota
- Puerto Real
- Chiclana de la Frontera
- Lebrija
- San José del Valle

Jerez de la Frontera is a city and one municipality within this wider area. It does not represent the whole legal designation.

You may also hear the term **Sherry Triangle**, generally referring to Jerez de la Frontera, El Puerto de Santa María and Sanlúcar de Barrameda. This is a useful wine-region shorthand, but it is not the complete legal boundary of the protected designation. The existing [Jerez guide](/jerez/) explains this geographic distinction in more detail.

A bottle made from Pedro Ximénez grapes is not automatically Jerez Sherry. The protected geographic name matters. Pedro Ximénez wines are also made outside the Jerez designation, including in other Spanish wine areas.

## How is PX Sherry made?

Traditional PX production often begins with very ripe grapes. The grapes may be spread out in the sun in a process called **soleo** or **asoleo**. In plain English, this is sun-drying.

As the grapes lose water, their sugars and flavours become more concentrated. The must—the juice produced from pressing the grapes—can become unusually dense and sticky. Fermentation is then slow or incomplete because of that concentration.

Wine alcohol is commonly added to stop or limit fermentation. This is **fortification**, the process that gives a wine additional alcohol. The exact sequence and final alcohol level can vary, so PX should not be assumed to have one identical production pathway in every bottle.

PX is generally matured **oxidatively**, meaning it is aged without the protective layer of flor associated with styles such as Fino and Manzanilla. Oxidative ageing can contribute darker colour and developed flavours such as dried fruit, nuts, coffee and caramel-like notes.

Some PX may be aged using a **solera** system, while an **añada** or vintage-based system is also permitted. Solera ageing is therefore relevant to PX, but it is not safe to assume that every PX is solera-aged. The general [solera ageing guide](/solera-ageing/) explains the system separately.

## What does PX taste like?

PX is intensely sweet, but sweetness is only part of its character.

Typical examples may be:

- deep amber, mahogany or dark brown;
- dense, coating and velvety in texture;
- rich in raisin, fig, prune or date flavours;
- reminiscent of treacle, molasses or caramel;
- marked by oxidative notes such as toasted nuts, coffee, cocoa or spice;
- long and persistent on the finish.

These are style tendencies, not a checklist. Colour does not automatically reveal a wine’s age or quality, and a label reading PX does not guarantee that every one of these flavours will appear.

Acidity is important because it helps give the wine shape alongside its sweetness and alcohol. It does not make PX a dry wine, but it can stop the palate feeling entirely heavy or flat.

## How is PX different from other fortified wines?

PX is different from **Fino and Manzanilla**, which are dry styles associated with flor ageing. It is also different from **Amontillado and Oloroso**, which are principally dry styles in the regulatory classification, although sweetened versions exist.

PX is not the same as **Cream Sherry**. Cream is a separate sweetened or blended category. PX may be used as a sweetening component in some Cream Sherries, but the two terms are not interchangeable.

PX and **Port** are both fortified wines, but they come from different protected places and have different grape varieties and production traditions.

Australian **tawny-style fortified wine** may also show sweetness, dried-fruit flavours and oxidative development. It is not interchangeable with PX. Tawny-style wine belongs to an Australian production and labelling context, while PX Sherry is associated with a Spanish grape and, when labelled Sherry, the protected Jerez designation.

## What can a PX label tell you?

A label may tell you:

- that the wine is Pedro Ximénez or PX;
- whether it carries the protected Jerez-Xérès-Sherry / Jerez / Xérès / Sherry name;
- the producer or bottler;
- alcohol by volume and bottle volume;
- permitted ageing or classification terms.

It cannot automatically tell you:

- the exact sugar level;
- how long the wine was aged;
- whether it was matured in a solera or añada system;
- how much sun-drying was used;
- the precise production sequence;
- the complete flavour profile.

If the label says only Pedro Ximénez, look for the geographic designation as well. That helps distinguish a Jerez Sherry from a PX wine made elsewhere.

## How should you serve PX in Australia?

PX is commonly encountered in Australian bottle shops, restaurant wine lists and Spanish-focused wine selections, although availability varies. It is usually poured in a small serving because it is concentrated and sweet.

Serve it chilled or cool, using the general principles in [Wine serving temperature explained](/wine-serving-temperature-explained/). A small wine glass is suitable; there is no need to fill a large glass when a little PX can provide plenty of flavour.

PX works naturally with foods that can meet its sweetness, including:

- blue cheese;
- mature hard cheese;
- vanilla ice cream;
- chocolate, caramel or sticky toffee desserts;
- dried-fruit desserts;
- roasted or spiced nuts.

The key idea is balance. If the food is much less sweet than the wine, the PX may make it seem heavy. Salty, creamy or bitter elements can provide useful contrast.

For Australian drinkers, the simplest way to remember PX is this: **it is a very sweet, fortified Spanish wine made in a distinctive Sherry style, often with concentrated dried-fruit character—but it is not all Sherry, not Cream Sherry and not Australian tawny.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Pedro Ximénez a grape or a wine?

Both. It is the name of a white grape variety and the common name of a naturally sweet wine style made from it.

### Is PX always made in Jerez?

No. PX Sherry made under the protected Sherry designation comes from the legally defined Jerez area, but Pedro Ximénez wines are also made elsewhere.

### Is PX the same as Cream Sherry?

No. Cream is a separate sweetened or blended Sherry category. PX may be used in some Cream Sherries, but the styles are distinct.

### Is PX always aged in a solera?

No. Solera ageing may be used, but añada and other permitted approaches also exist.

### Is PX the same as Australian tawny?

No. They may share sweetness and oxidative dried-fruit character, but they are distinct wines with different geographic and production identities.
