# Anjou wine region: a beginner’s guide

## Highlights

Anjou is a wine area centred around Angers in the western Loire Valley of France. It is not one single wine style. The name is used for a family of protected French appellations producing dry and sweet whites, reds, rosé and sparkling wines.

For an Australian wine learner, the most important point is this: **“Anjou” on a bottle identifies a place and regulated production category, not one grape, colour or sweetness level.**

### Highlights

- Anjou sits around Angers in the western Loire Valley.
- Angers is the most useful regional centre for understanding its location.
- Chenin Blanc is the key reference grape for Anjou whites and many sweet wines.
- Cabernet Franc and Cabernet Sauvignon are important in several red and rosé categories.
- Anjou wines can be dry, sweet, red, white, rosé or sparkling.
- Savennières, Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux and Saumur are separate appellation names, not synonyms for basic Anjou.
- A more specific name on the label usually tells you more about the wine’s category than “Anjou” alone.

## Where is Anjou?

Anjou is in France’s western Loire Valley, around Angers and principally in the department of Maine-et-Loire. The wider wine area follows parts of the Loire and its tributary valleys, with related appellations extending into neighbouring departments.

A useful way to picture the hierarchy is:

**France → Loire Valley wine region → Anjou wine area and appellation family**

Anjou is also discussed within the **Anjou-Saumur** section of the Loire. Saumur lies nearby but is a separate wine area and appellation family.

Angers is the best orientation point. From Nantes, which is west of Angers, parts of the Anjou wine area are roughly an hour by car. That is only a broad guide: the actual time depends on the destination, route and traffic.

Local names you may encounter include Savennières, Chalonnes-sur-Loire, Brissac-Quincé and the Coteaux du Layon area. These places help describe the landscape, but a town name does not automatically define the legal boundary of every Anjou appellation.

## What does “Anjou” mean on a wine label?

France uses protected geographic names for many wines. The term **AOP**, or Appellation d’Origine Protégée, indicates a protected origin category with defined production rules. It is related to the French AOC system and sits within European geographical-indication rules.

The principal Anjou appellation includes categories such as still white and red wines, as well as sparkling white and rosé wines. Other regulated names use Anjou alongside a more specific description, including:

- **Anjou blanc** — white wine
- **Anjou rouge** — red wine
- **Anjou mousseux** — sparkling wine categories
- **Anjou Gamay** — a specific Gamay-labelled category
- **Anjou Villages** and **Anjou Brissac** — more specific red-wine appellation names
- **Cabernet d’Anjou** and **Rosé d’Anjou** — rosé appellations

This is why an Australian shopper should avoid assuming that every Anjou wine tastes alike. A French appellation name may be more prominent on the label than the grape variety. Look for additional wording that identifies colour, style or a narrower category.

## Which grapes are important?

### Chenin Blanc

Chenin Blanc is the central reference point for Anjou’s white wines. It is particularly important in the region’s sweet-wine landscape and in related appellations such as Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux and Quarts de Chaume.

Chenin can produce wines that are dry, sweet or somewhere between those extremes, depending on the appellation, fruit condition, vintage and winemaking choices. That range is one reason Anjou cannot be reduced to a single flavour profile.

### Cabernet Franc and Cabernet Sauvignon

Cabernet Franc and Cabernet Sauvignon are important in Anjou’s red and rosé categories. Cabernet Franc is especially significant in more specific red-wine appellations such as Anjou Villages, while Cabernet varieties are central to Cabernet d’Anjou.

That does not mean “Anjou” generally means Cabernet. It means the relevant grape depends on the particular appellation name and wine category.

### Gamay

Gamay has a specific regulated use in **Anjou Gamay**. Seeing Gamay on a label therefore gives you more information than seeing Anjou alone. It refers to a defined category rather than suggesting that Gamay is the general grape of the whole region.

## What styles of wine does Anjou produce?

Anjou’s range is easier to understand by style than by trying to find one regional taste.

### Dry white wine

Dry white wines may be labelled Anjou blanc or carry a more specific appellation name. Savennières is a separate AOP associated with dry Chenin Blanc and sits within the broader Anjou wine landscape.

### Sweet white wine

Sweet wines are strongly associated with names such as Coteaux du Layon, Bonnezeaux, Quarts de Chaume and Coteaux de l’Aubance. These names are not interchangeable with basic Anjou. They indicate narrower protected categories with their own rules.

### Rosé

Cabernet d’Anjou and Rosé d’Anjou are separate rosé appellations. Their names tell you more about the category than the broad word Anjou alone. Do not assume that every rosé from the area has the same sweetness or texture.

### Red wine

Anjou also produces red wines. Anjou rouge, Anjou Villages and Anjou Brissac are distinct categories. The more specific red appellation name can provide a useful clue that you are looking at a particular regulated style rather than a generic Anjou red.

### Sparkling wine

Sparkling white and rosé wines are also part of the Anjou appellation family. The wording on the label matters: “Anjou mousseux” gives a different starting point from “Anjou blanc” or “Anjou rouge”.

## Why can Anjou wines taste so different?

Anjou’s vineyards sit on slopes and valleys shaped by the Loire and its tributaries. The area includes varied geological materials, including schistose, siliceous and calcareous formations. The western Loire also has Atlantic influence.

These conditions help explain why wines from different sites and appellations can vary. But geography does not mechanically determine flavour. Grape variety, vineyard exposure, vintage conditions, harvest decisions and winemaking also matter.

A useful beginner’s approach is to treat the place name as a starting clue, not a complete tasting note.

## An Australian way to read an Anjou label

When you see an Anjou wine, ask three questions:

1. **What is the full appellation name?**  
   Is it Anjou blanc, Anjou rouge, Cabernet d’Anjou, Anjou Villages or another name?

2. **Is the colour or style stated?**  
   Look for wording that indicates white, red, rosé or sparkling wine.

3. **Is the grape named?**  
   If the label mentions Chenin Blanc, Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon or Gamay, that adds useful information. If it does not, avoid guessing from “Anjou” alone.

This differs from the way many Australian wines foreground grape variety. French labels often expect the protected place name to do more of the work. The French and Australian systems are not identical, so the safest habit is to read the whole label rather than translate one familiar term directly.

## Anjou compared with nearby Loire names

- **Savennières:** a separate appellation associated with dry Chenin Blanc southwest of Angers.
- **Coteaux du Layon:** a separate appellation family strongly associated with sweet white wines.
- **Bonnezeaux and Quarts de Chaume:** narrower protected names associated with sweet wines.
- **Saumur:** a neighbouring but separate Loire appellation family, generally east of the core Anjou area.
- **Muscadet and the Nantes area:** farther west and a separate Loire context; do not treat them as Anjou.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Anjou wine always sweet?

No. Anjou includes dry wines as well as sweet wines. The full appellation name and label wording are important.

### Is Anjou made only from Chenin Blanc?

No. Chenin Blanc is a major reference grape, especially for white and sweet wines, but Cabernet varieties and Gamay also have important, category-specific roles.

### Is Savennières the same as Anjou?

No. Savennières is a separate protected appellation associated with Anjou’s wider wine landscape.

### What should I expect from an Anjou bottle?

Expect variation. The label may describe a dry or sweet white, red, rosé or sparkling wine. The more specific appellation name is your best first clue.

### How should I understand Anjou in one sentence?

Anjou is a western Loire wine area and family of protected appellations, best known for a broad range of wines rather than one fixed grape or style.
