# What is Zierfandler?

Zierfandler is an old Austrian white wine grape variety, principally associated with the Thermenregion in Lower Austria, particularly around the wine town of Gumpoldskirchen. It can produce both dry and sweet white wines, generally offering fine fruit, fresh or harmonious acidity and a fuller, extract-rich structure.

## Highlights

- Zierfandler is a grape variety, not a wine region or general wine style.
- Its main home is Austria’s Thermenregion, south of Vienna.
- **Spätrot** is its most useful alternative name.
- It can be made as a single-variety wine or blended with Rotgipfler.
- The wines may be dry or sweet, depending on ripeness, winemaking and residual sugar.
- Zierfandler is not the same grape as Zinfandel.
- Australian cultivation has not been verified in the available authoritative research, but Australian drinkers may encounter imported examples.

## Where does Zierfandler come from?

Zierfandler is strongly linked with the Thermenregion, an Austrian wine region in Lower Austria that lies south to south-west of Vienna. Gumpoldskirchen is one of its best-known localities, alongside places such as Baden, Pfaffstätten, Mödling, Sooß and Traiskirchen.

For orientation, Baden bei Wien is a useful regional centre. Gumpoldskirchen is roughly 25–35 kilometres from central Vienna, or about 30–45 minutes by car in ordinary conditions. That is a travel guide rather than a precise promise: traffic and your starting point will change the journey.

On a bottle, **Thermenregion** refers to the wider protected wine-region context. A name such as Gumpoldskirchen refers to a locality and may have specific Austrian legal usage. The two should not automatically be treated as interchangeable.

Zierfandler is also known as **Spätrot**. The name relates to the reddish colour that can develop on sun-exposed berries late in ripening. You may also encounter Zierfandler in the traditional **Spätrot-Rotgipfler** blend, made from Zierfandler and the separate white grape Rotgipfler.

## What does Zierfandler wine taste like?

There is no single guaranteed Zierfandler flavour profile. Authoritative Austrian descriptions point to a subtle bouquet, fine fruit, acidity and a full, fruity or extract-rich texture. In practical terms, that suggests a white wine that can feel both fresh and substantial rather than simply light and delicate.

The grape can be used for dry wines as well as sweeter styles. Ripeness, residual sugar, site, maturation and winemaking all affect the finished wine. A lighter, fresher example will not necessarily resemble a richer, more developed or sweeter one.

Suitable examples can also have ageing potential. That does not mean every bottle needs to be cellared; it means the grape can produce wines that develop positively over time when the wine, vintage and storage conditions support it.

Zierfandler is thought to be a natural crossing of Roter Veltliner and a Traminer-like variety, although the second parent has not been definitively identified. That detail helps explain its place in Austria’s grape history, but you do not need to know the parentage to understand the wine.

## How might Australians encounter it?

Australian cultivation was not verified in the available authoritative research, so it would be too strong to say that Australia has no Zierfandler vines. The variety is principally Austrian.

Australian consumers may nevertheless see the name on imported-wine listings, including blends that contain Zierfandler alongside other grapes. On a label, look for **Zierfandler**, **Spätrot** or the blend name **Spätrot-Rotgipfler**. Austrian regional wording may also identify the Thermenregion or a qualifying locality.

## Zierfandler or Zinfandel?

Despite the similar names, they are different grapes. Zierfandler is a white grape associated mainly with Austria. Zinfandel is a different red grape, particularly associated with the United States and known as Primitivo in Italy.

So if you see “Zierfandler”, think Austrian white wine with a potentially fresh, full and varied style—not Zinfandel under another spelling.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Zierfandler sweet?

It can be sweet, but it is not inherently a sweet grape. Zierfandler is used for both dry and sweet wines.

### Is Spätrot the same as Zierfandler?

Spätrot is a recognised name for Zierfandler. **Spätrot-Rotgipfler**, however, refers to a traditional blend of Zierfandler and Rotgipfler.

### Is Zierfandler the same as Zinfandel?

No. They are separate grape varieties with different identities and principal regions.
