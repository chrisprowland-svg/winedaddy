# What Are Wine Grape Clones?

## Highlights

- A grapevine clone is a selected vine reproduced through cuttings, buds or grafting.
- Clones belong to grape varieties such as Pinot Noir, Chardonnay or Shiraz.
- A clone is not the same as a rootstock: the clone provides the fruiting variety, while the rootstock provides the roots.
- Different clones may vary in ripening time, yield, bunch shape, berry size, vigour and health status.
- Clone choice can influence wine style, but it never determines the finished wine by itself.
- A clone number is not a guaranteed tasting note, quality score or prestige marker.

## Quick Answer: What Is a Wine Grape Clone?

A wine grape clone is a group of grapevines descended through vegetative propagation from one selected parent vine.

Instead of growing from seed, a vine is reproduced from a bud, cutting or grafted piece of the original plant. This preserves the selected vine’s genetic identity with a high degree of similarity.

The selected vine may have useful characteristics, such as a particular ripening time, yield, bunch structure, berry size or level of vigour. Once that material has been evaluated and multiplied, it may be identified as a clone within a grape variety.

For example, Pinot Noir is a grape variety. A particular Pinot Noir clone is one selected line within that variety.

Clone choice can affect how grapes grow and ripen, and may contribute to differences in the resulting wine. But the finished wine also reflects the vineyard site, climate, soil, rootstock, vine age, farming, vintage, harvest decisions and winemaking.

The simplest way to think about it is:

> **Variety + clone + site + season + farming + winemaking = finished wine**

A clone is one part of that equation, not the whole answer.

## Clone, Variety and Rootstock: What’s the Difference?

These terms describe different parts of a grapevine.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Grape variety** | The broad named type of grape, such as Pinot Noir, Chardonnay or Shiraz |
| **Clone** | A selected line within a variety, reproduced from one parent vine |
| **Rootstock** | The root-forming part of a grafted vine |
| **Scion** | The upper, fruit-bearing part grafted onto the rootstock |

A vineyard might therefore use a particular Chardonnay clone grafted onto a particular rootstock.

The clone supplies the fruiting variety. The rootstock forms the root system and can influence factors such as vigour, water access, soil adaptation and response to pests or conditions.

A clone is also not a new grape variety. Pinot Noir remains Pinot Noir whether a vineyard uses one selection or another.

## Why Do Growers Use Different Clones?

Grapevines have been cultivated and propagated for centuries. Individual vines within a variety can show natural differences. If one vine appears to have useful characteristics, growers or researchers can preserve those characteristics by reproducing it vegetatively.

Clonal selection involves identifying, evaluating and multiplying selected vine material. Selection may consider:

- how early or late the grapes ripen;
- yield and crop consistency;
- bunch compactness;
- berry size;
- vine vigour;
- colour or phenolic development;
- disease sensitivity or resilience;
- suitability to a particular site or climate.

These differences can matter in a vineyard planted for decades. An early-ripening selection may help in a cool region or short growing season. A later-ripening selection may suit a longer season or a site where growers want to avoid rapid sugar accumulation.

Neither is automatically better. The right choice depends on the vineyard’s conditions and the wine style the grower is trying to produce.

## How Can Clones Affect Grape Growing?

### Ripening

Clones within the same variety may reach maturity at different times. That can affect harvest planning and the balance between sugar, acidity, flavour and phenolic maturity.

The same clone will not necessarily ripen identically everywhere. Temperature, water availability, soil, crop level and seasonal weather all have an effect.

### Yield and bunch structure

Clones may differ in productivity, bunch size, berry size and bunch compactness. A loose bunch and a compact bunch can create different airflow and disease conditions.

Berry size can also change the relative amount of skin to juice. That may influence the raw material available for winemaking, but farming decisions and vintage conditions matter too.

### Vigour and site suitability

A vine’s apparent vigour is an interaction between clone, rootstock, soil, water, climate, planting density and vineyard management.

A clone that performs well on one site is not automatically the best choice for another. This is one reason clone information is most useful when it is accompanied by context about the vineyard.

### Plant health

Planting material also has an identity and health dimension. Growers want confidence that the material is the variety and selection it is claimed to be, and that it has been assessed for relevant health risks.

Identity, health status and field performance are separate things. A vine can be correctly identified without being suited to every site, and certified planting material does not guarantee a particular wine style.

## How Can Clones Affect Wine Style?

Clones can influence the grapes arriving at the winery. Differences in ripening time, berry and bunch structure, crop level or grape composition may contribute to differences in:

- acidity and sugar at harvest;
- colour potential;
- aroma-related compounds;
- tannin and phenolic development;
- texture;
- concentration.

But these effects are conditional. The same clone can produce different wines in different regions, vintages or wineries. Conversely, wines from different clones may taste similar when they come from similar sites and are made in similar ways.

This is why it is risky to translate a clone number directly into a tasting note. A named Pinot Noir clone does not guarantee floral aromas, and a named Chardonnay clone does not guarantee a particular texture or level of richness.

Clone choice may help explain a grower’s decisions. It cannot, by itself, explain the entire character of a wine.

## What Is Massal or Field Selection?

**Massal selection**, also called field selection, involves taking propagation material from several desirable vines in an existing vineyard or population rather than multiplying one formally identified clone.

This approach can preserve a broader range of genetic diversity and may help maintain heritage or locally adapted material. Formal clonal selection, by contrast, generally involves evaluating and documenting one selected line, including its agronomic and health characteristics.

Neither approach is automatically superior.

Clonal material may offer greater consistency and traceability. Massal selection may preserve diversity and history, but its identity and health status can be less uniform unless the material has been carefully tested and documented.

## What Does This Mean in Australia?

Australia uses planting material from different propagation and selection systems. Clone names and numbers should not be treated as a universal international code: their meaning can depend on the variety, collection, nursery or identification system involved.

Wine Australia is developing VitiCert Australia, a national certification programme for grapevine planting material. The programme is intended to improve confidence in vine identity and health across the propagation supply chain. It launched in 2026, with the first certified cuttings expected from winter 2028. That does not mean all Australian vine material is currently VitiCert-certified.

The Australian Wine Research Institute also provides DNA-based grapevine clonal identification using a verified reference database. This reflects a practical issue: names and records are important, but identity can sometimes require scientific verification.

Australia also maintains and develops collections of grape varieties, clones, rootstocks and heritage material. This work supports access to planting material with better-known identity and health status while helping preserve valuable older vines.

## How Should Wine Drinkers Interpret Clone Information?

If a producer mentions a clone, you can reasonably understand it as information about a selected planting line within a grape variety.

It may help explain:

- why the grower chose that material;
- how the vines suit a particular site;
- when the grapes tend to ripen;
- how the vineyard manages crop levels;
- why several selections may be blended.

You should not assume that a clone name or number tells you:

- exactly how the wine will taste;
- whether the wine is better;
- whether the material is prestigious;
- whether it is certified or disease-free;
- whether it is used throughout a region;
- whether it expresses terroir more strongly.

A useful question is:

> **What does this clone information add to my understanding of the vineyard?**

If the producer explains the clone’s role in ripening, crop level or site suitability, it may be genuinely useful. If the label simply lists a number, there may not be enough information to turn it into a reliable flavour prediction.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are clones genetically identical?

They are highly similar vegetative descendants of one selected vine, but “identical in every way” is too absolute. Health status, propagation history, rootstock, vine age and growing conditions can all affect field performance.

### Is a clone the same as a variety?

No. Pinot Noir and Chardonnay are varieties. A clone is a selected line within one of those varieties.

### Is a clone the same as a rootstock?

No. The clone normally refers to the fruiting part of the vine. The rootstock provides the root system beneath it.

### Does a clone determine a wine’s flavour?

No. A clone may contribute to grape composition and wine style, but site, climate, farming, vintage, harvest timing and winemaking also matter.

### Does certified planting material guarantee better wine?

No. Certification can improve confidence in identity and health. It does not guarantee quality, flavour or commercial success.

### Why might a winery mention a clone?

It may be giving context about vineyard material, ripening behaviour, site suitability or the way a wine was assembled. The meaning is strongest when the producer explains the clone alongside the vineyard and winemaking choices.

## The Takeaway

A wine grape clone is a selected vine line reproduced from one parent vine. It sits within a grape variety and is separate from the rootstock.

Clones can help growers manage ripening, yield, bunch structure, vine vigour and plant health. Those choices may influence the grapes and contribute to wine style.

But a clone is not a flavour shortcut. To understand the wine in your glass, consider the whole picture: the variety, the clone, the site, the season, the farming and the winemaking.
