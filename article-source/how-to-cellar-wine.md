# How to Cellar Wine: A Practical Guide for Australian Homes

**Primary topic:** Wine storage and cellaring

## Highlights

- Store unopened wine somewhere **cool, dark, stable and relatively undisturbed**.
- Avoid heat extremes and repeated temperature swings; these are often bigger risks than missing one “perfect” temperature.
- For long-term storage, a broad practical target is around **10–16°C**, depending on the guidance and conditions.
- Natural-cork bottles are conventionally stored on their side. Screw-cap bottles can stand upright.
- A wine fridge is useful when your home cannot provide stable conditions, but not every bottle needs one.
- Good storage protects wine; it does not guarantee that every wine will improve with age.

## Quick answer: how should you cellar wine?

To cellar unopened wine, choose the coolest suitable place in your home that stays dark, stable and free from excessive movement, heat and strong odours.

For long-term storage, many wine guides use a broad target of approximately **10–16°C**. Wine Australia’s educational guidance gives approximately **13–16°C**, while other recognised guidance uses a broader **10–15°C** range under controlled, relatively constant conditions. These figures are useful targets, not laws. In practice, avoiding a hot location or repeated large temperature changes is usually more important than chasing one exact number.

Keep bottles away from direct sunlight, kitchens, laundries, heating equipment and places that vibrate or are frequently disturbed. Natural-cork bottles are traditionally stored on their side for long-term storage. Screw-cap bottles do not need to lie down and can be stored upright.

You do not need a traditional underground cellar. A stable interior cupboard may work for a small collection. If your home becomes hot in summer, a wine fridge can provide more reliable conditions. But remember: storage preserves a wine; it does not make every wine suitable for ageing or guarantee that it will improve.

## What does “cellaring wine” mean?

Cellaring means storing unopened wine under conditions intended to preserve it over time. Some wines are also capable of developing positively in the bottle, but cellaring and ageing are not the same thing.

Good storage reduces avoidable risks such as heat damage, light exposure, closure problems and unnecessary movement. It cannot turn a young-drinking wine into a long-term ageing wine. Whether a bottle will improve depends on the individual wine, its structure, balance, closure, vintage and producer intent.

## The conditions that matter most

### Temperature and stability

Wine should be kept cool, but **stable coolness** is the more useful idea for most households.

Wine Australia gives approximately 13–16°C as a long-term cellaring guide. WSET educational material uses approximately 10–15°C under controlled, relatively constant conditions. A practical way to use these recommendations is to aim for roughly 10–16°C where possible, while prioritising protection from heat extremes and repeated large rises and falls.

Australian homes can vary dramatically by region, construction, insulation and orientation. A location that seems comfortably cool in winter may become hot in summer. Garages, sheds, roof spaces and sunny rooms are especially worth checking rather than assuming.

Repeated temperature changes—sometimes called **thermal cycling**—are undesirable. Significant changes can contribute to leakage and movement of cork closures, and temperature extremes can damage wine. If you are storing bottles for years, use a thermometer or temperature logger to check the location during hot weather.

### Light

Store wine in darkness or shade. Direct sunlight and strong prolonged light can affect wine quality, including through lightstruck flavours.

A dark cupboard, wine fridge or shaded cabinet is preferable to a bright display shelf near a window. Decorative presentation may look appealing, but exposed bottles are not in their best long-term storage environment.

Ordinary household lighting does not instantly ruin a bottle. The useful rule is simply to avoid strong or prolonged exposure.

### Movement and vibration

Keep long-term bottles somewhere they will not be repeatedly moved, knocked or rearranged. Avoid unstable shelving and locations beside appliances that create continuous vibration.

The evidence supports minimising unnecessary movement, but there is no need to treat every small household vibration as a disaster. Secure, undisturbed storage is the practical goal.

### Humidity

Humidity matters most for natural-cork closures. Wine Australia’s cellaring guidance gives approximately **70% relative humidity** as a traditional long-term reference. Very dry conditions may contribute to cork problems, while excessive dampness can damage labels, cartons, shelving and equipment.

Most people do not need to humidify a cupboard. Keep the storage space clean and dry, avoid extremely dry or damp conditions, and do not create a wet environment around the bottles.

### Cleanliness and odours

Choose a clean storage area away from petrol, paint, chemicals, mould and strong cleaning products. This is particularly sensible when bottles are stored in cartons or for long periods.

Wine does not automatically absorb every smell in the room. The practical point is that a clean, neutral environment is preferable to a garage shelf beside fuel cans or a cupboard used for strong chemicals.

## Should wine bottles lie down?

Bottle position depends on the closure.

- **Natural cork:** Side storage is the conventional recommendation for long-term cellaring because it helps keep the cork from becoming excessively dry.
- **Screw cap:** Upright storage is acceptable. A screw cap does not rely on being kept wet in the same way as natural cork.
- **Synthetic or other closures:** Do not impose one universal rule. Follow closure-specific or producer guidance where available.

The familiar instruction that every wine bottle must lie on its side is too broad. Closure, storage duration and the security of the shelf all matter.

## Where should you store wine in an Australian home?

Assess a location by its actual conditions rather than its name.

### Interior cupboard

A dark interior cupboard may suit a small collection if it remains reasonably cool and stable. Keep it away from ovens, cooktops, dishwashers, heating vents and hot-water systems. Check how warm it becomes in summer.

### Under-stair cupboard

An under-stair cupboard can be useful in a home without a basement, provided it is dark, dry, reasonably cool and not exposed to household heat. It is not automatically a cellar simply because it is under the stairs.

### Spare room

A shaded spare room may work if it remains temperature-stable. An indoor room that becomes very hot in summer is still a poor long-term location.

### Garage

Garages may experience heat extremes, seasonal cycling, vehicle fumes, light and vibration. Some insulated garages may perform differently, so measure the conditions before using one for valuable or age-worthy bottles.

### Shed or roof space

Sheds and roof spaces are commonly high-risk locations. Lightly insulated metal sheds and roof cavities can become extremely hot and change temperature quickly. They are generally unsuitable for long-term ageing unless conditions are properly controlled.

### Kitchen or laundry

Kitchens experience heat, light and frequent activity. Laundries may add humidity, appliance vibration and heat from dryers or hot-water systems. A cupboard well away from these sources is a separate case, but the room itself is not automatically suitable.

## Is a wine fridge necessary?

A wine fridge is useful when your home cannot provide a cool, stable place. It can be particularly practical for renters, people in hot homes, small collections intended for years of storage, or valuable and age-worthy bottles.

Its main benefit is control—not magic. A wine fridge still needs suitable placement and safe operation according to the manufacturer’s instructions. It also costs money, uses electricity and has limited capacity.

For a few bottles that will be enjoyed relatively soon, the best dark, stable cupboard in the home may be enough. For a collection intended to age for years, controlled storage becomes more valuable.

## A simple storage plan for renters and small collections

1. Choose the darkest, coolest stable location available.
2. Keep bottles away from kitchens, laundries, windows, heating equipment and garages where possible.
3. Check the temperature during hot weather, not only on a mild day.
4. Store natural-cork bottles on their side; screw-cap bottles may stand upright.
5. Use secure shelving and avoid repeatedly moving the bottles.
6. Keep the area clean, dry and free from strong chemical or fuel odours.
7. If the home becomes too hot or unstable, consider a wine fridge rather than trying to force an unsuitable cupboard to work.

## Cellaring unopened wine is different from storing an opened bottle

This guide concerns unopened bottles intended for storage. Once a bottle is opened, the priority becomes slowing oxidation: re-close it, store it upright and refrigerate where appropriate.

That is a different problem from long-term cellaring. See **How to store an open bottle of wine** when that guide is available.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I store wine in a normal kitchen fridge?

A normal refrigerator is useful for short-term chilling and opened-bottle preservation, but it is not automatically ideal for long-term unopened-bottle cellaring. It is colder than typical cellar targets, is opened frequently and may not provide the conditions you want for years of storage.

### Is a garage always bad for wine?

No. Garages vary with insulation, location and season. However, many become much hotter than the home and experience larger temperature swings, light, vibration or fumes. Check the actual conditions before storing wine there long term.

### Does every wine improve with age?

No. Some wines are intended to be enjoyed young, while others can develop over time. Correct storage helps preserve a bottle but does not guarantee that it will improve. A separate guide should cover ageing potential in detail.

### Do screw-cap wines need to lie down?

No. Screw-cap bottles can be stored upright. Side storage is the conventional long-term recommendation for natural-cork bottles.

### Do I need a cellar to store wine?

No. A dark, stable interior cupboard may be suitable for a small collection. If your home becomes hot or changes temperature substantially, a wine fridge can provide more reliable control.
