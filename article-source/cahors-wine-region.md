# Cahors wine region: a beginner’s guide to French Malbec

## Highlights

- Cahors is a wine region and protected French appellation in south-west France.
- It sits in the Lot Valley, in the Lot département of Occitanie.
- Cahors is best known for red wines based on Malbec, locally called Côt.
- Malbec must make up at least 70% of a Cahors blend; Merlot and Tannat may complete it.
- “Cahors” is the place name. “Malbec” is the grape name.
- Cahors reds are often deeply coloured, structured and fresh, with dark-fruit, floral, spicy or savoury notes.
- The appellation is reserved for still red wine.
- A Cahors label follows French and European rules, not the Australian GI system.

## Quick answer: what should you know about Cahors?

Cahors is both a wine-producing area in south-west France and the name of its protected AOC/AOP appellation. The vineyards follow the Lot River around the city of Cahors, in the Lot département of Occitanie.

The appellation is particularly associated with Malbec, known locally as Côt. Malbec must account for at least 70% of the blend, while Merlot and Tannat are authorised complementary varieties. Cahors is therefore a useful example of the difference between a **place** and a **grape**: Cahors tells you where the wine comes from, while Malbec tells you the principal variety.

Cahors wines are red only under the appellation. Broadly, they may show deep colour, dark fruit, violet-like floral notes, spice, liquorice, savoury flavours, freshness and firm tannins. That is a starting point rather than a guarantee. Site, vintage, producer and winemaking can all change the final wine.

## Where is Cahors?

Cahors is in France, in the wider South-West France wine area. More specifically, it is in the Lot département of the Occitanie administrative region, north of Toulouse.

The city of Cahors is the most useful regional centre and an important orientation point, but it does not define the entire appellation. The wine area includes localities such as Luzech, Puy-l’Évêque, Prayssac, Mercuès, Douelle, Caillac and Bélaye, among others.

From Toulouse, Cahors is roughly 1¼ to 1½ hours by road, depending on the route, traffic and starting point. Treat that as a travel orientation, not a precise promise.

Cahors is separate from Bordeaux, Bergerac, Gaillac and Fronton. Côtes du Quercy is also a separate appellation, even though it belongs to the broader Quercy landscape.

## What does “Cahors” mean on a wine label?

For an Australian drinker, the simplest comparison is with an Australian Geographic Indication. Both systems connect a wine name with a defined geographic origin. But they are not identical systems.

On a French label, **Cahors** is a protected appellation name governed by French and European rules. It is not simply the name of a town, and it does not function like a grape variety.

That means:

- **Cahors** means the regulated place of origin.
- **Malbec** or **Côt** means the grape.
- The appellation covers still red wines.
- The name does not promise one exact flavour profile.

This is different from an Australian bottle labelled Malbec, which may come from any number of Australian regions and is identified primarily by its grape variety and geographic indication. For a broader explanation of the terminology, see [French AOC and AOP](/french-aoc-and-aop/) and [Australian Geographic Indications](/australian-geographic-indications/).

Cahors was formally recognised as an appellation in 1971. That milestone helps explain why the name carries regulatory meaning beyond ordinary place description.

## Which grapes are used in Cahors?

Malbec is the principal grape of Cahors. In the region, you may also see it called Côt. Older historical sources may use the name Auxerrois, but that term needs care because it can be confused with Auxerrois blanc, a separate white grape.

Under the Cahors rules, Malbec must make up at least 70% of the blend. Merlot and Tannat are the authorised complementary varieties.

This blending rule does not mean every Cahors wine tastes the same. Merlot may contribute fruit and suppleness, while Tannat can add structure, but the result depends on the proportions, vineyard site and winemaking decisions.

For a general introduction to the grape itself, read [What is Malbec?](/malbec/).

## What are Cahors wines like?

Cahors has a long-standing reputation for dark, substantial red wines. Regional descriptions often mention blackberry, blackcurrant, violet, spice, liquorice and savoury notes. Tannins may feel firm and structuring, while acidity can provide freshness.

Some Cahors reds are concentrated and powerful. Others are more fruit-led, aromatic or supple. Oak maturation may add another layer, but oak is a winemaking choice rather than an automatic feature of the appellation.

It is safer to think in tendencies than rules:

> Cahors often gives Malbec a dark, structured and fresh French expression, but the bottle in front of you may be lighter, softer, more floral or more savoury than the traditional “black wine” image suggests.

The phrase “black wine” is historical language associated with the deep colour of some Cahors wines. It is not an official style category and should not be taken to mean every modern Cahors is opaque, heavy or highly tannic.

## How do the Lot River and landscape matter?

The Lot River is central to Cahors’ geography. Vineyards occupy parts of the winding valley, its alluvial terraces and the limestone slopes and plateaux known as the Causses.

These settings can differ in elevation, exposure, drainage, soil depth and the proportion of gravel, sand, silt, clay or limestone. Those differences help explain why wines from the same appellation can vary.

The river does not literally give the wine its flavour. A better way to understand its influence is that the valley creates a range of vineyard conditions. Climate does something similar: Cahors sits between Atlantic, continental and warmer southern influences, contributing to variation in ripening and freshness.

This is a useful way to think about [terroir](/what-is-terroir/) without turning soil or geography into a fixed flavour recipe.

## Cahors and other Malbec wines

Cahors and Argentine Malbec may share the same principal grape, but they are not interchangeable categories.

Cahors is identified first by its protected place name. Argentine Malbec is generally identified through Argentina’s geographic and labelling system, with the grape name often more prominent. Climate, elevation, soil, blending, extraction, oak and bottle age can all influence the difference.

The useful lesson is not that every French Malbec tastes one way and every Argentine Malbec tastes another. It is that the same grape can express itself differently when place, rules and winemaking change.

## What should you remember when buying or tasting Cahors?

If you see Cahors on a French wine label, expect a still red wine whose principal permitted grape is Malbec/Côt. Look for a wine that may combine dark fruit and floral or savoury character with freshness and tannin.

If you prefer softer reds, do not assume the appellation is automatically too powerful. Style varies. If you enjoy structured reds, Cahors may be a useful region to explore.

You do not need to pronounce every French term perfectly to enjoy it. Just remember the key distinction:

> **Cahors is the place. Malbec is the grape.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Cahors the same as Malbec?

No. Cahors is a protected French appellation and wine region. Malbec is the principal grape variety used there.

### Is Cahors wine always red?

Under the Cahors AOC/AOP, the designation is reserved for still red wines.

### What grapes are allowed in Cahors?

Malbec, locally called Côt, must make up at least 70% of the blend. Merlot and Tannat are authorised complementary varieties.

### Is Cahors part of Bordeaux?

No. Cahors is a separate appellation in the wider South-West France wine area.

### Is Cahors Malbec the same as Australian Malbec?

They may use the same grape, but they come from different geographic and regulatory systems. A Cahors label identifies a protected French place; an Australian Malbec label does not mean Cahors.
