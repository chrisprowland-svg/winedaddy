# What is Frappato?

Frappato is a red wine-grape variety associated mainly with southeastern Sicily, particularly the area around Vittoria. It commonly makes dry, fragrant red wines that are relatively light- to medium-bodied, fresh and gentle in tannin.

Frappato is also one of the two grapes used in Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG, a regulated Sicilian wine blend made with Frappato and Nero d’Avola. Frappato and Cerasuolo di Vittoria are not the same thing: one is a grape variety, while the other is an appellation-controlled wine.

## Highlights

- Frappato is a red grape variety from Sicily.
- It is especially associated with southeastern Sicily and the Vittoria area.
- Wines made from it are often ruby-coloured, aromatic and fresh.
- Red berries, flowers and spice are common flavour impressions.
- Many examples have relatively soft tannins rather than a firm, drying grip.
- Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG must contain both Frappato and Nero d’Avola.
- Site, vintage and winemaking can make Frappato taste lighter, fuller, fruitier or more savoury.
- A light Frappato can be enjoyable served slightly cool.

## What does Frappato taste like?

Frappato is often recognised by its perfume and freshness rather than by deep colour or powerful tannin. Many wines have a ruby appearance and aromas that suggest red berries, wild strawberry, raspberry, flowers or spice.

On the palate, Frappato is commonly dry, bright and relatively easy to drink. Its acidity can make the wine feel lively, while its moderate or gentle tannins usually create less of a drying sensation than you might find in a heavily structured red.

These are tendencies, not guarantees. A Frappato made from riper fruit, given more extraction or matured in wood may feel fuller and more savoury. Another example might be pale, delicate and strongly fruit-driven. The grape gives the wine a starting point, but it does not determine every detail.

## Where does Frappato come from?

Frappato is principally associated with Sicily, the large Italian island in the Mediterranean. Its strongest modern identity is in southeastern Sicily, especially around Vittoria and the province of Ragusa.

That association is useful when reading a wine label, but it does not mean every Frappato comes from Vittoria. The grape can be made into varietal wine outside that area, and its legal regional associations do not define the entire planting range of the variety.

The Vittoria DOC production area includes parts of the Sicilian provinces of Ragusa, Caltanissetta and Catania. This is the area covered by that appellation’s rules, not a complete definition of where Frappato can be grown.

## How is Frappato related to Nero d’Avola?

The most important relationship is found in Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG.

Under the appellation rules, Cerasuolo di Vittoria must contain:

- 50–70% Nero d’Avola
- 30–50% Frappato

This makes Cerasuolo di Vittoria a blend, not a synonym for Frappato. Nero d’Avola generally makes up the larger share, while Frappato is the other required grape. The blend’s final character depends on the proportions used, the growing conditions and how the wine is made.

You may also see Frappato used in a varietal wine. The Vittoria DOC includes a “Vittoria Frappato” category requiring at least 85% Frappato, with limited scope for other eligible red grapes. A bottle labelled simply “Frappato” may therefore be a varietal wine, while a bottle labelled “Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG” refers to a specific regulated blend.

## Why can Frappato wines taste different?

Grape variety is only one part of a wine’s identity. Several choices and conditions affect the finished style:

### Vineyard and vintage

Temperature, water availability, exposure and the timing of ripening can influence acidity, sugar, aroma development and tannin. Fruit from a warmer site or a warmer vintage may produce a rounder, more concentrated wine. A cooler or less ripe season may emphasise freshness and lighter fruit.

### Ripeness and extraction

Harvest timing affects the balance between fruit intensity, acidity and structure. Winemakers also decide how much colour and tannin to extract from the grape skins during fermentation. More extraction can create a deeper, firmer wine; gentler handling can preserve a lighter, more delicate profile.

### Fermentation and maturation

Frappato may be fermented under controlled temperatures to retain its aromatic character. It can be matured in stainless steel or in wood, depending on the desired style. Steel-aged examples may emphasise freshness and fruit, while wood can contribute additional texture and savoury or spicy impressions.

This is why two wines made from Frappato can share a family resemblance without tasting identical.

## How should you serve Frappato?

For a light, fresh example, try serving Frappato slightly cool—around 12–15°C is a useful starting point. If it seems too restrained straight from the refrigerator, allow it to warm in the glass.

You do not need specialist equipment. An ordinary red-wine glass is enough, and a light Frappato may be ready to enjoy without lengthy decanting.

Its freshness and moderate tannin make it a flexible food wine. Useful starting points include:

- tomato-based pasta or pizza;
- roasted peppers and other vegetables;
- poultry;
- lighter charcuterie;
- mushroom and herb dishes; and
- some grilled or roasted fish.

Pairing is not a set of rules. The preparation and intensity of the dish matter as much as the ingredient. A bright, lightly chilled Frappato may suit a delicate meal, while a fuller example can handle richer flavours.

## How might Australian drinkers encounter Frappato?

Australian readers may come across Frappato in imported Sicilian wines. The variety is also recognised in Australian wine-industry discussions of alternative grape varieties, although the available evidence does not establish broad Australian production, national importance or consistent retail availability.

Availability can vary between retailers and vintages. If you see “Frappato” on a label, expect a grape variety rather than a promise that every bottle will have exactly the same body, colour or flavour profile.

## Frappato FAQs

### Is Frappato a red wine or a grape?

Frappato is a grape variety. Wines made from it are commonly called Frappato wines.

### Is Frappato sweet?

Usually, Frappato is made as a dry red wine. Its red-fruit aromas can make it seem fruity, but fruitiness is not the same as sweetness.

### Is Frappato the same as Cerasuolo di Vittoria?

No. Frappato is a grape. Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG is a regulated blend that must include both Frappato and Nero d’Avola.

### Is Frappato a full-bodied wine?

Many Frappatos are light- to medium-bodied, fresh and relatively gentle in tannin. The finished style can be fuller depending on the site, ripeness, extraction, blending and maturation.

### Should Frappato be chilled?

A light Frappato can benefit from being served slightly cool, around 12–15°C. Avoid serving it ice-cold, which can mute its aroma and flavour.
