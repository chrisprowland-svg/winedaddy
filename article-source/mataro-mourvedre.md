# What is Mataro / Mourvèdre?

## Highlights

- **Mataro and Mourvèdre are names for the same red wine grape.**
- The grape is also known as **Monastrell**, particularly in Spain.
- In Australia, you may see **Mataro** or **Mourvèdre** on a label. The name alone does not identify a region or a fixed wine style.
- Mataro/Mourvèdre can contribute deep colour, tannin, structure, dark fruit, spice and savoury character.
- It is used in both varietal wines and blends, often alongside Grenache and Shiraz.
- It is a late-ripening grape that needs substantial heat and light to ripen fully.
- Climate, ripeness, blending and winemaking can make wines from the same grape taste quite different.

## Quick answer

Mataro and Mourvèdre are two names for the same red wine-grape variety. **Monastrell** is another established name for it.

Australian drinkers are likely to encounter **Mataro** on domestic labels, while **Mourvèdre** is also used in Australian industry, export and Rhône-influenced contexts. These names are naming choices, not different grape varieties. They do not, by themselves, tell you where the grapes were grown, whether the wine is a blend or what the wine will taste like.

Mataro/Mourvèdre is a late-ripening, heat-requiring variety. When it ripens well, it can make deeply coloured, structured wines with firm tannin. Flavours and aromas may include dark fruit, pepper, spice, herbs, leather or savoury notes. These are tendencies rather than guarantees: the region, vintage, harvest ripeness, blend and winemaking choices all matter.

The grape is important in blends as well as varietal wines. In Australia, it is part of the country’s warm-climate and Mediterranean-variety wine landscape, including blends with Grenache and Shiraz.

## Mataro, Mourvèdre and Monastrell: one grape, several names

The simplest way to read these names is:

- **Mataro** — a widely used Australian name
- **Mourvèdre** — the French name commonly used internationally
- **Monastrell** — the Spanish name

The choice of name can reflect regional history, market practice or producer preference. It should not be treated as a reliable shortcut for style. A wine labelled Mataro is not automatically more Australian in character than one labelled Mourvèdre, and a Mourvèdre label does not guarantee a French-style wine.

The grape name is also different from a regional name. For example, a regional indication on a bottle communicates where the grapes came from; **Mataro** or **Mourvèdre** tells you about the variety. One is not a substitute for the other.

## What does Mataro/Mourvèdre taste like?

Mataro/Mourvèdre is best understood through its structure as much as its flavours.

It can contribute:

- deep colour;
- firm tannin;
- body and structure;
- dark-fruit flavours such as blackberry or plum;
- pepper and other spice notes;
- herbal, earthy or savoury character.

With development, some examples may suggest leather, truffle or game-like notes. But not every bottle will show all of these descriptors. A cooler or earlier-picked wine may feel quite different from a ripe, concentrated example, and blending can change the balance further.

**Tannin** is the drying, gently grippy sensation that can make your mouth feel firmer after a sip. In a blend, Mataro may provide some of that grip and shape, helping a softer or more fruit-driven variety feel more structured.

## Why climate and ripeness matter

Mataro/Mourvèdre buds and ripens late. It needs a long period of heat and light to reach suitable ripeness, which helps explain its association with warm, Mediterranean-climate wine regions.

Ripeness matters because early-picked fruit may produce a lighter, less expressive wine, while suitably ripe fruit can provide more colour, flavour and structure. That does not mean every warm region produces the same result. Soil, water availability, site, crop level, harvest decisions and cellar techniques can all influence the finished wine.

This is why the grape name is a starting point, not a complete tasting prediction.

## Mataro in Australian wine

Wine Australia’s 2024 variety snapshot recorded **751 hectares planted** to Mataro/Mourvèdre and **6,516 tonnes crushed**. The largest listed shares of the 2024 crush came from:

- Riverland;
- Riverina;
- McLaren Vale;
- Limestone Coast zone; and
- Barossa Valley.

These are documented production associations for that year, not an exhaustive list of every Australian place where the grape is grown. The figures are also dated: production data can change.

Mataro is especially useful for understanding Australia’s interest in warm-climate and Mediterranean varieties. Australian examples may be made as varietal wines, where the grape takes centre stage, or as blends, where it contributes colour, tannin, structure and savoury complexity.

## How to read an Australian Mataro label

When you see **Mataro** or **Mourvèdre** on an Australian wine label, ask three simple questions:

1. **Is it a varietal wine or a blend?**  
   The grape may appear on its own or alongside varieties such as Grenache and Shiraz.

2. **Where was it made?**  
   The region can give useful context, but the grape is grown in more than one Australian area.

3. **What production choices shaped it?**  
   Ripeness, extraction, oak, fermentation and blending can all influence how much fruit, tannin or savoury character you notice.

A blend containing Mataro does not necessarily taste like a varietal Mataro. In a blend, the variety may be present mainly to add shape and depth rather than to dominate the aroma or flavour.

## Common questions

### Is Mataro the same as Mourvèdre?

Yes. Mataro and Mourvèdre are established names for the same grape variety. Monastrell is another name for it.

### Is Mataro always a full-bodied red?

No. It can make deeply coloured, structured wines, but the finished style depends on ripeness, region, winemaking and whether it is blended with other grapes.

### Is Mataro only used in blends?

No. It is made as both varietal wine and blended wine.

### Does “Mourvèdre” mean the wine is French?

No. The name is French, but the grape is grown and used in many wine-producing countries, including Australia.

### What food suits Mataro?

Its structure and savoury character can make it a natural match for hearty food, but there is no single compulsory pairing. The particular wine’s weight, tannin and freshness matter more than the grape name alone.
