# What Is Whole-Berry Fermentation? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Whole-berry fermentation means fermenting some or all grape berries while they are still intact and uncrushed.
- The berries may be destemmed first, so whole-berry fermentation does not necessarily include stems.
- It is a winemaking choice, not a guarantee of a particular flavour, quality level or “natural” style.
- Whole-bunch fermentation is different because the stems are retained.
- Whole-berry fermentation is not automatically carbonic maceration.
- The finished wine depends on fruit condition, temperature, yeast, oxygen, vessel and cap management.
- It may influence extraction, aroma, texture, freshness and tannin expression, but the results are not universal.

## Quick Answer: what is whole-berry fermentation?

Whole-berry fermentation is a winemaking process in which grape berries are kept intact and uncrushed during the beginning of fermentation, rather than being fully crushed before entering the fermenter.

The berries may be destemmed first. In that case, the stems are removed but the berries remain whole. Juice is released from berries that are damaged, pressed by the weight of fruit or gradually split during fermentation. Yeast then ferments that juice, while the remaining intact berries soften and become increasingly involved in the ferment.

This can change the timing and pattern of contact between juice, skins and seeds. Depending on the conditions, the wine may show fresh fruit character, distinctive aromas, a different texture or moderated early extraction. But whole-berry fermentation does not guarantee any one style. The result depends on the proportion of intact fruit and the winemaker’s broader process.

The method is used and studied in Australia, but it is not uniquely Australian. It is best understood as one variable in winemaking—not a promise that a wine is superior, more natural or lower intervention.

## What happens to the berries?

At the start, an intact berry holds its juice inside its skin. This is different from crushed fruit, where juice is immediately released and can contact the skins, seeds and other grape solids.

As fermentation progresses:

1. Juice from crushed or damaged berries begins to ferment.
2. Carbon dioxide is produced by fermentation.
3. Pressure, warmth and handling may cause intact berries to soften or split.
4. More juice is released.
5. Skins and seeds have increasing contact with the fermenting wine.
6. Cap management can break additional berries and influence extraction.

So “whole berry” usually describes the condition of the fruit at the start, or the intended handling method. It does not mean every berry will remain intact until pressing.

The process may also alter fermentation timing. In one Sangiovese study, yeast population development differed between whole-berry and crushed must, with the peak population occurring later in the whole-berry treatment. That supports the cautious wording “can alter fermentation kinetics”, rather than “always ferments slowly”.

For a broader introduction to the role of yeast and alcoholic fermentation, see [what is fermentation?](/what-is-fermentation/).

## Whole berry versus whole bunch

The simplest distinction is the stems.

- **Whole-berry fermentation:** berries are kept intact; stems may have been removed.
- **Whole-bunch fermentation:** intact bunches are fermented with stems retained, either partly or fully.

A whole-bunch ferment therefore contains whole berries at the beginning, but a destemmed whole-berry ferment does not contain retained stems. Stems can contribute their own tannin and aromatic compounds, so these techniques should not be treated as interchangeable.

Wine Australia’s Pinot Noir education material makes this distinction clearly, while Australian research has also examined how stem inclusion can affect tannin and aroma compounds. To explore that related process, read [what is whole-bunch fermentation?](/winemaking/whole-bunch.html).

## Is whole-berry fermentation the same as carbonic maceration?

No. The terms describe different things.

**Whole-berry fermentation** describes the condition of the fruit: some or all of the berries are intact and uncrushed.

**Carbonic maceration** describes a more specific environment. Intact fruit is held in an oxygen-depleted, carbon-dioxide-rich atmosphere, allowing intracellular activity inside some berries before or alongside conventional yeast fermentation in released juice.

Some whole-berry ferments may involve limited carbonic-like or intracellular activity, particularly where oxygen is restricted. That does not make every whole-berry ferment carbonic maceration. Intact berries alone are not enough.

**Semi-carbonic maceration** generally involves intact fruit alongside released or fermenting juice. The fermenting juice produces carbon dioxide, gradually creating more oxygen-poor conditions around some intact berries. The term is used somewhat differently across technical and trade sources, so the mechanism matters more than the label.

## How can it affect wine style?

Whole berries can delay the release of juice and reduce immediate contact between juice and seeds compared with fully crushed fruit. This may moderate early extraction, but the final result depends on fermentation temperature, time on skins, alcohol development, cap management and pressing.

Tannin is a good example of why simple claims can mislead. Whole-berry fermentation may change the pathway by which tannin is extracted, and Australian Pinot Noir research found that a whole-berry treatment had tannin composition more closely associated with skin tannin in the tested design. That does not mean every whole-berry wine has less tannin or a softer texture.

A winemaker may use the technique while seeking freshness, fragrance or prominent fruit character. Limited intracellular activity can contribute to distinctive aromas in some conditions. However, those sensory effects are more strongly associated with particular carbonic or partly carbonic processes than with every wine made using intact berries.

Colour is also difficult to predict. Variety, berry condition, temperature, skin contact and extraction time can outweigh the effect of berry integrity alone. Whole-berry fermentation does not automatically produce paler or darker wine.

## What factors matter?

The method is especially dependent on the condition of the fruit and the surrounding process.

- **Fruit health:** sound berries are important when the desired result depends on intact fruit and limited oxygen. Damaged or leaking berries release juice early.
- **Berry condition:** skin strength, seed maturity and ripeness affect how long berries remain intact and what becomes extractable.
- **Temperature:** temperature influences yeast activity, extraction and any intracellular activity.
- **Vessel and headspace:** an open ferment, a sealed vessel and a tank deliberately filled with carbon dioxide create different environments.
- **Yeast:** whole berries do not mean “wild fermentation”. Cultured yeast may be used, or fermentation may rely partly on native microorganisms.
- **Oxygen exposure:** intact fruit is not automatically fermenting anaerobically.
- **Cap management:** punch-downs and pump-overs affect how much fruit is wetted, broken or exposed to oxygen.

This is why two wines described as whole-berry fermented can taste quite different.

## How might you recognise it in a wine?

You cannot identify whole-berry fermentation with certainty from tasting alone. Depending on the fruit, proportion of intact berries and other winemaking choices, a wine may seem fresh, aromatic, fruit-driven, textured or less aggressively extracted. Another wine made with the same broad technique may show more structure or conventional fermentation character.

The useful question is not “Does this taste like whole berry?” but “How did preserving berry integrity influence this wine alongside everything else?”

## Australian context

Whole-berry fermentation is relevant to Australian red-wine production and is documented in Australian education and technical winemaking work. Wine Australia uses the term while distinguishing it from whole-bunch fermentation. The Australian Wine Research Institute has also documented trials involving 100% whole-berry Cabernet Sauvignon and work in which destemmed, crushed fruit still contained many intact berries.

These are examples of Australian practice and research, not evidence that all Australian wineries use the method in the same way. Whole-berry fermentation is not uniquely Australian, and it is not automatically linked to one region, grape variety or flavour profile.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does whole-berry fermentation include stems?

Not necessarily. Berries can be destemmed and left uncrushed. If stems are retained, the process is generally described as whole-bunch or whole-cluster fermentation.

### Does whole-berry fermentation use yeast?

Yes, it can. Whole-berry handling and yeast choice are separate decisions. A wine may use cultured yeast, native microorganisms or a combination of microbial activity.

### Is whole-berry wine always fruity or light?

No. Fruit character or a lighter-feeling texture may occur in some conditions, but the result also depends on grape variety, temperature, extraction, cap management and time on skins.

### Is whole-berry fermentation natural?

The term does not tell you whether a wine is natural, organic, spontaneous or low-intervention. It describes how the grapes were handled.

### Why would a winemaker use whole berries?

Preserving berry integrity can change how quickly juice is released and how juice, skins and seeds interact. A winemaker may use it to pursue a particular balance of aroma, freshness, texture or extraction.
