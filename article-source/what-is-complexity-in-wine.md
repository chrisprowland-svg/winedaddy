# What Is Complexity in Wine?

## Highlights

- Complexity is the impression that a wine offers several distinct, connected and sometimes evolving aromas, flavours and textures.
- It is not simply a long list of tasting-note descriptors.
- A wine can be intense but simple, or restrained but complex.
- Age, oak, price and concentration may contribute to complexity, but none guarantees it.
- Complexity is different from balance, length, quality and personal preference.
- You can look for complexity by smelling, tasting and revisiting a wine slowly.

## Quick Answer: What Does Complexity Mean in Wine?

Complexity in wine describes the range, interaction and development of the sensations a wine offers. A complex wine may show fruit, floral, herbal, spicy, savoury, earthy or developed characteristics, with these details appearing together or revealing themselves over time.

The key is not how many flavours you can name. Complexity is more about whether the wine offers several recognisable dimensions that feel connected and continue to interest you. One sip might begin with dark fruit, then reveal spice, savoury notes and a fine, lingering texture.

Complexity is a sensory impression, not a direct measurement of how many chemical compounds are in a wine. It is also partly subjective: your experience can be influenced by your expectations, vocabulary, tasting context and familiarity with wine.

A complex wine is not automatically better. It may be unbalanced, faulty or simply unlike your preferred style. Conversely, a straightforward wine can be clean, balanced and thoroughly enjoyable.

## How Can You Recognise Complexity?

Start by smelling the wine before swirling. What is the first clear impression? It might be citrus, red fruit, dark fruit, flowers or herbs.

Now swirl gently and smell again. Are there additional details, or has the original aroma simply become stronger? Complexity may involve a broader range of aromas rather than greater intensity alone.

Take a small sip and notice what happens across your palate. Do fruit, freshness, texture, warmth or savoury flavours appear at different points? Does the finish add another dimension? After a minute or two, taste the wine again. It may seem more open or show a different emphasis.

This is an observation framework, not a test with a correct number of answers. Not every complex wine changes dramatically in the glass, and a wine that changes quickly may be warming, oxidising or revealing a fault rather than becoming more complex.

Useful questions include:

- Does the wine offer more than one clear sensory dimension?
- Do the aromas and flavours develop or deepen?
- Are the details distinct but connected?
- Does the finish remain interesting?
- Can you recognise the same characteristics on a second sip?
- Is the wine genuinely layered, or simply dominated by one powerful flavour?

## Complexity Is Not the Same as Intensity

**Intensity** describes how strongly a characteristic is perceived. **Complexity** describes the range, relationship and development of the characteristics.

A wine with very powerful ripe-fruit aromas may be intense but straightforward. Another wine may be quieter yet reveal subtle floral, herbal, savoury and earthy details. It can be complex without being loud.

The same distinction applies to concentration. A concentrated wine can feel dense and full of flavour while still having a narrow flavour profile. Complexity does not require a wine to be full-bodied or powerful.

A helpful shortcut is:

> Intensity asks, “How strong is it?” Complexity asks, “What else is happening, and how do the different elements develop together?”

## Complexity, Balance and Finish

Complexity and balance can support each other, but they describe different things.

**Balance** concerns the relationship among a wine’s major components, such as fruit, acidity, sweetness, alcohol, tannin, body and texture. **Complexity** concerns the range and development of the wine’s aromas, flavours and sensations.

A complex wine can still feel unbalanced if alcohol, acidity, tannin or oak dominates. A simple wine can be well balanced and enjoyable without offering many layers.

A long **finish**—the sensations that remain after swallowing or spitting—may add to a wine’s complexity. But length alone is not complexity. A single flavour that lingers for a long time is still only one main dimension.

## Does Oak Create Complexity?

Oak can add aromas and flavours such as vanilla, toast, cedar, baking spice or smoke. It can also contribute to texture and maturation.

However, obvious oak flavour is not automatically complexity. Oak may add a convincing extra layer when it fits with the wine’s fruit, freshness, structure and development. If it dominates, it can hide other details.

An unoaked wine can be complex, and a heavily oaked wine can be simple. The question is whether the oak is integrated into a broader, coherent impression.

## Does Age Make Wine More Complex?

Bottle age can add or emphasise developed characteristics such as dried fruit, leather, tobacco, earth, nuts, dried flowers or savoury notes. This is one way a wine may gain new layers over time.

But an old wine is not automatically complex. Storage conditions matter, and some wines are made to be enjoyed young. A mature wine can also be tired, oxidised or faulty. Young wine is not automatically simple either: it may already show several integrated aromas, flavours and structural sensations.

Age is therefore a possible contributor, not a guarantee.

## What Contributes to Complexity?

Complexity can be influenced by many parts of a wine’s journey, including:

- grape variety;
- site, climate and vintage;
- fruit ripeness;
- blending;
- yeast and fermentation;
- malolactic fermentation;
- skin or stem contact;
- lees contact;
- oak;
- maturation;
- bottle age;
- serving temperature and aeration.

These factors create opportunities for different characteristics to develop, but they do not work like a recipe. The result depends on the wine, the producer’s choices, the vintage and how the wine is served.

Fermentation and maturation can add dimensions beyond fresh grape fruit. A blend may combine different components. Time in bottle may introduce developed characters. None of these automatically makes a wine complex.

## Australian Examples

In Australia, “complex” is generally used as a descriptive tasting or review term. It is not a regulated quality classification or mandatory label category.

A young Australian Shiraz might show dark fruit, spice, floral notes and firm structure. With suitable development, another Shiraz may gain savoury, earthy, dried-fruit, leather or tobacco-like characters. These are possible examples, not a fixed profile for every Australian Shiraz.

Australian Cabernet Sauvignon may combine dark fruit with leafy, herbal, cedar, spicy or savoury notes, depending on its site, vintage and winemaking. Chardonnay may bring together citrus or stone fruit, lees, toast, texture and bottle development. Australian fortified wines can show layered fruit, nutty, oxidative, spicy and spirit-related characters within their own styles.

None of these varieties or styles is inherently complex. Producer, vineyard site, vintage, winemaking and age all matter.

## Can a Simple Wine Still Be Good?

Absolutely. Complexity is only one possible quality attribute, and it is not a guarantee that you will enjoy a wine.

A wine may be complex but unbalanced. It may be technically impressive but made in a style you dislike. A simpler wine may be fresh, balanced, clean and exactly what you want with dinner.

Try to separate three questions:

1. What can I smell and taste?
2. How do the elements relate to one another?
3. Do I enjoy this wine?

There is no requirement to prefer complexity. Understanding the term simply makes tasting notes and wine conversations easier to interpret.

## A Simple Way to Taste for Complexity

The next time you open a bottle:

1. Smell before swirling and note the obvious first impression.
2. Swirl gently and look for additional aromas, not just a stronger version of the first one.
3. Take a small sip and notice flavour, freshness, texture and warmth.
4. Pay attention to the middle of the palate and the finish.
5. Wait a minute or two, then taste again.
6. Write down what you notice before deciding whether you like it.
7. Ask whether the characteristics are distinct but connected.

You do not need specialist vocabulary. “The fruit is joined by spice and a savoury note” is more useful than forcing yourself to identify an exact aroma.

Complexity is not a competition to name the most flavours. It is the experience of discovering range, connection and development in the glass.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is complexity the same as having many flavours?

No. Many flavours may contribute to complexity, but they need to be recognisable and meaningfully connected. A long or imaginative list of descriptors does not prove that a wine is complex.

### Are complex wines always expensive?

No. Price does not guarantee complexity, and complexity does not establish a wine’s price or quality.

### Are older wines more complex?

Not necessarily. Age can add developed layers to some wines, but storage, style and the wine’s condition matter. Young wines can also be complex.

### Does a complex wine have to be full-bodied?

No. Complexity can appear in a lighter-bodied or restrained wine. Power and weight are not requirements.

### Can a wine be complex but not enjoyable?

Yes. Complexity is different from personal preference. A wine can offer many connected dimensions while still being a style you do not like.
