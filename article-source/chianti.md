# Chianti: A Beginner’s Guide to the Tuscan Wine

## Highlights

- Chianti is a protected wine designation from Tuscany, Italy—not a grape variety.
- It comes from a legally defined area covering parts of six Tuscan provinces.
- Sangiovese is the defining grape, but Chianti is not always made from 100% Sangiovese.
- Chianti and Chianti Classico are separate protected designations.
- Chianti can be fresh, savoury and slightly tannic, but the name does not guarantee one fixed style.
- Terms such as Riserva, Superiore, Rosé and a named subzone provide additional information on the label.
- Chianti is often a useful match for tomato-based pasta, pizza, roast vegetables and grilled meats—but pairing is a starting point, not a rule.

## Quick answer: what is Chianti?

Chianti is a protected Tuscan wine designation, recognised in Europe as a Protected Designation of Origin (PDO, also called DOP) and in Italy as a DOCG. DOCG stands for *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*: an Italian classification for wines made under defined origin and production rules.

The Chianti production area lies in central Tuscany and covers parts of the provinces of Arezzo, Florence, Prato, Pistoia, Pisa and Siena. That means Chianti is broader than the group of picturesque central-Tuscany towns most commonly associated with the name.

The wine is principally associated with Sangiovese. Under the current specification, ordinary red Chianti categories other than Rosé must contain between 60 and 100 per cent Sangiovese. Other permitted Tuscan varieties may complete the blend under the relevant category rules.

For an Australian wine buyer, the simplest way to read “Chianti” is as a regulated place name. It tells you more about origin and production requirements than it does about one guaranteed flavour profile, body, producer, vintage or price.

## Where is Chianti?

Chianti is in Tuscany, in central Italy. Florence is a useful orientation point for the northern and central parts of the wider area, while Siena helps orient the southern and central Tuscan landscape. Arezzo is another important reference to the east.

Towns such as Greve in Chianti, Radda in Chianti, Castellina in Chianti and Gaiole in Chianti are strongly associated with the Chianti landscape. However, town names are not a complete definition of the Chianti DOCG area. The legal designation extends across parts of six provinces, including areas well beyond that familiar central cluster.

This is a useful distinction when looking at a map or a bottle: an informal landscape association and a legal wine boundary are not always the same thing.

## What does Chianti taste like?

Chianti is commonly associated with dry, savoury or lively red wines with noticeable freshness and some tannin. Tannin is the drying sensation that can make your gums feel slightly grippy. Acidity provides lift and freshness, helping a wine feel energetic rather than flat.

Official descriptions support a slightly tannic, dry and harmonious style. Young wines may show ruby colour, red-fruit or violet-like aromas and a fresh character. With age, some wines can become softer and more velvety.

These are broad tendencies, not promises. Chianti includes different subzones, categories, vintages and winemaking choices. One bottle may be relatively fresh and approachable, while another may be more concentrated or developed. The name alone does not mean that every Chianti is light-bodied, rustic, high-acid, inexpensive or intended for immediate drinking.

If you are learning to taste, try describing the wine in three simple parts:

1. **Freshness:** does the acidity make your mouth water?
2. **Grip:** do the tannins feel soft or firm?
3. **Weight:** does the wine feel light, medium or fuller-bodied?

## Chianti and Chianti Classico: what is the difference?

Chianti and Chianti Classico are related, but they are not interchangeable.

**Chianti** is the wider DOCG designation, covering parts of Arezzo, Florence, Prato, Pistoia, Pisa and Siena.

**Chianti Classico** is a separate PDO/DOCG designation from a distinct, legally delimited historic area in the provinces of Florence and Siena. It has its own production specification and categories, including Chianti Classico, Riserva and Gran Selezione.

Chianti Classico is not simply a premium label or an alternative spelling for Chianti. Nor is the wider Chianti designation merely a sub-brand of Chianti Classico. The two names identify different protected areas and rules.

## What do the label terms mean?

The current Chianti specification recognises several categories and geographic subzones.

- **Chianti:** the standard designation, with or without a named subzone.
- **Chianti Riserva:** a regulated ageing category. Under the current specification, it requires a minimum two-year ageing period calculated from 1 January after the harvest.
- **Chianti Superiore:** a separate regulated category with distinct production requirements, including stricter yield and analytical parameters. “Superiore” should not be read simply as an unrestricted guarantee of better quality.
- **Chianti Rosé:** a recognised rosé category with its own requirements.
- **Named subzone:** a more specific geographic indication within the Chianti designation.

The current specification lists eight subzones: Colli Aretini, Colli Fiorentini, Colli Senesi, Colline Pisane, Montalbano, Montespertoli, Rufina and Terre di Vinci.

This list matters because older summaries may mention only seven subzones. Terre di Vinci is included in the current 2026 specification. Regulatory information should therefore be checked periodically.

## What should Australian drinkers expect?

An Australian buyer can reasonably infer that a qualifying bottle labelled Chianti is a regulated Tuscan wine designation and that Sangiovese plays a central role in its production rules.

You cannot infer from “Chianti” alone:

- a precise town, vineyard or producer;
- that the wine comes from the Chianti Classico area;
- that it is 100 per cent Sangiovese;
- a guaranteed body, flavour profile or ageing potential;
- a particular price, vintage or level of quality.

The current rules also matter when reading older wine information. Some older sources state that Chianti must contain at least 70 per cent Sangiovese. The current Italian specification uses a 60–100 per cent range for the relevant red categories, so the newer official rule should take precedence.

## What food works with Chianti?

Chianti’s dry structure and freshness make it a practical starting point for savoury foods. Try it with tomato-based pasta, pizza, roast vegetables, bean-based dishes or grilled meats.

Acidity can work particularly well with tomato-based foods, while tannin often feels more comfortable alongside savoury dishes and protein. But there is no single correct pairing. The preparation, seasoning and intensity of the food matter just as much as the wine.

## The takeaway

Chianti is best understood as a place-based Tuscan wine designation built around Sangiovese. It has a wider legal area than the familiar central-Tuscany landscape, and it includes several categories and subzones.

Most importantly, Chianti is not the same as Chianti Classico. When you see either name on a bottle in Australia, you are seeing a protected geographic designation—but neither name, on its own, tells you everything about how the wine will taste.

**Suggested FAQs**

### Is Chianti a grape?

No. Chianti is a protected wine designation from Tuscany. Sangiovese is the principal grape associated with it.

### Is Chianti the same as Chianti Classico?

No. They are separate PDO/DOCG designations with different legally defined areas and production specifications.

### Is Chianti always made from Sangiovese?

Sangiovese is required under the current rules for the relevant red categories, at between 60 and 100 per cent. Chianti is not necessarily 100 per cent Sangiovese.

### Does Riserva mean the wine is better?

Riserva is a regulated ageing category, not an automatic guarantee that every bottle will be better suited to your taste.
