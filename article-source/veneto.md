# Veneto: a beginner’s guide to the wines of north-eastern Italy

## Highlights

- Veneto is a broad wine region in north-eastern Italy, not one single wine style.
- Its landscape runs from the Alps and foothills through hills and plains to Lake Garda and the Adriatic.
- Prosecco, Soave and Valpolicella are important names within Veneto’s wider wine geography.
- Glera is central to Prosecco, Garganega is strongly associated with Soave, and Corvina is central to Valpolicella.
- Amarone and Recioto both use dried grapes, but Amarone is dry-style while Recioto is sweet.
- Italian appellation names often matter as much as, or more than, grape names on the label.
- Prosecco DOC is not exclusively from Veneto: it also includes parts of Friuli-Venezia Giulia.

## Quick answer: what should an Australian wine learner understand about Veneto?

Veneto is one of Italy’s major wine regions. It sits in the country’s north-east and includes a remarkably varied landscape: Alpine and foothill areas, hills, broad plains, Lake Garda and the Adriatic coastline.

That variety helps explain why “Veneto wine” does not describe one consistent taste. The region is better understood through its individual appellations—regulated geographical wine names—and the grapes and methods associated with them.

Three useful starting points are:

- **Prosecco**, a sparkling wine designation mainly associated with the grape **Glera**.
- **Soave**, a white-wine appellation strongly associated with **Garganega**.
- **Valpolicella**, a family of red-wine designations associated with **Corvina** and other local varieties.

Veneto also helps explain an important Italian wine idea: one appellation can contain several different styles. Valpolicella may refer to Valpolicella DOC, Valpolicella Ripasso DOC, Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG or Recioto della Valpolicella DOCG. They are related, but they are not interchangeable wines.

## Where is Veneto?

Veneto is an administrative region of Italy in the country’s north-east. Venice is its regional capital and the most familiar broad orientation point for many Australian readers. Verona is a useful wine-centre reference, particularly for Soave and Valpolicella. Treviso is another useful name when thinking about eastern Veneto and Prosecco.

Veneto is approximately 120–130 kilometres west of Venice, with a drive commonly taking around 1¼–1¾ hours to Verona, depending on the route, traffic and starting point. Treat that as general orientation, not a precise travel promise. Veneto is large enough that the same journey does not represent its Alpine, eastern or coastal wine areas.

A map showing Veneto alongside the Alps, Lake Garda, the Adriatic, Venice, Verona and Treviso is particularly useful here. It also helps distinguish the broad administrative region from individual wine appellations.

The names on a bottle may not match the name of a town. Soave can refer to a town, a wider wine area or a regulated designation. Valpolicella refers to a wine area and appellation family rather than a grape variety. Prosecco DOC crosses the regional boundary into Friuli-Venezia Giulia, so it is a Veneto association but not an exclusively Veneto designation.

## Why Veneto is not one wine style

Veneto’s mountains, hills, plains, lake-influenced areas and coastal landscapes create different growing environments. These physical differences can help explain variation between its wines, but they do not guarantee a particular flavour in every bottle.

The more useful question is not “What does Veneto taste like?” but “Which Veneto appellation and style is this?”

That shift is helpful when reading Italian labels. A bottle labelled Soave is giving you a place-and-appellation clue. A bottle labelled Amarone della Valpolicella is giving you a specific appellation and production-style clue. The grape may be important, but it may not be the largest word on the label.

## Prosecco and Glera

Glera is the principal grape of Prosecco DOC. Under the cited Prosecco DOC summary, it must make up at least 85 per cent of the wine, with permitted complementary varieties accounting for the remainder.

Prosecco DOC covers five Veneto provinces and four in Friuli-Venezia Giulia. This is one reason not to treat every famous wine name as a simple synonym for one Italian region.

Prosecco labels also contain a useful trap for Australian drinkers. Within the cited Prosecco DOC categories, **Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut**. The wording can therefore be misleading if you assume “Extra Dry” means the driest option.

Look for both the appellation and the sweetness term. Prosecco may also be sparkling, frizzante or still depending on the permitted style, so the name alone does not tell you everything about the wine in the glass.

## Soave and Garganega

Soave is a white-wine appellation in the Verona area and is strongly associated with Garganega. The region also includes related designations such as Soave Superiore DOCG and Recioto di Soave DOCG.

For a beginner, the important point is that **Soave is a place and appellation name, not the name of a grape**. Garganega is the key grape association, but the appellation should not be treated as a guarantee of one exact flavour profile.

Terms such as *Classico* and *Superiore* can appear in Italian appellations, but their detailed meanings are specific to the relevant denomination. They should not be translated into a universal quality ranking.

## Valpolicella, Corvina and its red wines

Valpolicella is associated with Corvina, alongside Rondinella, Corvinone and other permitted local varieties. It is also a good example of why one appellation family can contain several styles.

The principal names include:

- **Valpolicella DOC**
- **Valpolicella Ripasso DOC**
- **Amarone della Valpolicella DOCG**
- **Recioto della Valpolicella DOCG**

These names are related, but they do not describe the same wine. A Valpolicella label may point towards a different style from an Amarone or a Recioto label.

## Appassimento, Amarone and Recioto

**Appassimento** is a production method involving the drying or partial dehydration of grapes before vinification. It is not a synonym for sweetness, and it is not another name for Amarone.

Amarone della Valpolicella and Recioto della Valpolicella both use dried grapes, but their finished styles differ. Amarone is the dry-style red expression, while Recioto is sweet because fermentation is stopped to retain sweetness.

That makes Recioto a useful reminder that drying grapes does not automatically produce one predictable result. The production method and the final wine style are related, but they are not identical concepts.

## DOC, DOCG and IGT: what do they mean in Australia?

Italian wine labels use terms such as **DOC**, **DOCG** and **IGT** within the broader European geographical-indication framework.

- **DOC** means *Denominazione di Origine Controllata*, or controlled designation of origin.
- **DOCG** means *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*.
- **IGT** means *Indicazione Geografica Tipica*, a traditional Italian geographical-indication term.

For an Australian learner, these terms are broadly comparable in purpose to geographical indications: they connect wine with a defined place and production rules. They are not exact one-to-one equivalents of Australian GI categories, which operate under Australian legislation and labelling practice.

DOCG should not automatically be read as “better”. These terms communicate legal origin and production requirements; they are not a universal guarantee of quality or personal preference.

## How to read a Veneto label

When choosing or discussing a Veneto wine:

1. **Look for the appellation name first.** Soave, Valpolicella and Amarone are place-and-style clues.
2. **Treat the grape as a supporting clue.** Glera, Garganega and Corvina are important, but Italian labels do not always foreground varieties in the way Australian labels often do.
3. **Separate method from sweetness.** Appassimento means grape drying; it does not automatically mean sweet wine.
4. **Read Prosecco sweetness terms carefully.** Extra Dry is sweeter than Brut in the cited categories.
5. **Do not assume all Veneto wines taste alike.** The region contains sparkling, white, red and sweet styles.
6. **Remember that a town, region and appellation are different things.** A familiar place name does not necessarily define the full legal production area.

Veneto becomes much easier to understand once you stop treating it as one style and start reading its appellation names as a map of its wine diversity.
