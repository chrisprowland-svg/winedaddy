# What Is Amphora and Clay Vessels? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- An amphora is a vessel used to ferment, mature or store wine.
- Amphorae are traditionally associated with fired clay or ceramic.
- “Clay vessel” is the broader term; not every clay vessel is an amphora.
- Clay does not automatically make wine earthy, mineral, natural or better.
- Some vessels may allow oxygen transmission or buffer temperature, but their behaviour depends on design and construction.
- Amphora can be used with red, white or other wine styles, with or without skin contact.
- The grape, fermentation, oxygen exposure, time and cellar management may matter as much as the vessel.

## Quick answer: what is amphora in winemaking?

An amphora is a clay or ceramic vessel used in winemaking for fermentation, skin contact, maturation or storage. The term comes from ancient vessel traditions, but modern wine amphorae can have different shapes, sizes, wall thicknesses and closures.

A **clay vessel** is the broader category. Amphora is usually a particular vessel form or a modern winemaking term for a clay-based or ceramic wine vessel. Related vessels such as Georgian qvevri, Portuguese talha and Spanish tinaja have their own shapes and traditions, so they should not automatically be treated as synonyms.

The important point is that amphora describes a **vessel**, not a wine style or a certification. A wine made in amphora is not automatically natural, organic, biodynamic, unfiltered or superior.

## How are clay wine vessels made?

Clay is shaped and then fired to create a ceramic vessel. The firing process affects the final material. Lower-fired earthenware may retain more open structure, while higher-fired ceramic can be denser and less permeable.

Some vessels are unglazed. Others may be glazed, lined or coated with materials such as wax or pitch. These treatments can change how much wine contacts the ceramic, how much liquid the vessel absorbs, how it is cleaned and how gases move through its walls.

That is why “clay is porous” is too broad a statement. Two vessels that are both described as clay may behave differently because of their clay body, firing temperature, wall thickness, coating, closure and maintenance.

## What do winemakers use amphora for?

### Fermentation

Grape juice can ferment in a clay or ceramic vessel. The vessel may be used for red, white or other wines, depending on the winemaker’s method.

Fermentation itself is the process in which yeast converts grape sugars into alcohol and other compounds. The amphora is the container in which that process takes place, not the process itself. You can learn more in [What is fermentation?](/what-is-fermentation/).

### Skin contact and maceration

Grape skins, seeds and sometimes stems may remain in contact with the juice or wine inside the vessel. This can affect colour, tannin and texture, but it is a separate winemaking decision.

An amphora does not automatically make an orange wine or a tannic wine. Those outcomes depend on the grape, the length and conditions of skin contact, extraction and other cellar choices.

### Maturation and storage

Wine may stay in an amphora after fermentation, or be stored in one for a period of time. Fill level, closure, temperature, time and oxygen exposure all matter.

A winemaker may also move wine between amphora, stainless steel, oak or other vessels. The final wine is usually the result of several decisions rather than one container alone.

## Can clay change the wine?

Sometimes—but not in one predictable way.

Some earthenware vessels may allow oxygen transmission through their walls. The amount varies with the material, firing, moisture, thickness, coatings and closure. Oxygen exposure can influence the wine’s development, but it must be managed alongside headspace, temperature, topping-up, time and hygiene.

Some clay vessels may also provide a degree of thermal buffering or insulation. This does not mean they maintain a constant fermentation temperature. Vessel size, cellar conditions and whether the vessel is buried or actively temperature-controlled also matter.

Clay and ceramic generally do not provide the wood-derived aromas and tannins associated with oak. That does not make them completely neutral: the vessel’s construction, coating and interaction with the wine can still influence the result. Research has found differences in some wines matured or fermented in earthenware or ceramic vessels, but these findings do not establish a universal “amphora flavour”.

So descriptions such as “amphora always tastes earthy” or “clay gives wine a mineral taste” go beyond the evidence. The wine’s variety, ripeness, yeast, fermentation, skin contact, oxygen exposure and maturation may be equally important.

## Amphora compared with other wine vessels

- **Oak:** A wooden vessel that can contribute oak-derived aromas and tannins, as well as oxygen exposure. See [What does oak do to wine?](/what-does-oak-do-to-wine/).
- **Stainless steel:** Generally a non-porous vessel chosen for hygiene, temperature control and limited vessel-derived flavour. See [What is stainless-steel winemaking?](/stainless-steel-winemaking/).
- **Concrete:** A separate mineral-based material. It is not clay or ceramic, even when a concrete vessel has a similar egg or rounded shape. See [What are concrete eggs and tanks?](/concrete-eggs-and-tanks/).
- **Qvevri:** A Georgian clay vessel traditionally buried in the ground. It is related to amphora traditions but is not simply another name for every amphora.

These comparisons are starting points, not guarantees. Different vessels vary considerably within each category.

## Amphora in Australia

Australian winemakers do use amphora and other clay vessels, but the available examples support describing the practice as documented and specialist rather than widespread across the national industry.

Wine Australia material has used McLaren Vale to illustrate amphora fermentation. Australian reporting has also documented clay-vessel work in places including Mudgee and the Yarra Valley. Tasmania has examples involving qvevri, which is useful because it shows why related clay-vessel terms should be kept distinct.

The practical lesson for an Australian wine learner is simple: if a label or tasting note mentions amphora, ask what the vessel was used for and for how long. “Made in amphora” does not tell you whether the wine was fermented, matured, exposed to skins or blended with wine from another vessel.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does amphora mean natural wine?

No. Amphora is a vessel choice. “Natural wine” is a separate and contested term involving broader production decisions, and amphora does not prove that a wine belongs in that category.

### Is amphora the same as clay?

Not exactly. Clay vessel is the broader description. Amphora usually refers to a vessel form or modern wine vessel made from clay-based ceramic.

### Does amphora make wine taste earthy?

Not necessarily. There is no single amphora taste. Sensory effects depend on the vessel, its lining and firing, the wine and the winemaking process.

### Is amphora better than oak or stainless steel?

No vessel is automatically better. Each offers different practical characteristics, and the best choice depends on the wine and the winemaker’s aim.

### Is qvevri an amphora?

Qvevri is a traditional Georgian clay vessel with its own design and use. It is related to amphorae, but the terms should not be treated as exact synonyms.

## The takeaway

Amphora is best understood as a winemaking vessel, not a promise about flavour or quality. Clay and ceramic vessels can be used for fermentation, skin contact, maturation and storage. Their effects depend on construction, firing, lining, closure and cellar management—as well as on everything else happening to the wine.
