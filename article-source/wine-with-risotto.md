# Wine with risotto: how to choose the right style

## Highlights

- There is no single best wine for every risotto.
- Start with the finished dish: its richness, salt, umami, main ingredients and finishing touches.
- A fresh, dry white wine or dry sparkling wine is the safest broad starting point.
- Seafood and delicate vegetable risottos usually suit bright, low-tannin wines.
- Mushroom, truffle, cheese-rich and meat-based risottos can handle more texture and savoury depth.
- Australian Chardonnay, Riesling, Pinot Gris/Pinot Grigio, sparkling wine and Pinot Noir can all work—but their styles vary.

## Quick answer

For a broadly defined risotto, begin with a **fresh, dry, moderate-bodied white wine** or a **dry sparkling wine**. Australian Riesling, fresh Pinot Gris or Pinot Grigio, unoaked or lightly oaked Chardonnay, and crisp Australian sparkling wine are useful starting points.

Why? Risotto is usually soft, starchy and rich, often with butter, stock, cheese or another savoury finishing ingredient. A wine with useful acidity brings freshness and contrast, while moderate body keeps the pairing from feeling either thin or heavy.

The recipe still matters more than the word *risotto*. A delicate seafood or lemon-finished version may suit a bright Riesling. Mushroom, truffle or Parmesan risotto may be better with a more textured Chardonnay. A rich mushroom-and-sausage version can even work with a fresh, light-bodied Pinot Noir.

Think of the wine as matching the **finished dish**, not simply the rice.

## Match the wine to the risotto

### Seafood risotto

Seafood risotto generally favours a fresh, dry white with little or no tannin. Tannin is the drying sensation most associated with red wine, and it can make a delicate seafood dish feel harder or less harmonious.

Look for:

- dry Australian Riesling;
- fresh Pinot Gris or Pinot Grigio;
- restrained, bright Chardonnay;
- dry sparkling wine when the dish is salty or savoury.

A lemony, herb-led seafood risotto usually calls for freshness. If the dish is heavily buttered, cheese-rich or made with a concentrated stock, a wine with more texture may work. The seafood itself is only part of the picture.

### Mushroom or truffle risotto

Mushrooms bring earthy, savoury flavours, while Parmesan can add salt and richness. These versions can support a fuller, more textured white than a delicate vegetable risotto.

Try a Chardonnay with restrained oak or lees-derived texture, or a fuller dry Riesling. Oak is not compulsory: it is simply one possible source of toast, spice and texture. Heavy oak can dominate a lighter mushroom preparation.

A fresh, light-bodied Pinot Noir is another conditional option, particularly when mushrooms are joined by meat or other strongly savoury ingredients. Choose freshness and moderate tannin rather than a large, heavily structured red.

### Vegetable and pumpkin risotto

Vegetable risotto can range from bright and herbaceous to sweet, roasted and rich.

Green vegetables, fresh herbs and lemon favour a lively dry white such as Riesling, Pinot Gris/Pinot Grigio or unoaked Chardonnay. Roasted pumpkin or other sweet vegetables may suit a wine with a little more fruit weight and body, but pumpkin does not automatically require a sweet wine.

Butter, cheese, chilli, spice and roasting often matter more than the vegetable’s name.

### Cheese-rich risotto

Parmesan and other aged cheeses add salt, umami and savoury depth. A dry sparkling wine can be a particularly useful choice here, especially when the dish is rich or has a crisp garnish. Fresh, textured white wines can also work well.

Do not assume that cheese automatically means red wine. A light red may suit a cheese-rich risotto when it also contains mushrooms or meat, but a fresh white or sparkling wine is usually the more adaptable starting point.

### Meat-based risotto

Chicken, sausage, beef, game or marrow can make risotto more intense and rich. In these cases, move towards a fuller white or a fresh, light- to medium-bodied red, depending on the stock, sauce, herbs and finishing ingredients.

A fresh Australian Pinot Noir can suit an earthy mushroom-and-meat risotto. A richer Chardonnay may work when the dish is buttery, creamy or cheese-forward. More tannic or high-alcohol reds are better reserved for genuinely robust preparations; they can overwhelm the soft texture of a typical risotto.

## The wine qualities that matter

### Acidity

Acidity is the mouth-watering quality that makes wine feel fresh. It provides useful contrast to risotto’s soft, rich texture. This is why fresh whites and sparkling wines are such dependable starting points.

### Body

Body means the wine’s perceived weight and fullness. A light, delicate risotto can be overshadowed by a full-bodied wine. A dish with butter, truffle, aged cheese or rich meat may make a very light wine seem insubstantial.

### Tannin

Tannin creates a drying, grippy sensation. Keep it restrained when the risotto is delicate, seafood-based or very soft in texture. Fresh Pinot Noir is more suitable than a strongly tannic red when a red pairing makes sense.

### Oak

Oak can contribute toast, spice, vanilla and texture. A restrained-oak Chardonnay may complement mushrooms, browned flavours, truffle or aged cheese. It is not a requirement for creamy risotto, and a heavily oaked wine may overpower seafood or fresh vegetables.

### Sweetness

Dry wine is the normal starting point for savoury risotto. If the dish contains noticeably sweet roasted vegetables or chilli, a wine with more fruit weight may be worth trying. That does not mean the wine must be sweet.

## What to look for on an Australian label

Australian varieties are not single, uniform styles.

**Chardonnay** can be light, fresh and vibrant, or fuller and more textured. Unoaked or lightly oaked examples are useful for lighter risottos; richer styles may suit mushroom, cheese or truffle versions.

**Riesling** is often light-bodied with vivid acidity and citrus or lime character. It is a natural style to consider with seafood, spring vegetables, herbs and lemon.

**Pinot Gris and Pinot Grigio** are names for the same grape. In broad terms, Pinot Grigio may suggest a fresher, more acid-driven style, while Pinot Gris may suggest a riper or fuller wine—but the winemaker and region can produce considerable variation.

**Sparkling wine** can range from light and crisp to complex and full-bodied. Choose by dryness, freshness and body rather than assuming every sparkling wine will taste the same.

**Pinot Noir** can provide a fragrant, lighter-bodied red option for earthy mushroom or meat-based risotto. Look for freshness and moderate tannin.

Region and variety can provide clues, but neither guarantees a particular flavour. If the label offers useful style information, words such as *dry*, *fresh*, *unoaked*, *textured* or *full-bodied* can help you make a more informed choice.

## A simple selection method

Before buying or opening a bottle, ask:

1. **What is the strongest flavour?** Seafood, mushroom, pumpkin, cheese, meat, herbs or lemon?
2. **How rich is the dish?** Is it lightly finished, or heavy with butter, cream, cheese or rich stock?
3. **How intense is it?** Match a delicate dish with a lighter wine and a deeply savoury dish with more texture.
4. **Do I want contrast or harmony?** Fresh acidity creates contrast; a textured Chardonnay can echo a richer, creamy texture.
5. **Would tannin help or distract?** For most risotto, especially seafood, restraint is safer.

If you are serving several different risottos, a fresh dry sparkling wine or versatile dry white is usually more adaptable than a heavily oaked white or tannic red.

Serve fresh whites and sparkling wines properly chilled, without treating one exact temperature as essential. If serving a red, avoid letting it become warm enough for alcohol to dominate. Most importantly, judge the whole plate: the stock, garnish and finishing ingredients can change the pairing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Chardonnay the best wine with risotto?

Not universally. Chardonnay ranges from fresh and unoaked to rich and oaked. A bright style may suit seafood or vegetables, while a more textured style can work with mushrooms, truffle or cheese.

### Can you drink red wine with risotto?

Yes, in selected cases. Fresh, light-bodied Pinot Noir may suit mushroom, sausage or meat-based risotto. Red wine is less likely to be a comfortable default with delicate seafood or vegetable versions.

### Is sparkling wine good with risotto?

It can be. Dry sparkling wine brings freshness and can work with salty, savoury, mushroom or cheese-rich dishes. Choose a style with enough body for the recipe.

### What wine goes with mushroom risotto?

Start with a textured dry white, such as a restrained-oak Chardonnay or fuller dry Riesling. A fresh Pinot Noir can also work when the mushrooms are joined by meat or other earthy flavours.

### What wine goes with seafood risotto?

A fresh, dry, low-tannin white is the safest direction. Consider Australian Riesling, fresh Pinot Gris/Pinot Grigio or restrained Chardonnay, adjusting for butter, cheese and stock.

### Does creamy risotto require oaked Chardonnay?

No. Creaminess describes the dish’s texture, not a compulsory wine style. Acidity, freshness and appropriate body matter more than oak.
