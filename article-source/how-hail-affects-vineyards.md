# How hail affects vineyards

## Highlights

- Hail is frozen precipitation that can injure vines through direct impact.
- Damage may affect leaves, shoots, flowers, bunches, berries, trunks, cordons and trellis structures.
- The outcome depends on hailstone size, wind, storm severity, vineyard exposure and the vine’s growth stage.
- Hail can reduce the current crop, but severe damage may also affect disease risk, ripening and the following season.
- Hailstorms are often localised and patchy, so damage in one vineyard does not automatically define an entire region’s vintage.
- Hail is different from frost, rain, wind and smoke exposure, although one storm can create several risks at once.

## Quick answer

Hail affects vineyards by striking exposed vine tissue and grape bunches with hard, fast-falling ice. It can strip leaves, break shoots, damage flowers, bruise or split berries, scar woody parts of the vine and damage posts or wires.

The consequences depend on more than the fact that hail occurred. Large stones, strong wind, a severe storm and exposed bunches can increase the damage. Timing matters too. Hail near flowering or fruit set can remove flowers and young fruit, while hail close to harvest can split berries and create wounds that are more vulnerable to rot.

A vineyard may therefore face immediate crop loss as well as longer-term effects on canopy recovery, vine structure, ripening or the following season’s production. But hail damage is not automatically a regional disaster. Australian viticulture guidance describes hailstorms as often short, localised and uneven, so one affected block may not represent every vineyard in the surrounding wine region.

## What does hail damage on a vine?

A grapevine has several parts that matter when hail strikes:

- **The canopy:** leaves and green shoots that capture sunlight and support ripening.
- **Flowers and bunches:** the structures that become the grape crop.
- **Berries:** the individual grapes, which can be bruised, punctured or split.
- **Trunks, cordons and canes:** woody parts that provide the vine’s permanent or seasonal framework.
- **Trellis components:** posts, wires and other supports that hold the vine in position.

Hail does not damage all these parts equally. A storm might strip leaves while leaving much of the crop intact, or it might pass through a block with enough force to break shoots and destroy bunches. That difference matters because a vine with fruit but little functioning canopy faces a different challenge from a vine with healthy leaves but widespread berry damage.

## Why timing matters

The same hailstorm can have different consequences depending on the vine’s growth stage.

### Around budburst and flowering

Young green shoots and leaves are relatively exposed. Hail can break shoots or remove flowers and inflorescences. If flowers or newly forming fruit are lost, the vine may have fewer bunches or berries to carry through to harvest.

### Around fruit set

Fruit set is when fertilised flowers begin developing into young berries. Hail at this point can remove young fruit or damage bunches before their crop potential is established.

### During berry development and ripening

Later in the season, hail can bruise or split berries. Damaged fruit may mature differently from sound fruit, and open wounds can become more vulnerable when wet or humid weather follows. This can create both a quantity problem and a quality-management problem.

### After harvest

Leaves and shoots still matter after the grapes have been picked. Severe damage may reduce the vine’s ability to build reserves before dormancy, potentially affecting vine condition and the next season. Recovery varies, so a damaged vineyard is not guaranteed to return to normal immediately.

## What happens after a hailstorm?

Growers generally need to understand how unevenly the storm affected the vineyard. Hail can be patchy even within one property, so the first step is to assess individual blocks rather than assume the whole vineyard suffered equally.

They may look at:

- how much leaf area remains;
- whether shoots are bruised or broken;
- how many flowers, bunches and berries are still sound;
- whether trunks, cordons or canes have been scarred;
- whether posts, wires or other trellis parts are damaged; and
- what growth stage the vines had reached when the storm arrived.

The remaining weather also matters. Hail wounds can expose fruit and plant tissue. Australian Wine Research Institute guidance identifies Botrytis risk after hail, particularly when wet or humid conditions follow. Injuries to woody parts can also create possible entry points for some vine diseases.

This does not mean every wound will become infected. It means the condition of the vineyard after the storm, including the weather that follows, helps determine the eventual outcome.

## Can a vineyard recover?

Often, vines can regrow after hail, particularly when the damage is early enough in the season and the permanent woody framework remains in reasonable condition. But regrowth does not guarantee a normal crop.

Recovery involves two connected questions: what can happen to the current crop, and how well can the vine prepare for the next one? A decision that supports future vine structure may not produce the same result as one focused on the remaining harvest.

Severe leaf loss can also reduce the canopy available to support fruit ripening. The AWRI reports that significant leaf loss around flowering has been associated with increased primary bud necrosis in the following season and an approximately 25 per cent reduction in the next crop. That is a source-specific observation, not a universal forecast for every hail event.

## Does hail ruin a wine region’s vintage?

Not necessarily. Hail can cause serious losses in an affected vineyard, but storms are often localised and patchy. One block may be badly damaged while another nearby receives little or no hail.

The final effect on wine also depends on how much fruit was lost, how much sound fruit remained, when the storm occurred and how the affected fruit was handled. Hail-damaged grapes do not automatically mean that every bottle from the region will be poor. It is more accurate to say that hail can create crop, ripening and fruit-selection challenges in the vineyards it affects.

This distinction matters when reading Australian vintage news. A report of hail in a wine region does not necessarily describe every vineyard, grower or wine from that region.

## Hail compared with other vineyard hazards

Hail is a mechanical-impact hazard: falling ice physically strikes the vine.

- **Frost** injures buds, shoots or flowers through freezing temperatures.
- **Rain** can contribute to fruit cracking or disease conditions but does not usually create the same impact wounds.
- **Wind** can break shoots, rub fruit or damage trellis structures.
- **Smoke exposure** is a separate fire-related risk involving smoke compounds and possible sensory effects in wine.

These hazards can occur together. For example, hail may split berries and rain afterwards may make conditions more favourable to rot. The mechanisms are still different, so “storm damage” does not always mean the same thing as hail damage.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are hail and frost the same thing?

No. Hail is frozen precipitation that causes physical impact. Frost is cold injury caused by freezing conditions. Both can damage a vineyard, but they affect vine tissue in different ways.

### Can hail damage the vine as well as the grapes?

Yes. Hail can damage leaves, shoots, flowers, bunches, berries, trunks, cordons and canes. It can also damage trellis components such as posts and wires.

### Does hail always destroy the crop?

No. Damage ranges from minor berry injury to severe crop loss. Hailstone size, wind, storm path, canopy protection, vine growth stage and vineyard exposure all influence the result.

### Does hail automatically make the wine faulty?

No. Hail may create damaged fruit, uneven ripening or disease risks in affected blocks, but it does not automatically determine the quality of every wine from a vineyard or region.

### Why can hail affect the following vintage?

Severe damage may reduce leaf area, injure woody structures or affect the vine’s preparation for the next season. The extent varies with the event, vine condition and subsequent recovery.
