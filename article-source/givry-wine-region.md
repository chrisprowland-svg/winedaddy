# Givry wine region

## Highlights

Givry is a French wine appellation in Burgundy’s Côte Chalonnaise. It produces still red and white wines from legally delimited vineyard parcels in the communes of Givry, Dracy-le-Fort and Jambles, in Saône-et-Loire.

For an Australian wine learner, the key idea is simple: **Givry is a regulated place name, not a grape variety**. Red Givry is based on Pinot Noir, while white Givry is made from Chardonnay and Pinot Blanc. The appellation also includes wines labelled with a named *climat* and the regulated mention “premier cru”.

Givry can be a useful way to understand how French wine labels connect a bottle to a specific place. However, the name does not promise one uniform flavour. Vineyard site, producer, vintage and winemaking still influence the wine in your glass.

## Where is Givry?

Givry sits in eastern France, within the Burgundy wine region and the Côte Chalonnaise.

A useful hierarchy is:

**France → Burgundy → Côte Chalonnaise → Givry**

The Côte Chalonnaise lies south of the Côte de Beaune and north of the Mâconnais. Givry is a few kilometres south-west of Chalon-sur-Saône, the nearest useful regional centre. From Dijon, a practical orientation is roughly 74 kilometres and about 52 minutes by car, although the actual time depends on the route, traffic and starting point.

Givry is also the name of a commune, but the **Givry appellation is not simply the village**. Its legal area includes parts of three communes:

- Givry
- Dracy-le-Fort
- Jambles

That does not mean every vineyard parcel in those communes qualifies for the appellation. The qualifying vineyard land is specifically delimited under the appellation rules.

Givry is separate from neighbouring Côte Chalonnaise appellations such as Mercurey, Rully, Montagny and Bouzeron. Those names identify their own regulated places; they are not subdivisions of Givry.

## What does “Givry” mean on a wine label?

“Givry” on a French bottle label identifies a regulated appellation of origin. It tells you that the wine comes from the defined Givry area and follows the relevant production rules.

You may see:

- **Givry** for the appellation;
- **Givry premier cru** when the wine comes from a qualifying named *climat* and can carry the regulated mention;
- a named **climat**, meaning a recognised vineyard site used with the appellation where the legal conditions are met.

A *premier cru* is not a producer rating, wine score or guarantee that every bottle will taste superior. It is a regulated vineyard tier within the Givry appellation. The wine can still vary according to its site, vintage and maker.

This is different from seeing a grape name such as Pinot Noir or Chardonnay on a label. In Givry, the place name is doing much of the labelling work.

## Which grapes and wines does Givry produce?

Givry’s appellation covers **still red and still white wines**. The approved evidence does not establish a Givry rosé, sparkling or sweet-wine category.

### Red Givry

Red Givry is based on **Pinot Noir**. The cited appellation specification also permits Chardonnay as an accessory grape in a limited proportion within the vineyard parcel.

That means it is safer to say that red Givry is Pinot Noir-based than to assume every bottle is necessarily 100 per cent Pinot Noir.

### White Givry

White Givry is made from:

- Chardonnay
- Pinot Blanc

Chardonnay is the principal variety most Australian drinkers are likely to recognise. For a more general explanation of the grape, see [what is Chardonnay?](/what-is-chardonnay/).

## What does Givry wine taste like?

Regional descriptions provide a useful starting point, but they are not a tasting guarantee.

### Red Givry

Red Givry may show bright red to darker fruit, floral notes such as violet, and savoury or spicy characters. Strawberry and blackberry are among the fruit descriptions associated with the appellation. Young examples can have noticeable tannin—a drying sensation on the gums—and a firm, balanced structure.

That does not mean every red Givry is heavy or rustic. A particular bottle may feel more delicate, fruit-driven, savoury or structured depending on its vineyard position, producer and vintage.

### White Givry

White Givry may show citrus, floral and lightly honeyed characters, with a balance between softness and acidity. Some regional descriptions also mention toasted or dried-fruit notes developing with age.

Think of these descriptions as signposts rather than rules. They help you know what to look for, but they should not replace your own judgement when tasting the wine.

## How do climate and soils shape Givry?

Givry has a continental climate, with hot summers and dry autumns. Its vineyards occupy varied hillside positions and elevations. Sources describe elevations from roughly 200 to 420 metres across the broader appellation context, while an INAO geographic page gives a narrower range of approximately 250 to 370 metres for the area it describes.

The soils are influenced by Jurassic limestone and include calcareous, clay-limestone and marl-rich material. Upper slopes can contain harder limestone, while lower slopes may have deeper soils with more marl. Elevation, aspect and slope position can therefore vary from one vineyard site to another.

This helps explain why two bottles carrying the same appellation name may not taste identical. Place matters, but it works alongside vintage conditions and human decisions. For a broader introduction, see [how climate affects wine](/how-climate-affects-wine/).

## Givry AOC/AOP compared with an Australian GI

French wine terminology can feel unfamiliar because it combines geography with detailed appellation rules.

**AOC** means *Appellation d’origine contrôlée*, or controlled designation of origin. **AOP** means *Appellation d’origine protégée*, the protected-origin framework used at European level. Givry is recognised as a French AOC appellation and may be discussed within the AOP framework.

An Australian **Geographic Indication**, or GI, also identifies where wine comes from. But an Australian GI and a French AOC/AOP are not interchangeable systems. They do not necessarily use the same hierarchy or impose identical rules about grapes, production and labelling.

You can learn more in [French AOC and AOP](/french-aoc-and-aop/) and [Australian Geographic Indications](/australian-geographic-indications/).

## The main thing to remember

Givry is a specific Burgundy appellation within the Côte Chalonnaise. Its name points to a legally defined place, with Pinot Noir-based reds, Chardonnay and Pinot Blanc whites, and a village-to-premier-cru vineyard structure.

When you see Givry on a bottle, start with the place—but leave room for the individual vineyard, vintage and winemaker to shape the final wine.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Givry a grape variety?

No. Givry is a French wine appellation and place name. Its red wines are based on Pinot Noir, while its white wines use Chardonnay and Pinot Blanc.

### Is Givry part of Burgundy?

Yes. Givry is a specific appellation within the Côte Chalonnaise, which sits within Burgundy’s broader wine geography.

### Is Givry the same as Côte Chalonnaise?

No. Côte Chalonnaise is the broader wine area. Givry is one specific appellation within it.

### Does Givry make red and white wine?

Yes. The appellation covers still red and still white wines.

### What does Givry premier cru mean?

It refers to Givry from a qualifying named vineyard site, or *climat*, that can carry the regulated “premier cru” mention. It is not a producer score or an absolute guarantee of quality.
