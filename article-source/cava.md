# What Is Cava? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

Cava is a Spanish quality sparkling wine protected by a European Union Protected Designation of Origin, or PDO. It is made under rules covering where it can be produced, which grapes may be used, how it is made, how long it is aged and what can appear on the label.

Cava is generally made using the traditional method. That means the wine undergoes a second fermentation in the bottle in which it will eventually be sold, then rests on its lees—spent yeast sediment—for at least nine months in the youngest category.

Cava is not a grape variety, and it is not a generic word for Spanish sparkling wine or Australian bottle-fermented sparkling wine.

## The highlights

- Cava is a protected Spanish designation, not simply a style name.
- It is usually made by the traditional method, with the second fermentation taking place in the final bottle.
- Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada are the classic white-grape trio, but nine grape varieties are authorised.
- Cava can be white or rosé, fresh and citrusy or more developed and toasty.
- Reserva and Gran Reserva describe ageing categories; Brut and Brut Nature describe sweetness.
- Cava is associated with Catalonia, but its legal production area extends across several parts of Spain.
- It shares a method with Champagne but is governed by different geographical and production rules.
- On an Australian label, look for the Cava designation, sweetness term, ageing category, alcohol information and standard drinks.

## How is Cava made?

Cava begins as still wine made from permitted grapes. The winemaker then adds a mixture of wine, yeast and fermentable sugar, known as tirage liqueur, before sealing the bottle.

A second fermentation takes place inside that bottle. The yeast converts sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide; because the bottle is sealed, the carbon dioxide dissolves into the wine and creates its bubbles.

The wine then rests on its lees. This period can add texture and flavours that may remind you of toast, pastry, yeast or roasted nuts. Younger Cava may instead feel fresher, fruitier and more citrus-driven.

Before release, the sediment is moved towards the neck of the bottle in a process called riddling. Disgorgement removes that sediment. A small addition called dosage, or expedition liqueur, may then replace lost liquid and establish the final sweetness. Brut Nature is the basic exception in this explanation.

The method matters, but it is only one part of Cava’s identity. Other sparkling wines can also use the traditional method. Only wine that meets the Cava designation’s rules can use the protected name.

For a broader explanation of the method, see [traditional-method sparkling wine](/traditional-method-sparkling-wine/).

## Where does Cava come from?

Cava’s historical centre is Penedès in Catalonia, particularly the area around Sant Sadurní d’Anoia. That makes the town an important point of reference, but it is not the whole legal Cava area.

The designation also recognises other Spanish production areas. Official Cava material groups them into four broad zones:

- Comtats de Barcelona
- Valle del Ebro
- Altos de Levante
- Viñedos de Almendralejo

This is why “Cava comes from Catalonia” is too narrow. Catalonia is central to Cava’s history and identity, but the protected designation is broader. For detailed zones, sub-zones and localities, see the separate guide to [Cava wine regions](/cava-wine-regions/).

## Which grapes are used?

The traditional white blend is associated with three Spanish varieties:

- **Macabeo**, often linked with freshness and fruit
- **Xarel·lo**, which can contribute structure and personality
- **Parellada**, often associated with fragrance and delicacy

Cava’s rules also authorise Chardonnay, Subirat Parent or Malvasía, Garnacha, Trepat, Monastrell and Pinot Noir. That does not mean every bottle contains all nine varieties.

The authorised red varieties help explain why Cava can also be rosé. Its final character depends on the grape mix, harvest conditions, dosage, ageing and winemaking choices.

## What do Cava ageing categories mean?

Ageing and sweetness are separate systems. One label term can tell you about time in bottle, while another tells you how much residual sugar remains.

| Category | Minimum ageing | What it tells you |
|---|---:|---|
| **Cava de Guarda** | 9 months | The youngest current ageing category |
| **Reserva** | 18 months | More time on lees and potentially more developed texture |
| **Gran Reserva** | 30 months | Extended ageing; restricted to Brut Nature, Extra Brut and Brut types |
| **Cava de Paraje Calificado** | 36 months | A recognised site category with extended ageing and additional requirements |

Longer ageing can bring more developed aromas and a creamier or more textured feel. It does not automatically mean that one Cava will suit you better than another.

## What do Brut and Brut Nature mean?

These terms describe sweetness, not quality.

| Label term | Residual-sugar range | Beginner meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| **Brut Nature** | 0–3 g/L | Very dry |
| **Extra Brut** | 0–6 g/L | Very dry |
| **Brut** | Up to 12 g/L | Dry |
| **Extra Seco** | 12–17 g/L | Slightly sweeter |
| **Seco** | 17–32 g/L | Sweeter than Brut |
| **Semi Seco** | 32–50 g/L | Medium-sweet |
| **Dulce** | Above 50 g/L | Sweet |

A common misunderstanding is that Brut means sugar-free. It does not. Also, fruitiness is not the same as sweetness: a wine can smell fruity while still tasting dry.

The terms on imported labels may vary slightly in spelling or translation, so keeping the Spanish wording in mind is useful.

## How does Cava compare with Champagne and Prosecco?

Cava and Champagne share the traditional method, including second fermentation in the final bottle. They are not the same wine, however. Champagne is a separate protected French designation with its own place, grapes and production rules. Cava is governed by the Spanish Cava PDO.

Prosecco is also a separate protected designation, from Italy. It is commonly associated with tank-method production rather than second fermentation in the final bottle, although comparisons should not be reduced to a single slogan.

Crémant refers to several protected regional sparkling-wine designations, generally made by the traditional method outside Champagne. Australian producers can also make bottle-fermented sparkling wine without it being Cava.

The useful question is not which name is “best”. It is: what place, grapes, method, ageing and sweetness rules sit behind the name?

## How should Australians read a Cava label?

You may encounter Cava in an Australian bottle shop, restaurant, Spanish-food venue or imported-wine list. Start with these details:

1. **The name:** Cava identifies a protected Spanish designation.
2. **The sweetness:** Look for Brut Nature, Extra Brut, Brut, Extra Seco, Seco, Semi Seco or Dulce.
3. **The ageing category:** Check for Cava de Guarda, Reserva, Gran Reserva or Cava de Paraje Calificado.
4. **The colour:** Cava may be white or rosé.
5. **Australian alcohol information:** Check the alcohol percentage and standard-drinks statement required for the Australian market.
6. **Country-of-origin information:** This is Australian consumer-labelling information, separate from the Cava PDO.

Australian Geographic Indications are a separate domestic geographical naming system. They do not govern Cava. A bottle-fermented Australian sparkling wine may use a similar method, but it cannot use “Cava” as a generic Australian term.

## How should Cava be served?

Serve Cava well chilled and open it safely. A tulip-shaped sparkling-wine glass can help show more aroma, while a flute places more emphasis on the visual stream of bubbles.

Cava’s acidity and bubbles can make it a useful partner for oysters, seafood, fried foods, tapas-style dishes and some cheeses. These are starting points rather than rules. The sweetness level and the intensity of the food still matter.

For safe opening guidance, see [how to open sparkling wine safely](/how-to-open-sparkling-wine-safely/). You can also explore [wine and seafood pairing](/wine-and-seafood-pairing/) and [wine and cheese pairing](/wine-and-cheese-pairing/).

As with any alcoholic drink, enjoy Cava responsibly and follow Australian drinking guidance.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Cava the same as Champagne?

No. They share the traditional method, but Cava and Champagne are different protected designations with different places, grapes and rules.

### Is Cava always made from Macabeo, Xarel·lo and Parellada?

No. Those are the classic white varieties, but nine varieties are authorised and not every Cava uses the traditional trio.

### Is Cava sweet?

It can be dry, off-dry or sweet. Brut Nature, Extra Brut and Brut are dry categories, while Semi Seco and Dulce are sweeter.

### Does Reserva mean sweeter?

No. Reserva describes ageing, not sweetness. A separate term such as Brut or Extra Brut describes the sugar level.

### Is Cava only made in Catalonia?

No. Catalonia is its best-known historical centre, but the Cava designation includes other legally recognised Spanish areas.

### Can Australian sparkling wine be called Cava?

No. Cava is a protected Spanish designation. Australian sparkling wine can be made by a similar method but is not Cava.
