# How long does opened red wine last?

## Highlights

- Most opened still red wine is best within **three to four days** if it is resealed and refrigerated.
- Some fuller, more tannic reds may remain enjoyable for **five to six days**.
- Delicate, lighter-bodied reds can lose freshness sooner.
- Refrigeration slows quality decline, even for red wine. It does not reverse oxidation.
- Keep the bottle upright and sealed, and minimise the air space if only a little wine remains.
- These are **quality guidelines, not guaranteed safety deadlines**.

## Quick answer

Opened red wine will often remain enjoyable for around **three to four days** when you replace the closure promptly and store the bottle in the refrigerator. A fuller, more tannic red may still be appealing after **five or six days**, while a lighter or more delicate red may decline sooner.

There is no single lifespan for every bottle. The wine’s style, how much remains, the amount of air in the bottle, the closure, temperature and how it has been handled all make a difference.

The practical approach is simple: reseal the bottle after pouring, keep it upright and refrigerate it. When you want to drink it, let it move back towards a suitable serving temperature, then assess it by sight, smell and taste.

## Why opened red wine changes

Opening a bottle allows oxygen to contact the wine. Over time, that exposure can change its aromas, flavours and colour. Fresh fruit may become less expressive, and the wine can taste flatter or less lively.

This process is often described as oxidation. It is gradual rather than an instant change, which is why a bottle can be pleasant one evening and noticeably tired a day or two later.

The amount of wine left matters too. A half-full bottle has more air space above the wine than a nearly full bottle, so it may decline more quickly, all else being equal. Warm conditions can also accelerate quality changes.

## How to store opened red wine

### 1. Reseal it promptly

Replace the cork, screwcap or another suitable wine closure as soon as you have finished pouring. Resealing limits further exposure, although it does not create an oxygen-free bottle.

### 2. Keep it upright

Store the bottle standing up rather than on its side. This reduces the wine’s exposed surface area inside the bottle.

### 3. Refrigerate it

Yes, you can refrigerate opened red wine. Cooler storage slows the changes associated with oxygen exposure, so it is generally more useful for preserving the wine than leaving it in a warm room.

This is especially sensible in an Australian summer or whenever your kitchen, dining area or storage cupboard becomes warm. Avoid direct sun, hot cars and other heated locations. There is no separate Australian number of days that applies to every bottle, but warmth is not helpful when you are trying to preserve quality.

Refrigeration slows oxidation; it does not undo changes that have already happened.

### 4. Reduce the air space if practical

If only a small amount remains, you can carefully transfer it to a clean, smaller bottle with a secure closure. Less empty space above the wine means less air available in the bottle.

This is optional rather than essential. Promptly resealing and refrigerating the original bottle will usually be the most convenient approach.

### 5. Bring it towards drinking temperature

A refrigerated red may taste muted simply because it is cold. Let it warm towards a suitable serving temperature before deciding whether it is still enjoyable. There is no universal warming time: room temperature, bottle size and wine style all vary.

## How to tell whether it is past its best

Pour a small amount and use your senses:

- **Look:** the colour may appear browner or more tawny than it was originally.
- **Smell:** the fruit may seem muted, stale or less fresh.
- **Taste:** the wine may be flat, tired, more sour or show balsamic or vinegar-like notes.

These are signs of quality decline, not a precise test or a fixed timetable. A wine can be past its best without every bottle showing the same symptoms.

If the wine appears contaminated, has an unusual film or growth, or otherwise seems abnormal, discard it. Do not treat “three to four days” as a universal safety guarantee. The available guidance is primarily about how long wine remains enjoyable, not a promise that a bottle is safe until a particular day.

## Do all red wines last the same time?

No. The three-to-four-day guide is for ordinary opened still red wine, particularly dry table wine.

Lighter and more delicate reds may lose freshness sooner. Fuller, more tannic reds may remain appealing for longer, but tannin does not guarantee a longer life.

Sparkling, sweet and fortified wines should not be judged by the same range. Sparkling wine’s condition depends heavily on how well it retains its bubbles, while sweet and fortified wines have different compositions and storage patterns. They need their own guidance.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should opened red wine go in the fridge?

Usually, yes. Refrigeration slows quality decline, including in red wine. Let it warm towards a suitable serving temperature before drinking.

### Does refrigeration reverse oxidation?

No. It slows further change but cannot restore freshness that the wine has already lost.

### Is opened red wine unsafe after four days?

Not necessarily. Four days is a practical quality guide, not a universal safety deadline. Judge the wine’s condition, and discard it if it appears contaminated or otherwise abnormal.

### Does a screwcap make opened wine last longer?

A secure closure helps limit further exposure, but it does not guarantee a particular number of days. Temperature, air space and wine style still matter.

### Can I leave opened red wine on the kitchen bench?

It may decline more quickly in a warm room. Reseal it and refrigerate it instead, particularly in hot weather.

## The simple rule

If you expect to drink the wine again, **reseal it promptly, store it upright in the fridge and try it within three to four days**. Some robust reds may give you longer, but the bottle itself—not the calendar—is the final guide to quality.
