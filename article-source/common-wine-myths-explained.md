# Common wine myths explained

Wine advice often arrives as a firm rule: expensive wine is better, red wine belongs at room temperature, or a screw cap means the bottle is cheap.

Most of these claims contain a small observation that has been stretched too far. Wine depends on style, production method, storage, serving conditions and personal preference. Here are seven common myths worth putting into perspective.

## Highlights

- Price can reflect scarcity, labour, ageing or reputation, but it cannot guarantee that you will enjoy a wine more.
- Most wine is made to be enjoyed relatively young; age is not automatically an advantage.
- Screw caps are an established part of Australian wine culture and do not signal poor quality.
- “Room temperature” traditionally meant a cool cellar, not necessarily a warm Australian living room.
- Sulphites affect some sensitive people, but they are not a safe general explanation for most wine reactions.
- Dryness describes sweetness, not fruit flavour.
- Sediment is often harmless and does not automatically mean a wine is spoiled.

## Myth 1: Expensive wine is always better

**Verdict: false.**

A higher price may reflect scarce fruit, labour, production costs, time in oak or bottle, packaging, distribution, reputation or demand. Those factors can contribute to a wine’s value, but price is not a universal quality scale.

Quality and enjoyment are also different questions. A wine may be carefully made, complex and ageworthy, yet not suit your taste. A less expensive wine can be well made and enjoyable without offering the same concentration or ageing potential.

A useful question is: **better in what way, and for whom?** Price may tell you something about the market around a wine, but it cannot tell you exactly how it will taste to you.

## Myth 2: Older wine is always better

**Verdict: false.**

Most wine is intended to be enjoyed relatively young. Some wines benefit from bottle ageing, while others are designed to show fresh fruit and brightness soon after release.

Ageing can replace youthful fruit characters with more developed aromas and flavours. That change may be fascinating, but “different” is not automatically “better”. A wine needs suitable balance and structure to develop well, and it must be stored in appropriate conditions.

Australia has examples of wines that can mature for many years. Well-balanced Australian Riesling, for instance, can develop from fresh citrus and floral characters towards richer, honeyed and nutty notes. That is an example of ageing potential, not a rule that every old bottle will be superior.

## Myth 3: Screw caps mean inferior wine

**Verdict: false.**

A screw cap is a closure, not a quality rating. It can provide a consistent seal and reduce the risk of cork taint. Its design also affects how much oxygen reaches the wine, which can influence how the wine develops over time.

Australia helped make screw caps a familiar sight, particularly on Riesling. Their widespread use does not mean every screw-capped wine is the same, nor that screw caps are always preferable to cork. Different closures involve different technical and practical choices.

The sensible conclusion is simple: judge the wine, not the stereotype attached to its closure.

## Myth 4: Red wine belongs at room temperature and white wine must be ice-cold

**Verdict: misleading.**

“Room temperature” is old serving advice that often assumes a cool European cellar. It does not necessarily suit a warm Australian indoor room, especially in summer. Served too warm, red wine can seem more alcoholic and less fresh.

The opposite extreme can also be unhelpful. Very cold white wine may seem refreshing, but excessive chilling can mute aroma and texture. Lighter, fresher styles generally benefit from being cooler, while fuller-bodied wines are usually better served less cold.

Instead of following a rigid rule, aim for balance: cool enough to feel fresh, but not so cold that the wine’s aromas disappear. If a bottle seems too warm, a short period in the refrigerator can help. If it seems too cold, let it sit briefly before judging it.

## Myth 5: Sulphites cause most wine headaches or reactions

**Verdict: misleading and individual.**

Sulphites, including sulphur dioxide, are used in winemaking to help protect wine from oxidation and unwanted microbial activity. Some sulphite-sensitive people, particularly some people with asthma, can experience reactions.

That does not make “sulphites cause most wine headaches” a safe general rule. Wine contains alcohol and many other compounds, and people can experience symptoms for different reasons. A label declaration also does not prove what caused an individual reaction.

Do not treat “no added sulphites” as meaning sulphite-free, or as a guarantee that a wine will prevent symptoms. If you have a recurring or concerning reaction, speak with a qualified health professional rather than trying to diagnose the cause from a label.

Australian wine labels and standard-drink information provide useful facts about a product, but they are not a personal medical assessment.

## Myth 6: A dry wine has no fruit flavour

**Verdict: false.**

In wine language, **dry** describes sweetness. A dry wine has no residual sugar, or so little that it is not detected as sweet. It does not mean the wine is flavourless, austere or lacking fruit character.

A dry wine can smell and taste of citrus, apple, pear, peach, berries or plums. These descriptions refer to aroma and flavour, not necessarily to sugar remaining in the wine.

This is why a dry Riesling, dry rosé or dry Shiraz can still taste strongly fruity. “Fruity” and “sweet” are related in everyday conversation, but they are not the same thing in wine tasting.

## Myth 7: Sediment means the wine is spoiled

**Verdict: false.**

Sediment is material that has settled in the bottle. It can develop as some wines age, particularly wines that have been lightly filtered, and it does not automatically indicate a problem.

Some deposits are tartrate crystals: harmless crystals formed from naturally occurring components of wine. They can look alarming, but they are not the same as spoilage.

Heavy sediment may affect texture, which is why a wine can be poured carefully or decanted away from the deposit. That is different from a wine showing obvious off-aromas or unpleasant flavours. Sediment alone is not proof of spoilage, but it also should not be used to dismiss clear signs that a wine is faulty.

## How to test a wine claim

When someone gives you a wine rule, ask four questions:

1. **Which wine does this apply to?** A light white, full-bodied red and sweet sparkling wine may behave differently.
2. **What condition matters?** Storage, temperature, closure, age and serving method can change the result.
3. **Is this a technical definition or a preference?** “Dry” has a specific meaning; “better” often depends on the drinker.
4. **Is the claim universal?** Words such as “always”, “never” and “must” deserve particular caution.

Wine becomes easier to understand when rules become starting points rather than laws. Taste, compare and stay curious—but let the evidence and the wine itself have the final say.

## Suggested questions

### Is expensive wine worth it?

Sometimes a higher price reflects extra labour, scarcity, ageing or complexity. It does not guarantee that the wine will be better for every drinker.

### Should I avoid screw-cap wine?

No. Screw caps are an established wine closure, including in Australia. The closure alone does not determine quality.

### Does dry wine mean low flavour?

No. Dryness refers to sweetness. A dry wine can have intense fruit aromas and flavours.

### Is sediment dangerous?

Sediment is often harmless, including tartrate crystals. It is separate from obvious spoilage such as unpleasant aromas or flavours.
