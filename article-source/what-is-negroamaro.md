# What is Negroamaro?

Negroamaro is a dark-skinned *Vitis vinifera* wine-grape variety, strongly associated with Puglia in southern Italy, particularly the Salento area. It is used for dry red and rosé wines, either on its own or in blends. A typical red Negroamaro may be deeply coloured, generously flavoured and structured, with dark fruit, herbal or earthy notes. But those are tendencies, not guarantees: the finished wine also reflects its vineyard, ripeness, winemaking, ageing and blending.

## Highlights

- Negroamaro is a grape variety, not a region or appellation.
- It is most closely associated with Puglia and Salento in southern Italy.
- Its wines can be red, rosé, sparkling or lightly sparkling.
- Red examples often show dark fruit, savoury or herbal flavours and noticeable structure.
- Ripe fruit flavour does not necessarily mean the wine is sweet.
- You may see the names **Negroamaro**, **Negro Amaro** or, in technical references, **Nero Amaro**.
- Australia grows and makes Negroamaro, although it remains a minor variety here.

## What does Negroamaro taste like?

Negroamaro is commonly associated with dark red or purple colour and flavours such as black cherry, plum and blackberry. Depending on the wine, you may also notice dried herbs, earth, olive-like savouriness or a slightly bitter edge.

In a concentrated red, the wine may feel medium- to full-bodied, with noticeable tannin and a warming level of alcohol. Tannin is the drying, gently grippy sensation you can feel on your gums. However, not every Negroamaro wine is heavy or powerful. Rosé versions and lighter red wines can feel fresher and less weighty.

The variety is generally used for dry wine, but a wine that smells of ripe fruit is not automatically sweet. Dryness refers to the amount of residual sugar left in the finished wine; fruit flavour is a separate part of the wine’s aroma and taste.

## Why can two Negroamaro wines taste different?

A grape variety provides a starting point, not a complete recipe. Climate and vineyard site influence how quickly the grapes ripen and how much flavour and acidity they retain. Harvesting earlier or later can also change the balance between freshness, fruit richness and alcohol.

Winemaking adds another layer. Longer skin contact and extraction can produce deeper colour and firmer tannin. Oak may add flavour and texture, while ageing can soften some youthful roughness and develop more savoury characters. A winemaker may also blend Negroamaro with other varieties to adjust the wine’s structure, aroma or freshness.

That is why it is more useful to think of Negroamaro as having a family resemblance than a fixed personality. Dark fruit and structure are common reference points, but the bottle in front of you may be fresh and bright, rich and powerful, or somewhere between the two.

## Where does Negroamaro come from?

Negroamaro is most strongly associated with Puglia, the long southern Italian region forming the heel of Italy’s boot. Within Puglia, Salento is its best-known geographic association. The provinces of Brindisi, Lecce and Taranto are included in the production area for some Negroamaro-based protected designations.

This does not mean that every Negroamaro wine comes from Salento. **Puglia** and **Salento** are place names; **Negroamaro** is the name of the grape.

That distinction matters when reading a label. A wine might identify the grape, a geographical indication, a protected designation, or a combination of these. Those terms do not all describe the same thing.

## Negroamaro and protected designations

**Negroamaro di Terra d’Otranto** is an example of a protected regional wine designation. The designation covers red and rosé categories, including some sparkling and lightly sparkling rosé styles, from parts of Brindisi, Lecce and Taranto.

For the described red and rosé categories, the designation requires at least 90 per cent Negroamaro, with limited use of other permitted black grapes. That is a rule for that named designation—not a rule for every wine labelled Negroamaro.

Other southern Italian appellations have their own requirements. For example, the cited Copertino DOC rules make Negroamaro the principal grape, with the variety representing at least 70 per cent of the blend under the described rules. Companion varieties may be permitted within specified limits.

The practical lesson is simple: a grape name tells you what variety is involved, while an appellation tells you that geographical and production rules apply. Neither one alone guarantees that every bottle will taste identical.

## What do the different spellings mean?

English-language wine writing commonly uses **Negroamaro** as one word. **Negro Amaro** is recorded as the official Italian variety name in the Italian Vitis Database and is also used in university and industry references. **Nero Amaro** is recorded as another synonym in the Italian database.

For an Australian shopper, these names can therefore point to the same grape variety. The spelling may vary between labels, technical documents and wine references. It is still worth reading the rest of the label: the wine could also state its region, geographical indication or whether it is a blend.

Negroamaro should not be confused with Primitivo, Malvasia Nera or Nero d’Avola. Those are separate grape varieties, even though some may be grown in the same broad part of Italy or used in related blends.

## Is Negroamaro grown in Australia?

Yes. Wine Australia’s 2021 National Vintage Report listed Negroamaro among the red varieties reported by Australian wineries. The report also indicates that it was a minor variety compared with Australia’s major red grapes.

Wine Australia has reported an Australian-grown Negroamaro example from Heathcote in Victoria. This shows that the variety is not limited to Italy, although Australian production remains relatively niche. The available evidence does not establish a current national planting total, so it is better to think of Negroamaro as an occasional Australian discovery rather than a mainstream category.

## How should you serve and pair it?

Start with the style of the particular wine.

A structured red Negroamaro is likely to work well with grilled or roasted meats, tomato-based dishes, aubergine, mushrooms, hard cheese and strongly seasoned vegetables. These are starting points, not rules. A fresher red or rosé may suit lighter meals, vegetables or dishes where a powerful wine would overwhelm the food.

Serve a fuller red slightly cool rather than warm. A lighter red or rosé can generally be served cooler still. Decanting is optional: it may help a young, concentrated wine open up, but it is not mandatory for every bottle.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Negroamaro a sweet wine?

Usually, Negroamaro is used for dry wines. However, ripe dark-fruit flavours can make a wine seem rich without making it sweet. Check the style and label rather than judging sweetness from the grape name alone.

### Is Negroamaro the same as Primitivo?

No. They are separate grape varieties, both associated with southern Italy and Puglia. Their wines may share some broad ripe-fruit associations, but one should not be used as a substitute for the other.

### Is Negroamaro only made as a red wine?

No. It is also used for rosé, sparkling and lightly sparkling styles under some regional rules.

### What is the correct spelling: Negroamaro or Negro Amaro?

Both spellings are used in authoritative references. Negroamaro is a common reader-facing spelling, while Negro Amaro is recorded as the official Italian variety name. Nero Amaro is also listed as a synonym in the Italian Vitis Database.

### Is Negroamaro grown in Australia?

Yes. Australian industry evidence records wineries working with the variety and includes an Australian-grown example from Heathcote, Victoria. It remains a minor variety in Australia.
