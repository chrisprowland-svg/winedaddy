# What Is Montepulciano? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Montepulciano is a dark-skinned red wine grape variety.
- It is most strongly associated with Abruzzo in central Italy.
- Montepulciano d’Abruzzo is a protected wine designation, not simply the grape’s full name.
- Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is a different Tuscan wine, made primarily from Sangiovese.
- Montepulciano wines are often deeply coloured, dry, fruity and tannic, but their style can vary considerably.
- Australian drinkers may encounter the grape in imported Italian wines and some Australian-produced wines.

## Quick answer: what is Montepulciano?

Montepulciano is a red wine-grape variety of *Vitis vinifera*. It is best known for producing deeply coloured Italian red wines, particularly in Abruzzo and other central and southern regions of Italy.

The name becomes confusing because it also appears in names of Italian wine designations. On a label:

- **Montepulciano** usually identifies the grape variety.
- **Montepulciano d’Abruzzo** refers to a protected wine designation associated with Abruzzo and linked to the Montepulciano grape.
- **Vino Nobile di Montepulciano** refers to a separate Tuscan designation named for the town of Montepulciano. It is made primarily from Sangiovese, locally known as Prugnolo Gentile.

So, Montepulciano the grape is not the same thing as Montepulciano the Tuscan town, and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is not a Montepulciano-based wine by definition.

## Where is Montepulciano grown?

Montepulciano is most strongly associated with Abruzzo, on Italy’s central Adriatic side. The grape is also grown in regions including Marche, Molise, Umbria, Lazio and Puglia, although its importance varies from place to place.

Abruzzo is the key association for wine learners because of the protected designation Montepulciano d’Abruzzo. The grape’s historical origin is less certain than its modern regional identity, so it is safer to say that Montepulciano is strongly associated with Abruzzo than to claim that Abruzzo is definitively its birthplace.

The grape is also grown in Australia. Wine Australia’s historical variety snapshot recorded 74 hectares planted in 2015 and 778 tonnes crushed in 2019, with fruit recorded from areas including Heathcote, the Murray–Darling–Swan Hill area, the Riverina, the Riverland and the Barossa Valley. These figures show an established but relatively small Australian presence; they are not current 2026 planting or availability figures.

## Montepulciano d’Abruzzo explained

“Montepulciano d’Abruzzo” combines the grape name with an Abruzzo designation context:

- **Montepulciano** identifies the grape associated with the wine.
- **d’Abruzzo** means the wine is connected with Abruzzo and its protected production rules.

It is therefore not simply another spelling of Montepulciano, and it is not the name of a town in Abruzzo. The exact permitted composition depends on the relevant category and production specification, so it is best not to assume that every bottle carrying the word Montepulciano has identical grape content or style.

This is also why an Australian wine labelled Montepulciano should not be described as Montepulciano d’Abruzzo. The Italian designation refers to its regulated geographical origin and production rules.

## What does Montepulciano wine taste like?

Montepulciano commonly produces red wines with:

- deep colour;
- a dry finish;
- red or darker fruit flavours;
- floral or spice-like notes;
- noticeable tannin; and
- medium to full body, depending on how and where the wine was made.

That is a useful starting point, not a rigid tasting formula. A cooler or higher site, different crop level, earlier harvest, gentler extraction or limited oak maturation can produce a fresher, lighter-feeling wine. Riper fruit, more extraction, oak and bottle age can create a broader, more powerful style.

“Dry” means the wine is not noticeably sweet from residual sugar. It does not mean the wine will taste austere or lacking in fruit. A dry Montepulciano can still taste generously fruity.

## How should you choose a bottle?

Start with the label rather than assuming every Montepulciano tastes the same.

Look for:

1. **The place** — an Italian designation and an Australian regional wine may express the grape differently.
2. **The vintage** — a younger wine may show more primary fruit, while bottle age can soften and develop structure.
3. **The producer’s style** — winemaking choices affect extraction, oak and texture.
4. **The food you are serving** — consider the wine’s body, tannin and freshness together.

If you enjoy red wines with colour, fruit and some grip, Montepulciano is a useful variety to explore. If the wine feels firm or drying, that sensation is likely to come from tannin rather than sweetness.

## What food goes with Montepulciano?

Pair the wine with the structure of both the bottle and the dish.

Fuller, more tannic examples can suit grilled or roasted foods, especially preparations with protein or fat. Fruitier and fresher examples can work with tomato-based pasta, pizza and vegetable dishes. Richer oak or more concentrated wines may need more intensely flavoured food.

These are starting points, not rules. A lighter Montepulciano may be more comfortable with a simple meal than a heavily extracted example, while a richer bottle may stand up to roasted or grilled flavours.

## Common questions

### Is Montepulciano a grape or a region?

Montepulciano is primarily the name of a red wine-grape variety. The word also appears in names of Italian wine designations, which creates the confusion.

### Is Montepulciano the same as Sangiovese?

No. They are distinct grape varieties. Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is primarily made from Sangiovese, despite containing the word Montepulciano.

### Is Montepulciano d’Abruzzo made in Australia?

An Australian wine may be made from the Montepulciano grape, but it should not be treated as the Italian Montepulciano d’Abruzzo designation. That designation is tied to Abruzzo and its regulated production rules.

### Is Montepulciano always full-bodied?

No. It can produce medium- to full-bodied wines, but climate, site, ripeness, yields, extraction, oak and maturation all influence the final style.

### Is Montepulciano sweet?

Most examples discussed here are dry wines. Fruitiness can make a dry wine seem generous or sweet-fruited, but fruit flavour is not the same as residual sugar.
