# What is Trousseau?

## Highlights

- Trousseau is a **red wine-grape variety**, not a region or winemaking method.
- It is associated most strongly with the **Jura in eastern France**.
- You may also encounter it as **Bastardo** in Portugal or **Merenzao** and **Maturana Tinta** in Spain.
- Its wines can be deeply coloured, fragrant, fresh and tannic.
- Trousseau can make anything from light, lively, fruit-driven reds to more structured wines suited to ageing.
- It is present in Australia, but the available evidence does not show large national plantings or broad commercial significance.
- Trousseau is not the same reader-facing identity as the similarly named **Trousseau Gris**.

## The quick answer

Trousseau is a red grape variety from eastern France, particularly associated with the Jura. It belongs to the species *Vitis vinifera*, the same broad species that includes most familiar wine grapes.

A bottle made from Trousseau may be labelled **Trousseau**, **Trousseau Noir**, **Bastardo**, **Merenzao** or **Maturana Tinta**, depending on where it was grown. The name changes, but the underlying variety is understood to be the same in the principal French, Portuguese and Spanish references.

In the glass, Trousseau is usually recognised for a combination of colour, perfume, freshness and tannin. Expect red or wild berry flavours, sometimes alongside spice, savoury notes or an earthy character. It is not always a light red, and it is not always a powerful one: vineyard conditions, crop levels, ripeness and winemaking can shift the style considerably.

## Where does Trousseau come from?

Trousseau’s established historical association is with eastern France and the Jura, including the former Franche-Comté region. The precise route by which it reached the Iberian Peninsula is less certain, but DNA evidence supports its relationship with Portugal’s Bastardo.

In Portugal, Bastardo is grown in several areas and is particularly associated with the Douro. The name can appear in both still and fortified-wine contexts, but Trousseau is not simply a “Port grape”. In Spain, corresponding names include Merenzao, Maturana Tinta and Verdejo Negro.

That makes Trousseau a useful example of why grape names can be confusing. A variety may travel across borders and acquire a local name, while the name on the label tells you more about regional tradition than about a completely different grape.

## What does Trousseau wine taste like?

Trousseau commonly produces red wines with:

- deep or striking colour;
- fresh, lively acidity;
- noticeable to firm tannin;
- red berry and wild berry flavours;
- floral or perfumed aromas;
- spice, savoury or earthy notes in some examples.

The wines may be light-to-medium-bodied, medium-bodied or fuller and more concentrated. In the Jura, controlled yields can help produce deeper colour and more structure. Other winemaking choices can produce a more delicate, fruit-focused wine that emphasises freshness and perfume.

“Tannic” does not necessarily mean harsh. Tannin is the drying, textural sensation that can make a wine feel structured, particularly around the gums. In Trousseau, acidity and tannin can give the wine energy and shape, while fruit and perfume keep it expressive.

There is no single Trousseau flavour formula. A warm, sunny site and full ripeness may encourage a darker, fuller style. Earlier picking or gentler extraction may preserve lighter red fruit and a more delicate texture. Maturation choices can also affect how savoury, spicy or rounded the wine feels.

## Why does the style vary?

Trousseau needs enough warmth and sunlight to ripen reliably. It also buds early, which can expose vines to spring-frost risk. The variety is reported to have irregular yields, so the amount of fruit on the vine can influence concentration and structure.

These vineyard factors matter because grapes do not arrive at the winery with a fixed flavour profile. Crop level, sunlight, water availability, harvest timing and extraction all influence the finished wine.

A more concentrated Trousseau may be dark, firm and capable of developing in bottle. A lighter example may be best enjoyed for its brightness, perfume and fresh berry character. Neither style is automatically more “correct”; both can reflect sensible choices in the vineyard and winery.

## Trousseau, Bastardo and Trousseau Gris

The most useful names to recognise are:

- **Trousseau:** the main French name.
- **Trousseau Noir:** a distinguishing form of the French name for the dark-berried red variety.
- **Bastardo:** a major Portuguese name.
- **Merenzao:** a Spanish name.
- **Maturana Tinta:** another Spanish synonym.

**Trousseau Gris** deserves caution. It is a closely related or separate grey- or rose-berried naming category in grape references, rather than a name to use casually as though it were interchangeable with red Trousseau. If you see Trousseau Gris, check the specific wine and regional context rather than assuming it is simply “white Trousseau”.

## Is Trousseau grown in Australia?

Yes, Trousseau has been identified in Australia, including under names such as **Bastardo** and **Cabernet Gros**. Australian Wine Research Institute material documents its presence in historical vineyard-variety surveys, and Portuguese official material also records Bastardo in Australia.

However, the approved evidence does not establish a reliable current national planting area or broad commercial importance. For Australian drinkers, Trousseau is best understood as a niche variety: present, but much less familiar than Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinot Noir or Chardonnay.

That also makes correct identification important. Historical vineyard names and older plantings can create confusion, particularly when a variety has been known by more than one name.

## How should you serve Trousseau?

For a red Trousseau, try serving it slightly cool, around **13–16°C**, rather than at a warm room temperature. A standard red-wine glass is sufficient; you do not need specialist equipment.

Its freshness and tannin make it a natural partner for foods with some savoury weight. Try it with:

- charcuterie;
- roast poultry;
- grilled meat;
- red meat;
- game;
- smoked foods.

Lighter, fruitier examples may be enjoyable soon after release. More structured wines can develop with age, but ageing potential depends on the individual wine, its acidity and tannin, its vintage, how it was made and how it has been stored. “Trousseau” on the label is not a guarantee that every bottle is built for long-term cellaring.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Trousseau a red or white grape?

Trousseau is a red wine-grape variety. The similarly named Trousseau Gris should not automatically be treated as the same reader-facing wine identity.

### Is Trousseau the same as Bastardo?

In the principal grape-variety references supplied for this guide, Bastardo is a recognised name for Trousseau, particularly in Portugal. Local naming and historical records can still make labels complicated.

### Is Trousseau the same as Port?

No. Bastardo can be used in Portuguese fortified-wine contexts, but Trousseau is a grape variety, not a style of Port or a synonym for Port.

### Is Trousseau always a light red?

No. It can be light and fragrant, but controlled yields and suitable warm sites can produce wines with deeper colour, firmer tannin and greater structure.

### Should Trousseau be aged?

Some structured examples can develop with age. Lighter, more fruit-driven examples may be intended for earlier drinking. The individual wine matters more than the grape name alone.
