# What Is Skin Contact in White Wine? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Skin contact means leaving white-grape juice with the grape skins for part of winemaking.
- It can happen before fermentation, during fermentation or, in longer examples, afterwards.
- The process may add colour, aroma, flavour, body and phenolic grip.
- Skin contact is not automatically the same as orange wine, natural wine or unfiltered wine.
- Stems, amphorae, oak and native yeast are optional—not requirements.
- Longer contact is not necessarily better. It can increase extraction, bitterness or astringency.

## Quick answer: what does skin contact mean?

**Skin contact in white wine means allowing juice from white grapes to remain in contact with the grape skins for some part of the winemaking process, either before fermentation, during fermentation or, in longer examples, after fermentation.**

Most white grapes are pressed relatively quickly, separating the juice from the skins. With skin contact, the winemaker deliberately leaves some or all of the juice with the grape skins for a period. Seeds and sometimes stems may also remain in the mixture.

This is a **process choice**, not one fixed wine style. Contact might last for hours, several days or much longer. It may be used to influence aroma and texture without producing a strongly coloured wine, or it may form part of a prolonged, more deeply coloured style often associated with amber or orange wine.

The result depends on the grape variety, ripeness, temperature, oxygen exposure, contact time, vessel and handling. Skin contact can make a wine more textural or phenolic, but it does not guarantee a particular flavour, colour or level of quality.

## How does the process work?

A simplified white-wine pathway looks like this:

1. White grapes are harvested.
2. The fruit may be crushed, destemmed or pressed.
3. Some or all of the juice remains with the skins.
4. Contact takes place before fermentation, during fermentation or—less commonly—in a longer post-fermentation period.
5. The juice or wine is pressed or separated from the grape solids.
6. Fermentation begins or continues, depending on when the contact occurred.
7. The wine is clarified, matured and bottled according to the winemaker’s broader choices.

This is different from **pressing**. Pressing is the mechanical process of extracting juice from grapes or grape solids. Skin contact is the time that juice remains with those solids. A winemaker can press immediately to minimise contact, or delay pressing to allow more extraction.

Skin contact and **maceration** overlap, but the words emphasise different things. Skin contact describes the physical relationship between juice and grape solids. Maceration usually highlights the extraction taking place during that contact.

It is also different from **fermentation**. Fermentation is the conversion of grape sugars into alcohol and other products by microorganisms. Juice can spend time on skins before fermentation begins, or it can ferment while still on skins. So skin contact does not necessarily mean skin fermentation.

## What can skin contact do to white wine?

Grape skins contain compounds that can affect a wine’s appearance, aroma and mouthfeel. The possible effects include:

### Colour

Longer contact can deepen a wine’s colour from pale gold towards richer gold, amber or orange tones. The exact result depends on the variety, skin pigments, oxygen exposure and extraction conditions. Short skin contact may produce little obvious colour change.

### Aroma and flavour

Skin contact can extract flavour compounds and aroma precursors from the skins. A wine may therefore seem more aromatic, savoury or layered, although the result is not guaranteed. Longer contact can also reduce the impression of fresh white-fruit aromas in some wines.

### Body and texture

Phenolic compounds from skins and seeds can add weight, viscosity or a grippy texture. This is why some skin-contact whites feel less like simple, smooth white wines and more like wines with structure.

### Bitterness, tannin and astringency

White grapes contain phenolic material, including compounds that can contribute bitterness or a drying sensation. Longer, warmer or more vigorous extraction may increase phenolic grip and astringency.

People often describe this as “tannin” in a white wine. The sensation can resemble the drying grip of red wine, although the amount and type of phenolic material vary. Skin contact does not always make a wine bitter or tannic, and more extraction is not automatically better.

## Short contact versus prolonged contact

Short pre-fermentation contact may be measured in hours. It can be used to influence aroma, flavour, body and phenolic composition while keeping the finished wine relatively pale and recognisably “white” in style.

More prolonged contact can continue through fermentation and sometimes beyond it. This generally extracts more material from the skins and seeds, making changes in colour and texture more noticeable. It may also increase the risk of coarse bitterness or excessive astringency if the fruit and conditions are not well suited to the approach.

There is no universal point at which ordinary skin contact becomes “extended maceration”. The term is comparative and depends on the context. Broader, longer maceration is covered separately in [extended maceration](/extended-maceration/).

## Is skin contact the same as orange wine?

Not exactly.

**Orange wine** is commonly used for some wines made from white grapes with substantial or prolonged skin contact. These wines may have amber or orange colour and more noticeable phenolic texture. However, not every skin-contact white wine is made in this style, and the terms are not perfect synonyms.

Australian drinkers also need to be careful with the wording. Wine Australia notes that **“Orange” is a registered geographical indication**. Its compliance guidance discusses the use of “orange wine” and identifies “amber” as an alternative descriptive term for some skin-contact white wines or wines with an amber colour. The precise wording on a label must also avoid misleading consumers.

In other words, a wine can be made with skin contact without being called orange wine. “Skin-contact”, “skin-fermented” and “amber” may describe related ideas, but they do not promise exactly the same production method or appearance.

## What skin contact does not tell you

The phrase does not automatically mean that a wine is:

- natural, organic or biodynamic;
- made with native yeast;
- low-intervention;
- unfiltered or cloudy;
- made with stems;
- matured in oak or amphora;
- higher in quality;
- more complex or ageworthy.

These are separate winemaking or descriptive questions. A skin-contact wine may use native or cultured yeast, stainless steel or another vessel, and destemmed or whole-bunch fruit. Stems are optional, not a defining requirement.

The same caution applies to oxygen. Oxygen management can influence browning, aroma and phenolic development, but oxidation is not a necessary feature of skin-contact or orange wine.

## How should you read and taste one?

When you see “skin contact” on a menu, label or wine list, treat it as a clue about **texture and production**, not a complete tasting prediction.

You might encounter a pale, fresh wine with only subtle extra texture. You might also find a deeper-coloured wine with savoury aromas, a firmer finish and a drying or slightly bitter sensation. Both can reasonably fall under the broad skin-contact description.

Look for additional information about colour, sweetness, filtration, yeast or maturation if those details matter to you. In the glass, pay attention to:

- whether the colour is noticeably deeper than expected;
- whether the wine feels smooth or grippy;
- whether the finish is drying or bitter;
- whether fruit aromas are joined by savoury, herbal or tea-like impressions.

The most useful takeaway is simple: **skin contact tells you that the juice spent time with grape skins. It does not tell you exactly how long, how the wine fermented or what it must taste like.**

## Frequently asked questions

### Does skin contact always make white wine orange?

No. Short contact may create little visible colour change. Longer contact is more likely to produce gold, amber or orange hues, but the outcome varies.

### Does skin contact require fermentation on the skins?

No. Contact can happen before fermentation, during fermentation or, in longer examples, afterwards.

### Are stems necessary?

No. Stems may be included or excluded. Their presence is a separate choice.

### Is skin-contact wine always natural wine?

No. Skin contact describes one process. It does not establish the wine’s yeast, farming, filtration or broader intervention choices.

### Is more skin contact better?

No. More time or stronger extraction can add texture, but it can also increase bitterness, astringency or coarseness. The appropriate approach depends on the fruit and the desired wine.
