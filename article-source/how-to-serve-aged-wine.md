# How to serve aged wine

## Highlights

- Aged wine is not automatically better, ready or delicate. Every bottle has its own history.
- Stand a bottle upright for several hours when sediment is likely.
- Serve mature wine cool, not at the warm temperature of many Australian rooms.
- Open gently, especially if the cork looks fragile.
- Taste a small pour before deciding whether to decant.
- Decanting can remove sediment, but too much air may harm a fragile wine.
- Re-cork and refrigerate leftovers promptly: mature wine can change quickly.

## Quick answer

Serve aged wine cautiously and make decisions based on the bottle in front of you. Check for leakage, a low fill level, a pushed cork or other signs of poor storage. If sediment is likely, stand the bottle upright for several hours, or overnight if substantial sediment is expected.

Cool the wine to suit its style. A full-bodied red is generally better around 16–18°C than at the temperature of a warm Australian room. Open the bottle gently, pour a small sample and check its appearance, aroma and flavour.

If the wine is fragile, very old or already expressive, pour it directly into glasses and let it develop there. If it is sound and has sediment, decant it slowly into a clean vessel, stopping when the deposit approaches the neck. Decanting is a choice, not an automatic requirement for old wine.

## Before opening: check the bottle

“Aged wine” has no universal age threshold. It simply means a wine that has had meaningful time to develop. Age does not guarantee quality or readiness to drink. Style, closure, storage history and bottle condition all matter.

Before opening, look for:

- leakage or sticky seepage around the capsule;
- a noticeably low fill level;
- a cork that appears pushed upwards;
- signs that the bottle may have been exposed to excessive heat; and
- a damaged or loose capsule.

These clues do not prove that the wine is spoiled, but they suggest you should handle it conservatively. If the wine is rare, valuable or visibly compromised, specialist advice may be sensible.

Also identify whether the bottle is still, sparkling or fortified. The process below is primarily for still wine. Sparkling wine is usually not decanted because exposure to air can reduce its bubbles.

## Stand it up if sediment is likely

Sediment is a deposit that can form as wine matures. It may look like fine grit or dark flakes, particularly in some mature red and fortified wines. It is not automatically a fault, although it can feel unpleasant in the mouth.

If sediment is likely, stand the bottle upright for several hours so the deposit settles at the base. Overnight may be useful when substantial sediment is expected. This is practical guidance rather than a fixed rule.

Avoid unnecessary shaking or carrying the bottle around once it is standing. If you do not expect sediment, there is no need to leave every bottle upright for a long period.

## Get the temperature right

“Room temperature” is not a reliable instruction in an Australian home. A warm room can make mature wine seem heavy, soft and less fresh.

Use these approximate starting points:

- **Light reds:** lightly chilled if the room is warm.
- **Medium- and full-bodied reds:** around 16–18°C; mature reds can often benefit from being slightly cooler rather than warmer.
- **Light whites and rosés:** chilled.
- **Full-bodied whites:** lightly chilled.
- **Sweet and sparkling wines:** well chilled.

A brief period in the refrigerator can help bring a red down to a more suitable temperature. An ice-water bucket is another practical option. Avoid serving a still wine so cold that its aromas are difficult to smell, unless its style calls for it.

## Open gently

Wipe the capsule and bottle lip before opening. Use a steady opener and apply as little force as possible. Old natural cork can become brittle, particularly when storage has been poor.

If the cork begins to crumble, do not keep pulling aggressively. Remove loose pieces carefully. If fragments fall into the wine, strain the affected pour through a clean, unbleached coffee filter before serving.

Cork fragments are not the same as cork taint. Cork taint is a smell and taste fault, often associated with damp cardboard or mould, that suppresses the wine’s fruit. A broken cork may simply be an old, fragile closure.

## Taste before serving everyone

Pour a small sample into a clean glass. First look at the wine’s colour and clarity, then smell it and taste it.

Mature wine may have softer structure and more savoury, earthy or delicate character than it had when young. Those changes are not automatically faults. The style matters: a nutty or honeyed note may be part of some deliberately oxidative wines, but it can suggest unwanted oxidation in a wine expected to be fresh.

Be cautious if the wine smells strongly musty, like damp cardboard, or seems sharply vinegar-like, flat, stale or clearly compromised. Extended decanting will not reliably rescue a damaged bottle.

## Decant or pour directly?

Decanting has two different purposes:

1. **Separating sediment:** pouring the clear wine away from the deposit.
2. **Adding air:** exposing the wine to oxygen, which can change its aroma and flavour.

For mature wine, sediment separation is usually the clearer reason to decant. If the bottle appears sound and sediment is present, pour slowly into a clean decanter or jug. A phone light or small torch behind the bottle can help you see when sediment is approaching the neck. Stop pouring before the deposit enters the vessel.

Pour directly into glasses when the wine seems fragile, very old, already aromatic or likely to decline quickly. Letting it develop in the glass gives you more control than exposing the whole bottle to air at once.

A robust wine that seems closed or reduced may benefit from limited aeration, but avoid fixed instructions such as “decant every old wine for an hour”. Mature wine can sometimes lose its remaining aroma and structure quickly after too much air.

## Serve and watch the wine

Use clean glasses that give the wine enough room to smell. Start with modest pours so everyone can reassess the wine as it opens.

Keep the bottle or decanter away from direct sun and heat. Wine may change during the meal: it can become more expressive, or it can lose freshness. There is no universal timetable for what will happen.

If there is sediment, leave the final centimetre or so in the bottle rather than trying to extract every drop. A little wine left behind is preferable to a gritty pour.

## What to do with leftovers

Re-cork the bottle or use a suitable stopper as soon as possible. Store it upright in the refrigerator. Cooling slows the changes that follow opening, but it cannot reverse oxidation or restore a wine that has already deteriorated.

When you return to it, let the wine move back towards an appropriate serving temperature. Do not assume every mature wine will remain enjoyable for a particular number of days. Its condition, fill level, closure, style and exposure to air all affect how quickly it changes.

## A simple aged-wine service checklist

1. **Inspect:** Are there leaks, a low fill level or a pushed cork?
2. **Settle:** Is sediment likely? If so, stand the bottle upright for several hours.
3. **Cool:** Bring the wine to a style-appropriate temperature in °C.
4. **Open:** Work gently and stop if the cork crumbles.
5. **Sample:** Check appearance, aroma and taste.
6. **Choose:** Pour directly if fragile; decant briefly if sound and sedimented.
7. **Serve:** Use clean glasses and small initial pours.
8. **Save:** Re-cork, store upright and refrigerate promptly.

## FAQs

### Does every old wine need to be decanted?

No. Decanting is most clearly useful for separating sediment. Aeration is a separate choice and may harm a fragile mature wine.

### Does sediment mean the wine is faulty?

No. Sediment can form naturally as wine matures. It is not usually a sign that the wine is spoiled, although it may be gritty.

### What if the cork breaks?

Remove loose pieces carefully. Filter the first pour if cork fragments enter the wine. A broken cork does not automatically mean cork taint.

### Should aged red wine be served at room temperature?

Not necessarily. In a warm Australian home, a mature red may taste fresher around 16–18°C than at room temperature.

### How long will aged wine last after opening?

There is no reliable universal answer. Mature wine can change quickly. Re-corking and refrigeration slow the process but do not stop or reverse deterioration.
