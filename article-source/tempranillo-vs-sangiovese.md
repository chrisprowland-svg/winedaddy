# Tempranillo vs Sangiovese: What’s the Difference?

Tempranillo and Sangiovese are different red wine-grape varieties. In broad terms, Tempranillo tends towards structured red and dark fruit, moderate-to-firm tannin and a strong association with oak and ageing in classic Spanish styles. Sangiovese is usually brighter and more savoury, with marked acidity, red cherry, floral notes and firm tannin.

Neither grape has one fixed taste. Climate, site, ripeness, yield, blending, extraction, oak and bottle age can change the finished wine considerably. So the useful question is not which grape is “better”, but which style sounds more appealing to you.

## Highlights

- **Tempranillo** is especially associated with Spain, including Rioja and Ribera del Duero.
- **Sangiovese** is especially associated with central Italy, particularly Tuscany.
- Tempranillo often feels rounder and more structured; Sangiovese often feels fresher and more acid-driven.
- Tempranillo may show red plum, strawberry, darker fruit, liquorice, cedar, vanilla or cocoa.
- Sangiovese is commonly associated with sour red cherry, herbs, violet, earth and savoury spice.
- Oak is a winemaking choice, not an automatic flavour of either grape.
- Both varieties are grown in Australia, where local examples may differ from Spanish and Italian benchmarks.
- Sangiovese often suits tomato-based dishes; Tempranillo often suits grilled, roasted or slow-cooked foods.

## Quick answer: Tempranillo or Sangiovese?

Choose a **Sangiovese style** if you enjoy fresh, savoury red wines with noticeable acidity, cherry fruit and a firm, food-friendly feel. It can be a natural match for tomato-based dishes, herbs and grilled meats.

Choose a **Tempranillo style** if you prefer a more rounded or structured red, with red and dark fruit and possible oak characters such as vanilla, cedar, cocoa or liquorice. It can work especially well with grilled meats, roast foods and slow-cooked dishes.

That is a starting point, not a rule. A cool-climate Tempranillo can be light and fresh, while a ripe Australian Sangiovese can be fuller and more concentrated than a classic Italian example.

## What is Tempranillo like?

Tempranillo is a dark-skinned red grape strongly associated with Spain. Rioja and Ribera del Duero are important regional and appellation contexts for the variety, but neither name is another name for the grape.

Tempranillo commonly produces medium- to full-bodied wines with medium to medium-high acidity and moderate-to-firm tannin. Its fruit can include red plum, strawberry, cherry, forest fruit and darker plum. Depending on the wine, savoury characters such as liquorice, tobacco, leather, earth or balsamic spice may also appear.

Oak and ageing are particularly important style cues in many classic Spanish examples. Vanilla, cedar, cocoa, toast and liquorice can develop or become more noticeable with maturation. These are not all direct grape aromas: oak and bottle age can contribute significantly.

Tempranillo has several recognised names, including Tinto Fino, Tinta del País, Cencibel, Tinto de Toro and Ull de Llebre. Their legal use can vary by region, so they should not automatically be treated as interchangeable on every label.

## What is Sangiovese like?

Sangiovese is an Italian red grape most strongly associated with Tuscany and central Italy. It is central to wines such as Chianti, Chianti Classico and Brunello di Montalcino. **Chianti is not a synonym for Sangiovese**: it is a regulated Tuscan wine designation in which the cited production rules allow 70–100 per cent Sangiovese, alongside permitted varieties.

Sangiovese is commonly recognised for marked acidity and firm tannin. This gives the wine freshness and grip, particularly when young. Its fruit profile often centres on sour or bright red cherry, with plum, red berries and sometimes darker fruit.

Floral and savoury notes may include violet, dried herbs, earth, tea, tobacco and spice. Oak can add vanilla, coffee, toast and further texture, but an unoaked or lightly matured Sangiovese will show the grape and site differently.

## Why the same grape can taste different

Climate influences ripeness, acidity, colour, tannin and alcohol. Cooler or higher sites may retain more freshness and red-fruit character. Warmer conditions can increase ripeness, body, alcohol and darker-fruit impressions, although excessive warmth or later harvesting can reduce perceived freshness.

Site, water availability, exposure, vine age, crop level and harvest timing also matter. Higher yields may produce lighter, less concentrated wines, while lower yields can increase concentration without guaranteeing quality.

Winemaking creates another layer. Blending can alter colour, aroma and texture. Extraction affects colour and tannin. Oak can add flavour and soften or reshape texture. Bottle age can bring savoury, earthy and tobacco-like complexity to either variety.

This is why grape variety is best understood as a starting point rather than a complete tasting note. For more background, see [What Is Tempranillo?](/what-is-tempranillo/) and [What Is Sangiovese?](/what-is-sangiovese/).

## Tempranillo and Sangiovese in Australia

Both varieties are established in Australian wine production, although neither is a mainstream national red variety on the scale of Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon.

Wine Australia’s **2024 National Vintage Report** recorded 3,937 tonnes of Tempranillo and 5,906 tonnes of Sangiovese crushed. These are dated annual crush figures, not current planting forecasts or a measure of national quality.

Wine Australia’s 2024 Sangiovese snapshot reported 438 hectares planted and identified the Murray Darling–Swan Hill, King Valley, Riverina, Riverland and McLaren Vale among the leading regions by Sangiovese crush. For fuller Australian context, see [Tempranillo in Australia](/tempranillo-in-australia/) and [Sangiovese in Australia](/sangiovese-in-australia/).

Australian examples can differ from Spanish and Italian benchmarks because of region, climate, irrigation, clones, crop level, harvest decisions and winemaking. The label’s region, alcohol level and stated oak or ageing treatment may tell you as much about the style as the grape name.

## Which one suits food better?

Sangiovese’s acidity can work especially well with tomato-based dishes because the wine remains lively beside tomato’s own acidity. Its savoury and herbal character can also suit dishes featuring herbs, garlic, mushrooms or earthy vegetables. Its tannin can support grilled meats, sausages and roast foods.

Tempranillo’s body, fruit and tannin can suit grilled or roasted meats, smoky foods and slow-cooked dishes. Oak-aged examples may stand up to richer food, while fresher examples can also work with tomato-based dishes.

The practical rule is to pair the wine with the dish’s **acidity, tannin, salt, fat, sauce and intensity**, not simply its country of origin. [How Wine Pairing Works](/how-wine-pairing-works/) explains this approach in more detail.

## A simple choice guide

- Want freshness, cherry fruit and savoury lift? Try a **Sangiovese style**.
- Want a rounder, more structured red with possible oak? Try a **Tempranillo style**.
- Dislike drying tannin? Look for younger, softer or less extracted examples of either grape.
- Dislike oak? Look for unoaked, young or minimally matured wines.
- Want an Australian expression? Pay attention to the region and winemaking details, not just the variety name.

### Frequently asked questions

#### Is Tempranillo the same as Sangiovese?

No. They are separate red grape varieties, with Tempranillo principally associated with Spain and Sangiovese principally associated with central Italy.

#### Is Tempranillo sweeter than Sangiovese?

Not necessarily. Both commonly produce dry wines. A wine can taste fruity without containing noticeable sweetness; fruitiness and dryness are different concepts.

#### Is Sangiovese always lighter than Tempranillo?

No. Sangiovese often appears medium-bodied and can have a relatively translucent colour, but ripeness, extraction, blending and climate can produce fuller or darker wines. Tempranillo can also be made in fresher, lighter styles.

#### Which grape has more acidity?

Sangiovese commonly has the more marked acidity, while Tempranillo is generally medium to medium-high. Climate and harvest timing can narrow or widen that difference.

#### Can both varieties age well?

Yes, some examples of both can age well when acidity, tannin, fruit, alcohol and oak are in balance. The grape name alone does not guarantee longevity.
