# How to choose wine for a dinner party

Choosing wine for a dinner party becomes much easier when you stop looking for one “best” bottle. Start with four things: your guests, the menu, the occasion and your budget. Then choose a small group of versatile wine styles, plan realistic quantities and serve them in a sensible order.

## Highlights

- Choose for the people and food, not just the main ingredient.
- A few flexible styles are usually more useful than many highly specific bottles.
- Acidity, body, sweetness, alcohol, tannin, spice and richness all affect a pairing.
- A 750 mL bottle provides about six 125 mL pours.
- Water and appealing non-alcoholic options are part of good hosting.
- Pairing advice is a starting point, not a set of rules.

## The quickest way to choose

Before shopping, ask:

1. **Who is coming?** Do they usually prefer white, red, sparkling, rosé or something lighter? Will anyone be driving, avoiding alcohol or simply choosing not to drink?
2. **What is being served?** Look at the whole dish, including sauces, seasoning and cooking method—not only whether it contains seafood, chicken or meat.
3. **What is the occasion?** A casual barbecue, long lunch and formal multi-course dinner may call for different amounts and styles.
4. **What is the budget?** Decide what you are comfortable spending overall, rather than assuming a particular region, grape or price guarantees quality.

For a mixed group, two or three broad styles can cover more preferences than several bottles chosen for very specific pairings. For example, you might offer a fresh white or rosé, a light- to medium-bodied red and, if suitable, a sparkling wine for the start of the evening.

This is not a formula that guarantees everyone will agree. It is a way to reduce the risk of making the evening depend on one narrow choice.

## Choose by the food’s character

Wine generally works best when its intensity and structure make sense alongside the food.

- **Freshness and acidity** can make rich or creamy food feel lighter.
- **Body** describes how full or weighty a wine feels in your mouth. More substantial food can often handle a fuller-bodied wine.
- **Sweetness** may soften the perception of chilli heat. Very hot food can make high alcohol and firm tannin feel more aggressive.
- **Salt, fat, umami, herbs and smoke** can change how a wine tastes.
- **The sauce may matter more than the protein.** Roast chicken with a rich sauce is a different pairing challenge from poached chicken with herbs.

Some useful starting points:

- **Seafood:** begin with fresh, lighter-bodied wines, then consider the preparation. Fried fish, creamy seafood and strongly spiced seafood may call for different choices.
- **Poultry:** white or red wine can work. Think about roasting, skin, herbs, smoke and sauce.
- **Vegetarian food:** identify the dominant feature—tomato, mushroom, cream, char, chilli, sweetness or umami—instead of treating all vegetarian dishes alike.
- **Red meat:** a fuller-bodied red may suit a rich, grilled or roasted dish, but salt, smoke and sauce can change the balance.
- **Rich sauces:** freshness can provide contrast and stop the meal feeling heavy.
- **Spicy food:** consider freshness, moderate alcohol and possibly some sweetness. Personal preference still matters; there is no rule that excludes every red wine.

A useful question when shopping is: “What is the strongest flavour or texture in this dish?” That usually gives you more information than the ingredient name alone.

## Pick broad styles, not rigid rules

You do not need to memorise every grape variety. Ask a retailer or wine professional for broad styles instead:

- a **fresh, aromatic white** for lighter dishes, herbs and seafood;
- a **fuller-bodied white** for richer textures, roast flavours or creamy sauces;
- a **dry rosé** for a flexible middle ground at a casual meal;
- a **light- to medium-bodied red** for poultry, mushrooms, vegetables and lighter red-meat dishes;
- a **fuller-bodied red** for strongly flavoured, grilled or roasted food;
- a **sparkling wine** as a welcome drink or with salty, fried or celebratory foods.

These categories are starting points, not guarantees. Two wines in the same category can differ in sweetness, oak, alcohol and texture.

Read the label for clues such as alcohol percentage and standard drinks, but remember that a label or tasting description cannot promise exactly how a wine will taste to every person.

## Work out how many bottles you need

A standard wine bottle is usually 750 mL. The number of glasses depends on the pour:

- 100 mL: about 7.5 pours;
- 125 mL: about 6 pours;
- 150 mL: about 5 pours.

A simple planning formula is:

**guests × expected wine serves per guest × pour size ÷ 750 mL**

For example, if eight guests are likely to have about two wine serves each, using 125 mL pours:

**8 × 2 × 125 ÷ 750 = 2.67 bottles**

That suggests planning around three bottles. You might allow for another bottle if the event is long, wine is the main beverage or guests are likely to receive generous pours. You may need less if beer, spirits and non-alcoholic drinks are also available.

This is a stock-planning example, not a recommendation that anyone should drink a fixed amount. A standard drink in Australia contains 10 g of pure alcohol, and the number of standard drinks in a bottle varies with its alcohol percentage. Check the label rather than estimating from the wine’s colour or style.

## Serve the wines in a comfortable order

If you are serving several styles, a practical sequence is usually:

1. sparkling or another light, fresh wine;
2. fresh whites or rosé;
3. fuller-bodied whites or lighter reds;
4. fuller-bodied reds or sweeter styles, where relevant.

The menu and your guests matter more than following this order perfectly.

Approximate serving ranges are:

- sparkling: **5–10°C**;
- white and rosé: **7–14°C**;
- light-bodied red: **12–17°C**;
- fuller-bodied red: **17–21°C**.

These are useful guides, not strict laws. “Room temperature” can be too warm for red wine in a heated Australian home, so lightly chilling a lighter red may make it taste fresher.

Chill whites, rosé and sparkling before guests arrive, and keep opened bottles cool. One clean, reasonably sized glass per person is perfectly workable for an informal gathering. Put water within easy reach and serve food alongside the wine.

## Good hosting includes every guest

Wine does not need to be the only drink—or a required part of the evening. Offer water and appealing non-alcoholic or low-alcohol choices, and never pressure anyone to drink. Guests may be driving, avoiding alcohol, managing personal circumstances or simply not interested.

In Australia, the legal drinking age is 18. Keep responsible hosting simple: provide food and water, make alternatives easy to choose and help guests plan a safe way home. Detailed alcohol laws can vary between states and territories, so avoid assuming that one rule applies everywhere.

## FAQs

### Is it better to serve one wine or several?

For a varied group, two or three versatile styles often give guests more choice without making the evening complicated. One wine can work well when the menu and guest preferences are quite consistent.

### Should red wine always go with meat?

No. Preparation, sauce, seasoning and richness matter. Poultry can work with white or red wine, and vegetables may suit either depending on their dominant flavours.

### How do I ask for help at a retailer?

Describe the number of guests, menu, budget, expected pour size and the styles your group usually enjoys. Ask for versatile options rather than simply asking for the “best” wine.

### Do I need special wine glasses?

No. Clean, suitable glasses are enough for most dinner parties. Different shapes can influence how wine’s aromas are presented, but glassware should not make hosting stressful.
