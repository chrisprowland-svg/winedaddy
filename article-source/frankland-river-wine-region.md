# Frankland River wine region: a beginner’s guide

Frankland River is a protected Australian wine Geographical Indication (GI) in Western Australia. It sits inside the Great Southern region, within the South West Australia zone.

In simple terms:

**Australia → Western Australia → South West Australia zone → Great Southern region → Frankland River GI**

It is best understood as an inland part of Great Southern, known particularly for Shiraz and Riesling. Its wines can offer a different perspective on Western Australian wine from the more maritime regions closer to the coast.

## Highlights

- Frankland River is an official Australian wine GI.
- It was entered in the Register of Protected Names on 24 August 2000.
- The GI is within Great Southern, South West Australia and Western Australia.
- Frankland River is also commonly described by the wine industry as one of Great Southern’s five subregions.
- Its inland position brings warmer days and cooler nights than the more ocean-influenced parts of Great Southern.
- Shiraz and Riesling are the region’s clearest wine associations.
- “Frankland River” on a label is an origin indication, not a guarantee of a particular flavour or quality.
- The Frankland River townsite is useful for orientation, but it does not define the entire GI.

## Where is Frankland River?

Frankland River is in the inland, northerly part of Western Australia’s Great Southern wine region. The locality is in the Shire of Cranbrook, with the townsite approximately 47 kilometres west of Cranbrook.

From Perth, Frankland River is roughly 320–330 kilometres south-east by road. Allow approximately 3½ to 4+ hours, depending on your route and destination. These figures are useful for orientation rather than precise travel promises.

Albany is the most useful major regional centre for understanding the broader Great Southern area, while Cranbrook is the relevant local-government centre for the Frankland River locality.

Frankland River is not the same thing as the Frankland River town, the river itself or the whole of Great Southern. The official GI covers a defined geographic area described in Wine Australia’s register using technical boundary information.

## How Frankland River fits into Great Southern

Great Southern is a large Western Australian wine region with varied landscapes and climates. Wine-industry sources commonly describe five subregions within it:

- Albany
- Denmark
- Frankland River
- Mount Barker
- Porongurup

Here, “subregion” is useful wine-industry language. The legally protected name is **Frankland River Geographical Indication**, as recorded by Wine Australia.

Frankland River is distinct from nearby Western Australian GIs, including Manjimup, Pemberton and Margaret River. It is also distinct from Great Southern as a whole. A bottle labelled **Great Southern** may come from anywhere within that broader region; a bottle labelled **Frankland River** makes a more geographically specific claim.

## What is the climate like?

Frankland River has a broadly Mediterranean climate, with warm days and cool nights. Its inland position gives it a more continental influence than the coastal areas associated with Albany and Denmark.

Regional descriptions also refer to long sunlight hours. In practical terms, this combination can support ripening while helping retain freshness. However, conditions vary from site to site, and not every vineyard or wine will show the same character.

The region’s soils are described in regional sources as including lateritic gravelly sands and sandy loams over clay. Some areas also include sandy loams associated with granite and gneissic bedrock.

These details help explain the setting in which vines grow, but soil and climate do not mechanically determine flavour. They are part of a wider combination that includes grape variety, vineyard site, vintage and winemaking choices.

## Which grapes is Frankland River known for?

### Shiraz

Shiraz is one of Frankland River’s strongest wine associations. In this inland part of Great Southern, it may show ripe fruit alongside savoury, spicy or peppery notes and a structured shape.

Those are broad regional tendencies, not a tasting guarantee. A lighter, fresher Shiraz and a fuller, more powerful example can both carry the Frankland River name.

### Riesling

Riesling is the other especially recognisable variety. Great Southern Riesling is often associated with dry, crisp and refreshing styles, sometimes with citrus-led aromas and flavours.

If you enjoy bright acidity and aromatic white wines, Frankland River Riesling is a useful place to begin exploring the region.

### Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay

Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay are also important varieties across Great Southern. They can be relevant to Frankland River, but the evidence is stronger at the broader Great Southern level than for treating them as equal regional signatures.

Cabernet Sauvignon may offer a firmer, more structured red style. Chardonnay may appear in fresh or elegant cool-climate expressions. The individual wine’s label and producer information will tell you more than the regional name alone.

## What does Frankland River taste like?

There is no single Frankland River flavour. A region can establish useful expectations, but it cannot guarantee what every bottle will taste like.

As a starting point, you might expect:

- **Shiraz:** red or dark fruit, with possible savoury, spicy or peppery notes and noticeable structure.
- **Riesling:** dry, bright and refreshing, with acidity and possible citrus character.
- **Cabernet Sauvignon:** a firmer, more structured red style.
- **Chardonnay:** fresh or elegant styles, depending on the wine and winemaking.

When tasting, treat these as questions rather than rules: Is the wine fruit-driven or savoury? Does the acidity feel bright? Is the texture firm or supple? This is a more useful approach than trying to force every bottle into a regional stereotype.

## What does “Frankland River” mean on a wine label?

“Frankland River” on an Australian wine label is a geographically specific origin indication. It is narrower than **Western Australia** or **South West Australia**, and more specific than the broader **Great Southern** name.

The name can tell you where the wine’s grapes come from, subject to Australian wine-labelling rules and applicable blending requirements. It does not, by itself, guarantee:

- a particular grape variety;
- a specific flavour profile;
- a winemaking method;
- a quality level; or
- that the wine comes from the Frankland River townsite.

For a clearer idea of what is in the bottle, read the regional name alongside the grape variety, vintage and back-label information.

## How should a beginner explore the region?

A simple starting point is to compare a Frankland River Shiraz with a Frankland River Riesling. The contrast shows how one place can support very different wine styles.

Choose Riesling if you want a dry, bright white with refreshing acidity. Choose Shiraz if you want a red that may combine ripe fruit with savoury or spicy structure. Cabernet Sauvignon is worth considering if you prefer a firmer red shape.

Most importantly, use the GI as a clue rather than a verdict. Frankland River tells you something meaningful about origin, but the grape, vintage, vineyard site and winemaking decisions complete the picture.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Frankland River part of Great Southern?

Yes. Wine Australia places the Frankland River GI within the Great Southern region, South West Australia zone, Western Australia and Australia.

### Is Frankland River the same as Great Southern?

No. Great Southern is the broader parent region. Frankland River is a more specific GI within it.

### Is Frankland River the same as Manjimup or Pemberton?

No. Manjimup and Pemberton are separate Western Australian wine GIs.

### Which wines is Frankland River best known for?

The strongest associations are Shiraz and Riesling. Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay are also relevant in the broader Great Southern context.

### Does Frankland River on a label guarantee a particular taste?

No. It indicates geographic origin, not a guaranteed flavour, quality level or winemaking method.
