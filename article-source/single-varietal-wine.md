# Single-varietal wine: what it means on an Australian wine label

## Highlights

- A single-varietal wine is identified principally by one grape variety.
- In Australia, a wine labelled with one grape variety generally needs at least 85% of that variety.
- A grape name on the label does not necessarily mean the wine is 100% that grape.
- Single-varietal describes grape composition—not quality, price, region, vineyard, ownership or winemaking method.
- A single-varietal wine can still be made from grapes grown in more than one vineyard.
- Blending is not automatically a sign of inferior wine.
- Single-varietal, single-vineyard, estate-grown and field blend mean different things.

## Quick answer: what is a single-varietal wine?

A single-varietal wine is a wine identified principally by one grape variety, such as Chardonnay, Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon or Riesling.

The important Australian label detail is that a named grape variety does not necessarily mean the wine is 100% that variety. Under Australia’s wine-labelling rules, a single variety claim generally requires at least 85% of the wine to come from that variety. Wine Australia states that there are no general tolerances or exemptions to this blending rule.

So, an Australian wine labelled “Chardonnay” qualifies for a Chardonnay variety claim, but the label alone does not prove that every drop came from Chardonnay.

“Varietal wine” is a closely related educational term. Both terms are commonly used for a wine identified by one main grape variety, although the exact legal meaning of variety labelling can differ between countries.

## What does the grape variety tell you?

The named variety is a useful starting point for understanding a wine’s likely character. Grape varieties can contribute recognisable patterns of aroma, flavour, acidity, tannin, texture and body.

But variety is only one part of the story. Climate, vineyard site, soil, harvest conditions, vintage, yeast, fermentation, oak and ageing can all affect the finished wine. Two Australian wines made principally from the same variety can therefore taste quite different.

This is why “single-varietal” should not be read as “the grape tells you exactly what the wine will taste like”. It tells you about composition and gives you a helpful style clue—not a complete tasting description.

A single-varietal wine may be red, white, rosé or sparkling. The term describes the grape composition, not the wine’s colour or production method.

## How to read a single-variety claim in Australia

When one grape variety appears prominently on an Australian label:

- at least 85% of the wine generally comes from that variety;
- the remaining proportion is not an unrestricted free-for-all: other applicable labelling and composition rules still apply;
- the claim does not establish that the wine comes from one vineyard or one region;
- it does not establish quality, price, sweetness, organic status or a particular winemaking method.

For example, “Shiraz” identifies the qualifying variety. It does not, by itself, tell you whether the grapes came from one vineyard, whether the wine was aged in oak, or whether the wine is full-bodied, light-bodied, dry or sweet.

## Single-varietal wine versus a blend

A blend is made by combining wine components. Those components might come from different grape varieties, vineyards, regions, vintages, barrels or production lots.

A wine can therefore be blended in several ways while remaining single-varietal. For instance, a winemaker might combine Chardonnay from different vineyard sites or barrels. If the final wine still meets the relevant variety-claim rules, it can be labelled Chardonnay.

A multi-variety blend combines two or more grape varieties. Blending can be a deliberate winemaking tool used to shape balance, texture, aroma, structure and consistency. It is not evidence that a wine is lower quality.

The useful question is not “single-varietal or blend—which is better?” It is “what kind of composition and style is this wine aiming for?”

## Related terms that are easy to confuse

| Term | What it describes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-varietal** | The wine is identified principally by one grape variety | One vineyard, one region or 100% composition |
| **Wine blend** | Wine components have been combined; these may include varieties, sites, vintages or barrels | Inferior quality |
| **Single-vineyard** | The stated vineyard or site is the source of the grapes | That only one grape variety was used |
| **Estate-grown** | A relationship between the grapes, vineyard and producer or estate | That the wine is single-varietal |
| **Field blend** | Multiple varieties are grown together and commonly harvested or fermented together | A single-varietal composition |
| **Cuvée** | A context-dependent selection, blend or bottling term | Single-variety composition |

A single-vineyard wine can contain more than one grape variety. A single-varietal wine can come from multiple vineyards. These terms describe different parts of a wine’s identity.

## What single-varietal does—and does not—tell a drinker

The term helps you understand the wine’s principal grape composition. That can make it easier to compare bottles and learn what you enjoy. Trying several wines identified by Chardonnay, Shiraz or Riesling can help you notice how variety, place and winemaking work together.

It does not tell you:

- whether the wine is better or more expensive;
- whether it is made from one vineyard;
- whether it is estate-grown;
- whether it is 100% one grape;
- whether it is dry, sweet, light-bodied or full-bodied;
- whether it was made with oak, stainless steel or another vessel;
- exactly how it will taste.

It is also separate from vintage. A vintage tells you about the harvest year; a variety claim tells you about grape composition. See [Vintage wine vs non-vintage wine](/vintage-wine-vs-non-vintage-wine/) for that distinction.

## The ordinary-drinker takeaway

When you see “Chardonnay”, “Shiraz” or another grape variety on an Australian label, think: **this wine is principally identified by that grape, but it is not automatically a 100% guarantee**.

Single-varietal wine is a useful way to understand composition. It is not a promise of quality, purity or a fixed flavour profile. The grape matters—but so do the place, the year and the winemaking.

## Suggested FAQs

### Is single-varietal wine the same as 100% one grape?

No. In Australia, a single variety claim generally requires at least 85% of the wine to come from the named variety. The label alone does not prove 100% composition.

### Is single-varietal wine better than blended wine?

No. Single-varietal and blended wines are different compositional choices. Blending can be used to create balance, complexity and a particular style.

### Can a single-varietal wine come from several vineyards?

Yes. Single-varietal describes the grape composition, while single-vineyard describes the source. A wine can be made principally from one variety sourced from multiple vineyards.

### Can single-varietal wine be sparkling or rosé?

Yes. The term describes grape composition, not colour or production method.

### Is “varietal wine” a different type of wine?

It is usually a closely related educational term for wine identified by one principal grape variety. Labelling rules and terminology can vary between jurisdictions.
