# Pantelleria wine: Zibibbo, Passito and the wines of a windswept island

Pantelleria wine comes from Pantelleria, a volcanic island in the Strait of Sicily. It is best understood as both a place and a regulated Italian wine designation: **Pantelleria DOC**.

The island is especially associated with **Zibibbo**, the local name commonly used for Muscat of Alexandria, and with aromatic sweet wines such as **Moscato di Pantelleria** and **Passito di Pantelleria**. But Pantelleria wine is not one single style. The DOC also includes sparkling, white, sweet and fortified categories.

## Highlights

- Pantelleria is an Italian island administratively belonging to Sicily and Trapani Province.
- The Pantelleria DOC covers the entire island.
- Zibibbo is the principal grape associated with the designation.
- Moscato di Pantelleria is a sweet, aromatic wine made from fresh Zibibbo grapes.
- Passito di Pantelleria uses grapes dried wholly or partly before fermentation.
- “Liquoroso” identifies a fortified category; it does not describe every Pantelleria wine.
- The island’s fierce winds, low rainfall and traditional low-trained vines make viticulture unusually labour-intensive.
- Zibibbo on a label does not, by itself, prove that a wine comes from Pantelleria.

## Where is Pantelleria?

Pantelleria lies south-west of Sicily and east of Tunisia, in the Strait of Sicily. It is part of Italy’s autonomous region of Sicily and belongs administratively to Trapani Province.

The island’s main administrative centre is Pantelleria town, also known as the comune of Pantelleria. That town is a useful orientation point, but it does not define the whole wine area: the Pantelleria DOC covers the entire island.

For an Australian learner, Palermo is the most useful major-city reference on Sicily. Pantelleria is not connected to mainland Italy by road; it is reached by air or sea. A combined road-and-ferry journey from Palermo may take roughly 3.5 to 5 hours or more, depending on the port, sailing and connections. This is only a broad orientation, not a fixed travel time.

Pantelleria is separate from other Sicilian wine areas, including **Marsala DOC** in the wider Trapani area. Sicily is the broader region; Pantelleria is the more specific island origin.

## Is Pantelleria wine a place, a grape or a style?

It can refer to all three ideas, but they should not be confused.

**Pantelleria** is the island and geographic origin. **Pantelleria DOC** is the regulated Italian designation for wines made under the applicable rules across the island. **Zibibbo** is the principal grape variety.

Zibibbo is also known as Muscat of Alexandria or Moscato d’Alessandria. The grape is used beyond Pantelleria, including elsewhere in Sicily, so the word “Zibibbo” alone is not a guarantee of island origin.

The official specification requires the DOC wines to be made exclusively from Zibibbo, with an exception for Pantelleria Bianco. That wine may contain up to 15 per cent of other white grape varieties suitable for cultivation in Sicily.

The name **Moscato di Pantelleria**, meanwhile, refers to a wine type, not simply to the grape synonym Moscato d’Alessandria.

## What styles are made?

The Pantelleria DOC includes eight named types:

- Moscato di Pantelleria
- Passito di Pantelleria
- Pantelleria-Moscato spumante
- Pantelleria-Moscato dorato
- Pantelleria-Moscato liquoroso
- Pantelleria-Passito liquoroso
- Pantelleria-Zibibbo dolce
- Pantelleria-Bianco, including Frizzante

In practical terms, the most important distinction is between fresh-grape wines, dried-grape wines and fortified wines.

### Moscato di Pantelleria

Moscato di Pantelleria is made from fresh Zibibbo grapes. It is described in the specification as sweet and aromatic, with a recognisable Moscato character. Expect a wine that is fragrant and generous rather than a dry, neutral white.

### Passito di Pantelleria

Passito means that grapes have been dried, either on the vine or after harvest, wholly or partly before fermentation. Drying removes water and concentrates the grape material. In Pantelleria, Passito is associated with sweet, aromatic wines that can show golden to amber colour and concentrated dried-grape character.

Passito does **not** automatically mean fortified. The DOC separately identifies Passito liquoroso, which is a fortified category.

### White, sparkling and fortified wines

Pantelleria also produces white and lightly sparkling expressions. Pantelleria Bianco and Pantelleria Bianco Frizzante need not be sweet, which is an important corrective to the idea that every wine from the island is a dessert wine.

The DOC includes Moscato dorato and liquoroso categories as well. In Italian wine language, **spumante** indicates sparkling wine, **frizzante** indicates a lightly sparkling style, and **liquoroso** identifies a fortified category made under its relevant rules.

For a broader explanation of sweetness, see WineDaddy’s guide to [sweet wine](/what-is-sweet-wine/). If the label says liquoroso, the distinction is closer to [fortified wine](/what-is-fortified-wine/) than to an ordinary sweet white.

## Why is growing grapes difficult there?

Pantelleria’s natural conditions are demanding. The official specification describes a volcanic island with low rainfall, a summer dry period of about five months and winds recorded on average on 338 days each year. Average annual rainfall is approximately 409 millimetres.

Those conditions help explain the island’s traditional vine shape. The **vite ad alberello**, or head-trained bush vine, is kept low to the ground in a hollow. The hollow helps shelter the vine from wind and retain limited moisture. Terraces and lava-stone walls are also part of the island’s viticultural landscape.

This is not a claim that volcanic soil automatically creates a particular flavour. Wine character also depends on the grape, harvest timing, drying, fermentation, maturation and many decisions made during production. Geography sets the conditions; it does not write the tasting note by itself.

UNESCO recognised Pantelleria’s traditional alberello cultivation as intangible cultural heritage in 2014. The practice includes manual work and hand harvesting, and is maintained through practical knowledge passed between generations.

## How should an Australian buyer read the label?

Look first for the relationship between the place, the wine type and the grape.

- **Moscato di Pantelleria DOC** means a regulated Pantelleria Moscato wine.
- **Passito di Pantelleria DOC** points to a dried-grape wine.
- **Liquoroso** signals a fortified category.
- **Pantelleria Bianco** may be a still white, while **Frizzante** indicates light sparkle.
- **Sicilia** is a broader geographic name than Pantelleria.
- **Zibibbo** identifies the grape, but not necessarily the island.

“Moscato” does not automatically mean sparkling or low in alcohol. “Passito” does not automatically mean fortified. And “Pantelleria wine” does not automatically mean sweet: the designation includes white and sparkling expressions as well.

The safest expectation is to treat the specific wording on the label as important. A Passito, a Moscato, a liquoroso and a Bianco may all come from the same island but offer very different experiences.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Pantelleria wine always sweet?

No. Sweet and dried-grape wines are central to Pantelleria’s identity, but the DOC also includes white and sparkling styles that need not be sweet.

### Is Pantelleria wine fortified?

Not always. Pantelleria has liquoroso, or fortified, categories, but standard Moscato and Passito categories should not automatically be described as fortified.

### Is Zibibbo the same as Pantelleria?

No. Zibibbo is the principal grape associated with Pantelleria, but it is also used in other places. Pantelleria is the island; Pantelleria DOC is the regulated designation.

### What does Passito mean?

Passito refers to wine made with grapes that have been dried wholly or partly before fermentation. In Pantelleria, it is associated with sweet, aromatic wines. Fortification is a separate question.

### What makes Pantelleria distinctive?

Its combination of island geography, intense wind, low rainfall, volcanic terrain and traditional low-trained bush vines gives Pantelleria a distinctive viticultural context. It is the relationship between those conditions and the island’s regulated wine traditions—not one guaranteed flavour—that makes the place worth understanding.
