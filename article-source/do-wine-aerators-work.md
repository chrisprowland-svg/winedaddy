# Do wine aerators work?

## Highlights

- Wine aerators can make some wines smell or taste different.
- A noticeable change is not automatically an improvement.
- Swirling already exposes wine to some air, without special equipment.
- Decanting can aerate wine and help separate sediment; an aerator generally cannot.
- Young, robust wines are reasonable candidates for a trial.
- Delicate or mature wines may lose attractive aromas with rapid exposure.
- The easiest test is to compare a small aerated pour with an unaerated one.

## Quick answer

Yes—wine aerators can work in the limited sense that they increase a wine’s contact with air and may create a perceptible change. But they do not reliably make every wine better, and they are not essential equipment.

An aerator is usually a pour-through device. As wine passes through it, the wine–air contact may affect aroma expression, flavour and the perception of texture or balance. The result depends on the wine, its condition, how much air it receives and the device itself.

So “working” does not mean “guaranteed improvement”. It means the wine may become different. Sometimes that difference is more expressive; sometimes it is less fresh; sometimes you may notice nothing at all.

## What does an aerator do?

Aeration exposes wine to air. Oxygen can participate in chemical changes in wine and can influence the release or perception of volatile aroma compounds—the compounds that contribute to what you smell.

A Cornell study of two commercial wine-aeration devices found that aerated and non-aerated red-wine samples could differ in chemical and sensory measures. However, it examined particular devices and a particular wine, so it does not prove that every aerator will improve every bottle.

An aerator also cannot recreate years of bottle ageing. Nor does it reliably remove tannins, reduce acidity or “fix” a wine that is faulty or already oxidised.

## Aerator, swirling or decanter?

**Swirling** is the simplest form of aeration. Rotating wine in a glass increases its exposed surface and mixes it with air. It costs nothing and lets you observe the wine gradually.

**An aerator** is mainly a quick, convenient way to expose wine to air while pouring. It may be useful when you want to serve wine immediately and see whether a particular bottle responds.

**A decanter** transfers wine into another vessel. This can provide broader air exposure and, when done carefully, separate wine from sediment. An aerator is not a sediment-removal tool.

No method is universally best. An aerator prioritises speed, swirling provides gradual exposure in the glass, and decanting offers aeration plus the possibility of sediment separation.

## Which wines might respond?

Young, bold or concentrated red wines are reasonable candidates for an aerator trial, particularly when you want a quick result. This is a practical starting point, not a rule: some such wines will change noticeably and others will not.

Delicate or mature wines deserve more caution. Their lighter aromas may fade with rapid or excessive exposure. Rather than aerating the whole bottle immediately, try a small amount in a glass first.

Some white wines can also respond to air, so it is too broad to say that aeration is only for reds. There is no need to treat an aerator as mandatory for any style, and this article does not make a general recommendation about sparkling wine.

## Try a before-and-after tasting

You can test an aerator without committing the whole bottle:

1. Pour small amounts into two glasses.
2. Leave one glass unchanged.
3. Pass the other pour through the aerator.
4. Taste them side by side.
5. Taste again after a short interval.

Look for whether the aerated wine seems more aromatic, less fresh, more balanced, simply different—or unchanged. If you prefer the unaerated sample, you have your answer for that bottle.

## Common questions

### Can an aerator fix bad wine?

No. Aeration is not a reliable way to correct faults or reverse oxidation. If a wine smells spoiled, overly oxidised or otherwise faulty, exposing it to more air is not a guaranteed rescue.

### Do aerators soften tannins?

Do not treat this as a guaranteed effect. Aeration may change how a wine’s structure is perceived, but it does not simply remove tannins from the wine.

### Is an aerator better than a decanter?

Not generally. An aerator is faster and more convenient. A decanter can provide aeration and help separate sediment. The useful choice depends on the wine, the time available and what you want the serving method to do.

### Do I need to buy an aerator?

No. Swirling the wine in a glass already introduces some air, and you can compare the wine before deciding whether more aeration is useful. An aerator is optional equipment, not a requirement for enjoying wine.
