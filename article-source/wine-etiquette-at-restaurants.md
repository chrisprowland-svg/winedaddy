# Wine etiquette at restaurants: a practical guide for Australian diners

Restaurant wine etiquette is not a test of sophistication. It is a set of useful conventions that helps diners, staff and other guests enjoy the meal comfortably.

You can ask for help with the wine list, mention your budget, choose wine by the glass if it is available, and ask questions in plain English. When a bottle is opened, the tasting pour is generally a check that the wine is the bottle you ordered and appears sound—not a test of whether you have the “right” opinion about it.

If something seems wrong, speak up promptly and politely. Restaurant procedures vary, from casual service to formal dining, so think of etiquette as helpful guidance rather than a book of rigid rules.

## Highlights

- You do not need specialist wine knowledge to ask for help.
- Giving staff a price range is practical, not embarrassing.
- Check the bottle before it is opened, especially the producer, wine, vintage and size.
- The tasting pour is mainly for checking the wine’s condition and identity.
- The person who orders or pays does not universally have to be the person who tastes.
- A wine you dislike is not necessarily faulty, but a suspected problem is worth raising.
- Australian licensed venues have responsible-service obligations that are separate from personal etiquette.

## Start with the wine list

A restaurant wine list may organise wines by colour, style, variety, region, country or price. You do not need to understand every heading before making a choice.

Start with what you enjoy or would like to try. You might say:

- “Could you suggest something fresh and light?”
- “I’d prefer a red that isn’t too heavy or tannic.”
- “What do you have by the glass?”
- “Could you recommend something around this price?”
- “Is there a dry white that would work with our meal?”

It is also useful to mention how much wine you need. A bottle may make sense for a group, while wine by the glass may suit a shorter meal or a table with different preferences.

Being clear about your budget helps the server or sommelier suggest relevant options. It is not a challenge to their expertise, and you do not need to apologise for it.

## Place the order clearly

Before ordering, confirm the details that matter:

- the producer or winery name;
- the wine’s name or style;
- the variety, if needed;
- the vintage, where relevant;
- whether you want a glass or bottle;
- the bottle size and quantity.

This is particularly helpful when several wines have similar names. Restaurant lists can also change, and an online list may not exactly match what is available on the day.

When the bottle arrives, look at the label before it is opened. If it is not the wine you ordered, say so straight away. This is easier to resolve before the seal is removed.

## Who tastes the wine?

A server will often offer the first taste to the person who ordered the bottle. That is a common service convention, not a universal rule about who has authority at the table.

The person who tastes might be:

- the person who chose the wine;
- the host;
- the person paying;
- another guest who feels comfortable checking it; or
- simply whoever the table and server agree on.

If you ordered the bottle but would rather someone else taste it, you can say, “Could you please ask them to check it?” No technical performance is required. You do not need to swirl dramatically, name aromas or give a speech.

## What is the tasting pour for?

The small tasting pour is generally a check of three things:

1. Is this the bottle that was ordered?
2. Does the wine appear sound?
3. Is there an obvious problem, such as a fault or poor condition?

It is not usually a chance to reject a correctly supplied bottle simply because you have changed your mind or do not enjoy the style you selected. Policies about returns and substitutions vary between venues.

That distinction matters. A wine can be unfamiliar, more acidic, more tannic or less fruity than you expected without being faulty. On the other hand, a wine that smells noticeably musty, stale or otherwise unusual may deserve a second opinion.

You do not have to diagnose the exact problem. Try saying:

> “I’m not sure this is showing as expected. Could you please check it?”

A server or sommelier can assess the wine. It is better to describe what you notice than to announce a confident technical diagnosis that may be wrong.

## If the bottle is incorrect or seems wrong

Raise an issue early, calmly and clearly.

### The wrong bottle

If the producer, wine, vintage, size or quantity is incorrect, mention it before the bottle is opened. For example:

> “I ordered the 2022, but this looks like the 2023. Could we check the order?”

### A suspected fault

If the wine seems faulty, ask staff to assess it. Terms such as “corked” are sometimes used too broadly; a piece of cork in the wine is not the same thing as cork taint. You do not need to know the difference to ask for help.

### Wine that is too warm or too cold

Serving temperature depends partly on the wine’s style and the venue’s approach. However, if a wine seems excessively warm or cold, it is reasonable to ask whether it can be adjusted.

The response might be to chill it, let it warm slightly, decant it, explain the intended service style or replace it if the venue considers that appropriate. A replacement is not automatically guaranteed in every situation.

## Opening, corks, glasses and decanting

Restaurants differ in how formally they serve wine. A bottle may be opened at the table or nearby. The cork may be shown, but you are not required to smell it or perform an inspection. The wine itself is the useful evidence of how the bottle is showing.

Glassware also varies. A venue may use different shapes for different styles, while a casual restaurant may use one practical glass for several wines. You do not need to understand glass anatomy to order confidently.

Decanting is another service choice rather than an automatic ritual. It may help separate sediment or allow some wines to open up, but not every red wine needs to be decanted. You can ask:

> “Would you recommend decanting this, or is it best served from the bottle?”

Refills are not automatic either. You can accept a pour, decline one, ask for water or request that staff leave the bottle on the table, depending on the venue’s service style and safety requirements.

## Etiquette in an Australian restaurant

The same broad principles apply whether you are eating at a casual neighbourhood restaurant or a formal dining room: be clear, considerate and willing to ask.

Licensed venues in Australia also have legal responsibilities that are separate from etiquette. The detailed rules vary between states and territories, but responsible-service obligations can include checking age, refusing service to an intoxicated person and managing unsafe behaviour.

A refusal of service is therefore not simply a matter of whether staff are being polite. Staff may be required to follow liquor laws or licence conditions. Guests should not pressure employees to ignore those obligations.

Considerate behaviour includes accepting a venue’s decision, avoiding pressure on others to drink, respecting nearby diners and treating hospitality staff as professionals rather than performers. Formal wine service is not “more correct” than casual service; it is simply a different setting.

## A simple restaurant checklist

Before the bottle is opened:

- Ask for help if you need it.
- State your preferred style and budget.
- Confirm the wine, vintage where relevant, size and quantity.
- Check the label.

At the tasting pour:

- Taste calmly.
- Decide whether the wine appears sound and correct.
- Do not reject it merely because it is unfamiliar or not what you hoped.
- Ask staff to check it if something seems genuinely unusual.

During the meal:

- Accept or decline refills as you wish.
- Ask about temperature, glassware or decanting without feeling you need specialist language.
- Respect staff decisions about responsible service.
- Enjoy the wine rather than trying to perform expertise.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I ask a sommelier or server for a wine within my budget?

Yes. A price range is useful information. You can also describe the style you want without naming a grape variety or region.

### Can I reject a wine because I do not like it?

Usually, the tasting pour is not intended as a general approval of personal preference. If the bottle is correct but the style is not what you expected, the venue may not treat that as a fault. Policies vary, so ask politely rather than assuming.

### Must the person who pays taste the wine?

No universal rule requires that. The ordering guest often tastes by convention, but another diner can check the wine if the table prefers.

### Do I have to smell the cork?

No. Cork presentation is a service convention, not an examination you must pass. Pay attention to the bottle and the wine.

### What should I do if the wine seems faulty?

Raise it promptly and politely. Describe what seems unusual and ask the server or sommelier to check it. You do not need to identify the fault yourself.

### Can restaurant staff refuse to serve me wine?

Licensed venues have responsible-service obligations, and the details vary between Australian states and territories. Staff may be required to refuse service in some circumstances, particularly where intoxication or safety is involved.
