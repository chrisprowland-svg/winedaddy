# How to open an old bottle of wine

Opening an older bottle is less about force and more about control. The cork may be fragile, and the wine may contain sediment that you want to leave behind.

Keep the bottle still, clean the neck, choose an opener that suits the cork and pull slowly. If the cork breaks, do not panic: a broken cork does not automatically mean the wine is spoiled or “corked”. If sediment is present, pour gently and taste the wine before deciding whether it needs air.

## Highlights

- Keep an older bottle as still as practical before opening.
- Clean the capsule, cork and bottle rim before you begin.
- A standard waiter’s friend may suit a firm, intact cork.
- An Ah-So, also called a two-pronged opener or butler’s thief, may help with a fragile cork.
- If the cork crumbles, avoid forcing it down straight away.
- Sediment is not automatically a fault, but it can make the wine gritty.
- Decanting is optional: it can remove sediment, but extra oxygen may be too much for a delicate wine.
- Smell and taste the wine before serving it to everyone.

## The careful way to open an old bottle

### 1. Prepare the bottle and tools

If you expect sediment, stand the bottle upright for several hours where practical. This gives the deposits a chance to settle towards the bottom. It is helpful, rather than a guarantee that every particle will stay put.

Have a clean cloth, suitable glasses and a corkscrew ready. If the cork looks vulnerable, a two-pronged opener may be useful. Keep a clean container and an unbleached coffee filter or other suitable neutral filter nearby in case cork fragments fall into the wine.

Older bottles can also show signs of leakage or a damaged capsule. These observations may tell you to take extra care, but they do not prove that the wine is faulty.

### 2. Clean the bottle neck

Cut or remove the capsule below the lip of the bottle. Wipe the top of the cork and the rim with a clean cloth.

This removes dust and residue before the cork is pulled, and makes it easier to see whether the cork is firm, dry, split or already crumbling.

### 3. If the cork looks firm

A folding waiter’s friend—a compact corkscrew commonly used in wine service—may be suitable for an intact cork.

Centre the screw carefully and insert it without pushing sharply. Pull slowly and vertically, avoiding sudden sideways movements. As the cork comes free, ease it out rather than snapping it from the neck.

The aim is not to win a contest with the cork. Slow, steady pressure gives you more control and reduces the chance of tearing it.

### 4. If the cork looks fragile

A two-pronged opener is often called an **Ah-So** or **butler’s thief**. Instead of driving a screw through the cork, its prongs slide between the cork and the bottle neck and grip the cork from the sides.

This can be helpful when a cork looks soft, dry or likely to crumble, but it is not foolproof. The tool requires practice, and the prongs can slip or tear a very soft cork. Use gentle, controlled movements rather than forcing it.

If you are unfamiliar with the tool and the bottle is especially important to you, aggressive experimentation may create more risk than simply seeking help from someone experienced.

## What to do if the cork crumbles or breaks

Stop applying force if the cork begins to disintegrate. Try to remove the remaining section in controlled stages rather than immediately pushing the whole cork into the bottle.

If pieces fall into the wine, remove only what is necessary. You can filter the wine through a clean, neutral filter, such as an unbleached coffee filter, to catch fragments. Filtering directly into a glass may involve less handling than transferring the whole bottle, although the best choice depends on how much cork has entered the wine and how delicate the wine seems.

Extra pouring, filtering and splashing expose wine to more air. That may matter if the wine is fragile, so handle it as little as practical.

A crumbling cork is not the same as cork taint. Cork pieces are a physical nuisance; cork taint is a wine fault associated with musty, damp-cardboard-like aromas and flavours. Judge the wine itself rather than the cork alone.

## How to pour around sediment

Sediment is material that has settled in the bottle. It can be a normal deposit in an older wine, although visible particles can also include cork dust or other deposits.

To leave most of it behind:

1. Keep the bottle still after opening.
2. Pour slowly and steadily.
3. Avoid splashing the wine.
4. Watch the shoulder and neck of the bottle as the wine level falls.
5. Stop pouring when sediment begins moving towards the neck.
6. Leave the final sediment-containing portion in the bottle.

A light behind the bottle can make moving sediment easier to see. You do not need to recover every last drop if doing so sends sediment into the glass.

## Should you decant the wine?

Decanting can have two different purposes: separating wine from sediment and exposing it to oxygen.

Separating sediment may be useful. Giving an old wine lots of air is less predictable. Some wines open up with oxygen, while a delicate wine can lose its character quickly after opening.

Taste a small amount soon after opening. If the wine smells fresh and seems to benefit from air, you can pour it into a decanter and check it regularly. If it seems delicate, tired or vulnerable, pouring directly into glasses may be the gentler choice.

There is no universal rule that every old wine should be decanted—or that it should never see air. The wine’s condition and history matter more than age alone.

## Taste before serving the bottle fully

Smell and taste a small amount before serving everyone.

Look for obvious musty or damp-cardboard-like aromas, which may suggest cork taint. Do not confuse that with a few visible cork fragments. Also notice whether the wine seems fresh, muted or tired. These simple checks are more useful than assuming that an old bottle must be good, bad or ready to drink.

If the wine seems sound, serve it carefully and keep checking how it develops in the glass. If it seems delicate, avoid leaving it exposed to air unnecessarily.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- Do not yank a fragile cork out quickly.
- Do not assume every old bottle requires an Ah-So.
- Do not force a crumbling cork down into the bottle as your first response.
- Do not treat sediment as proof of quality—or proof that the wine is spoiled.
- Do not filter, splash and decant more than the situation requires.
- Do not leave a delicate old wine in a broad decanter without tasting it.
- Do not judge the wine solely by the condition of its cork.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a broken cork a sign that the wine is bad?

No. An old cork can break because it has weakened, but that does not automatically mean the wine is spoiled or corked. Remove or filter fragments where necessary, then smell and taste the wine.

### Is an Ah-So better than a normal corkscrew?

It may be more suitable for a fragile cork because it grips from the sides. A standard corkscrew may be fine for a firm, intact cork. Neither tool is guaranteed to work in every situation.

### Should I always decant an old wine?

No. Decanting can separate sediment, but extra oxygen can be too much for a delicate wine. Taste first and decide whether the wine needs air.

### Can I drink wine with sediment in it?

Sediment is not automatically harmful or a sign of a fault, but it can feel gritty. Pour carefully and leave the sediment-containing portion behind where possible.
