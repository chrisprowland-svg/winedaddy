# How to organise a wine cellar

A well-organised wine cellar does two jobs: it keeps bottles stable and protected, and it helps you find the right bottle without rummaging through everything else.

You do not need an underground cellar or an elaborate computer system. A cupboard, rack, case or wine fridge can work as part of a home collection, provided the space is suitable and your system is easy to maintain.

## Highlights

- Organise bottles for **stability, visibility and easy retrieval**.
- Start by separating wines into **drink soon, hold and review**.
- There is no single correct way to sort wine. Style, grape, region, producer and occasion can all work.
- Record each bottle’s **name, vintage, quantity, location and drinking priority**.
- Keep wine away from direct sunlight, heat and large temperature swings.
- Store natural-cork bottles on their side for long-term holding unless the producer advises otherwise.
- A cellar is a storage and retrieval system—not a guarantee that every wine will improve with age.

## Quick answer: what is the best way to organise a wine cellar?

The most useful approach for many households is to create three or four broad zones:

1. **Drink soon** — bottles intended for near-term drinking.
2. **Hold** — bottles being kept for later.
3. **Review** — bottles with uncertain timing, damaged labels or an unclear place in the collection.
4. **Special or less frequently accessed** — older, fragile or occasion-specific bottles.

Then choose a secondary system within each zone, such as colour, broad style, grape variety, region, producer or occasion.

For example, your “drink soon” section might contain sparkling wine, whites and lighter reds for weeknight meals, while your “hold” section is divided by region or producer. The best system is the one that answers the question you actually ask when choosing a bottle: “What do I want to drink tonight?”

## Check the space before sorting

Organisation cannot compensate for a poor storage environment. Before moving bottles, assess the space for:

- direct sunlight or strong artificial light;
- heat from an oven, hot-water system, heater or roof space;
- substantial day-to-night or seasonal temperature changes;
- unstable shelves or unsupported stacks;
- strong odours, dirt or dampness;
- awkward access that could make bottles difficult or unsafe to remove.

Australian homes vary widely. An internal cupboard, garage, shed, under-bench wine fridge and purpose-built cellar can all provide different conditions. An unconditioned garage or shed may become very hot or fluctuate significantly during summer, while a cupboard may be more stable—but not if it sits beside a heat source.

Judge the actual space rather than relying on its name. Detailed storage-temperature guidance belongs in a dedicated storage guide; the central principle here is to choose the coolest practical location with the least fluctuation and to keep wine away from light and heat.

## A practical setup sequence

### 1. Measure the space and its capacity

Note how many bottles each shelf, rack, case or fridge section can safely hold. Leave enough room to read labels and remove bottles without disturbing neighbouring ones.

Do not build tall, unstable stacks simply to increase capacity. A slightly smaller collection that is easy to inspect is more useful than a crowded one.

### 2. Inspect and group the bottles

Bring bottles together and check what you have. Group obvious categories such as sparkling, white, rosé, red and fortified wine if that helps you see the collection.

At this stage, set aside bottles with damaged labels, leaking closures, broken capsules or uncertain identities. A warning sign is not automatically proof that a wine is ruined, but it is a reason to inspect it and decide whether it needs attention.

### 3. Separate drinking priorities

Create your main zones: drink soon, hold and review. These are household planning categories, not guaranteed drinking windows.

A vintage can help identify a bottle, but it does not automatically tell you when the wine should be opened. If a producer’s guidance or your own tasting history gives you a useful drinking note, record it. Otherwise, use cautious language such as “check soon” or “open when convenient”.

### 4. Choose one primary sorting method

Within each zone, choose the method that makes retrieval easiest.

**By colour or broad style** is intuitive and useful when choosing wine for a meal. Its limitation is that it does not show which bottle should be opened first.

**By grape variety** is helpful if you enjoy comparing styles such as Shiraz, Chardonnay or Riesling. Blends and wines that do not prominently name a variety can be harder to place.

**By region** suits anyone learning about Australian or international wine geography. However, region alone does not tell you a wine’s drinking priority.

**By producer** works well when you regularly buy from a small number of producers, but it can become awkward in a mixed collection.

**By occasion**—such as weeknight, entertaining, sparkling or special occasions—can be very practical in a small home cellar. The trade-off is that one bottle may fit several categories.

For most beginners, use **drinking priority first**, then add one secondary system only if it genuinely saves time.

### 5. Give each location a simple code

Label shelves, racks or fridge sections with codes such as A1, A2 and B1. The code should appear in your inventory as well as on the physical location.

Keep frequently used bottles where they are easy to reach. Place older, fragile or sediment-bearing bottles where routine retrieval is less likely to disturb them. Keep drink-soon bottles visible rather than burying them behind bottles intended for long-term holding.

Bottle position should take account of the closure. Natural-cork bottles are generally stored on their side for long-term holding because extended upright storage can allow the cork to dry and become more susceptible to oxygen permeation. Screwcap bottles do not rely on cork moisture in the same way and can generally be stored upright. Unusual closures and producer instructions may require different handling.

## Create a lightweight inventory

Your inventory does not need to become a second job. Start with these five fields:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Wine | Producer and wine name |
| Vintage | Vintage year, or non-vintage |
| Quantity | Number of bottles |
| Location | Shelf, rack, case or fridge code |
| Priority | Drink soon, hold or review |

You can add variety or style, region or country, acquisition date, source, intended drinking note and comments if those details will help you.

For Australian-labelled wines, vintage, variety and geographical indication may be useful identification fields where they appear on the label. Accurate records make it easier to find a bottle and understand what you own; they do not predict whether a wine will improve with age.

## Make mixed collections and small spaces manageable

A small collection does not need a complex taxonomy. Three clearly labelled zones may be enough. You could use one shelf for drink-soon wines, one for bottles being held and a small box or section for review.

Cases can function as storage zones if they are stable, dry, accessible and not overloaded. Record the case location rather than expecting to remember what is inside it.

In a wine fridge, use shelves or sections as location codes. Keep the system simple enough that adding or removing one bottle does not require a complete reorganisation. A wine fridge can provide a controlled storage environment, but detailed appliance-selection and temperature advice are separate topics.

## Review the cellar regularly

Wine collections change. Bottles are opened, moved, gifted, forgotten or reassessed.

A light review every few months and a more complete review at least annually can help you check:

- whether the inventory matches the bottles present;
- whether bottles are in the recorded locations;
- whether labels, capsules or closures are damaged;
- whether the storage space has developed heat, light, dampness or access problems;
- which bottles deserve attention soon;
- whether the sorting system still suits your household.

The review is also a chance to move bottles into more useful positions. A wine you bought for a future occasion may now be the ideal bottle for an ordinary Friday night.

## Organisation is not the same as ageing

Organising a cellar helps you store and retrieve wine. It does not make a wine age well, and not every bottle is intended for long-term holding.

For the difference between storage and ageing, see [Does wine improve with age?](/does-wine-improve-with-age/) and [What makes wine ageworthy](/what-makes-wine-ageworthy/). For more on the changes that happen in a sealed bottle, see [Bottle ageing](/bottle-ageing/).

A good cellar system should leave you with fewer forgotten bottles, fewer unnecessary searches and a clearer answer to the most important question: what would you enjoy opening next?

## Suggested FAQs

### Should all wine bottles be stored lying down?

No. Natural-cork bottles are generally stored on their side for long-term holding, but screwcap bottles can generally be stored upright. Closure type, bottle format and producer guidance matter.

### Is a garage suitable for storing wine?

It depends on the actual conditions. An unconditioned garage may experience heat and temperature fluctuation, particularly during an Australian summer. Assess the space rather than assuming that every garage is either suitable or unsuitable.

### What is the simplest wine-cellar organisation system?

Separate bottles into drink soon, hold and review. Add a shelf or location code and record the wine name, vintage, quantity and location.

### Does an older bottle belong in the long-term section?

Not automatically. Age alone does not prove that a wine will improve with further cellaring. Use available producer information and your own records, and place uncertain bottles in a review section.

### Do I need an app to manage a wine cellar?

No. A notebook, spreadsheet or simple list can work. The best inventory is the one you will keep accurate.
