# Ghemme: a beginner’s guide to this Piedmont red wine

## Highlights

- Ghemme is a protected Italian red-wine designation, not simply a town name or grape variety.
- It comes from Alto Piemonte, in the northern part of Piedmont.
- The defined production area covers specified parts of Ghemme and Romagnano Sesia, in Novara province.
- Nebbiolo—locally called Spanna—is the principal grape.
- Vespolina and Uva Rara may be included in a combined proportion of up to 15%.
- Ghemme wines are generally dry, savoury and structured, with acidity and tannin playing important roles.
- Ghemme is separate from Barolo and Barbaresco, even though all are Piedmont designations associated with Nebbiolo.
- On an Australian wine label, Ghemme tells you about regulated geographic origin and production—not automatically about price, quality or vintage character.

## Quick answer: what should you understand about Ghemme?

Ghemme is a **DOCG red-wine designation from Alto Piemonte**, the northern part of Italy’s Piedmont region. DOCG means *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*—Controlled and Guaranteed Designation of Origin. At European Union level, Ghemme is also recognised as a protected designation of origin, or PDO.

The important distinction is simple: **Ghemme is the place-based designation; Nebbiolo is the main grape**. The current rules require Nebbiolo, locally known as Spanna, to make up 85–100% of the wine. Vespolina and Uva Rara may be used together for the remaining proportion, up to 15%.

Ghemme is not made across all of northern Piedmont. Its defined production area is limited to specified parts of the municipalities of **Ghemme and Romagnano Sesia**, in the province of Novara. That means the name refers to a regulated area and set of production conditions, rather than every vineyard around the town of Ghemme.

Expect a dry, savoury red with the firm shape that Nebbiolo can provide: noticeable acidity, tannin and aromatic development. The official description refers to a distinctive, fine and complex aroma, a harmonious palate and a pleasantly bitter finish. Individual wines can still vary with site, vintage, producer choices and bottle age.

## Where is Ghemme?

Ghemme sits in **Piedmont, in north-western Italy**, within the broader Alto Piemonte wine area. It is in Novara province, north-west of Milan and north-east of Turin, near the Sesia valley and the Alpine foothills leading towards Monte Rosa.

The useful local reference points are:

- **Ghemme**, the namesake municipality;
- **Romagnano Sesia**, which also contains part of the official production area;
- **Novara**, the nearest useful provincial and regional centre;
- **Valsesia**, a nearby valley that helps orient the landscape but is not another name for the Ghemme appellation.

From Milan, Ghemme is roughly 90–110 kilometres north-west by road. That is approximately 1 hour 15 minutes to 1 hour 45 minutes by car, depending on the route, traffic and starting point. Treat this as geographic orientation, not a fixed travel promise.

The hierarchy is therefore:

**Italy → Piedmont → Alto Piemonte → Novara province → Ghemme DOCG**

Alto Piemonte is the broader regional context. Ghemme is the specific regulated designation.

## What grapes are used in Ghemme?

Nebbiolo is the foundation of Ghemme. In the local terminology used by the specification, it is also called **Spanna**. This does not mean Ghemme and Nebbiolo are interchangeable terms.

A useful way to remember the relationship is:

- **Nebbiolo or Spanna:** the principal grape;
- **Ghemme:** the regulated place name and wine designation;
- **Vespolina and Uva Rara:** permitted complementary varieties, up to 15% combined.

For a fuller explanation of the grape itself, see [What is Nebbiolo?](/what-is-nebbiolo/). Ghemme adds something that a grape name alone cannot: rules about where the grapes come from and how the wine is made.

## What does Ghemme wine taste like?

Ghemme is a red wine, usually understood through its structure as much as its fruit. **Acidity** gives the wine freshness and lift, while **tannin** can create a drying, firm sensation across the gums. These features may be especially noticeable in a Nebbiolo-led wine, although the exact balance varies.

The official description supports a profile that is:

- dry rather than sweet;
- savoury and harmonious;
- aromatic, fine and potentially complex;
- ruby red, with the colour tending towards garnet as the wine develops;
- marked by a pleasantly bitter finish.

It is safer to treat these as useful expectations than guarantees. A Ghemme from a warmer or cooler vintage, a younger or older bottle, or a different maturation approach may show quite differently.

The designation includes **Ghemme** and **Ghemme Riserva**. Riserva is a regulated category with longer maturation requirements, so it should not be read simply as a marketing adjective. The longer period can contribute to a more developed presentation, but it does not mean every Riserva will taste better to every drinker.

## How do place and climate shape Ghemme?

The vineyards are described as hilly, generally between **220 and 400 metres above sea level**. The specification also identifies clayey, sandy, silty and stony soils, with the wider Alto Piemonte landscape associated with glacial and alluvial deposits.

These details help explain why Ghemme should not be treated as identical to every other Piedmont wine area. Elevation, exposure, drainage, soil variation and Alpine influence can affect how grapes ripen and how wines develop.

They do not, however, produce one guaranteed flavour. It is too strong to say that a particular soil automatically gives every Ghemme a mineral taste. Place provides conditions and possibilities; the final wine also reflects the vintage, vineyard, winemaking and maturation.

## Ghemme, Piedmont, Barolo and Barbaresco: what is the difference?

**Piedmont** is the broad Italian region. Ghemme is one specific designation within it, in Alto Piemonte.

**Barolo** and **Barbaresco** are also separate Piedmont designations associated with Nebbiolo, but they come from different defined areas and follow their own production specifications. Ghemme is not a sub-zone of either wine, and Barolo or Barbaresco is not another name for Ghemme.

The useful comparison is geographic and regulatory, not a ranking:

- all three are regulated Piedmont wine designations;
- Nebbiolo is central to each;
- Ghemme belongs to Alto Piemonte;
- Barolo and Barbaresco are associated with the Langhe;
- each name refers to its own defined origin and production rules.

For broader regional context, read [Piedmont](/piedmont/), then return to Ghemme’s more specific identity.

## How should an Australian reader read a Ghemme label?

If you encounter Ghemme on an Australian restaurant list, specialist retailer page or imported bottle, read it first as a **regulated geographic designation**. It is broadly comparable, as a learning idea, to understanding that an Australian geographic indication communicates regulated origin—but Italian DOCG and Australian GI systems are not identical legal categories.

Look for these separate pieces of information:

- **Ghemme:** the appellation or protected geographic name;
- **Ghemme Riserva:** the regulated longer-matured category;
- **vintage:** the year of grape production, which is required under the current specification;
- **producer and bottler:** who made and bottled the wine;
- **importer:** the Australian business that brought it into the market;
- **Vigna:** an additional vineyard or place indication where permitted, not a separate appellation.

The name alone does not tell you the wine’s price, producer reputation, current availability in Australia or whether that particular vintage will suit your taste. It also does not mean the wine is made from 100% Nebbiolo.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Ghemme a grape?

No. Ghemme is a regulated geographic wine designation. Nebbiolo, locally called Spanna, is its principal grape.

### Is Ghemme the same as Barolo?

No. Both are separate Piedmont designations associated with Nebbiolo, but Ghemme comes from Alto Piemonte and has its own defined area and production rules.

### Is Ghemme only made in the town of Ghemme?

No. The official production area includes specified parts of Ghemme and Romagnano Sesia. The legal area is not automatically the whole of either municipality.

### Is Ghemme always 100% Nebbiolo?

No. Nebbiolo must form 85–100% of the wine. Vespolina and Uva Rara may make up the remaining proportion, to a combined maximum of 15%.

### Should Ghemme be served like a big red wine?

It is a structured, dry red that can benefit from thoughtful serving, but the right approach depends on the individual bottle and its age. Read the label and consider the wine’s acidity, tannin and development rather than assuming every Ghemme will have exactly the same profile.
