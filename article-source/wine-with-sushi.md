# Wine with sushi: how to choose a bottle that works

Sushi is not one single pairing. A delicate piece of nigiri, a rich salmon roll, a soy-heavy maki, a spicy mayonnaise roll and tempura all put different demands on wine.

For a reliable starting point, choose a **fresh, high-acid wine with moderate alcohol, moderate body and restrained oak**. Australian sparkling wine, dry Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Gris/Grigio and light, unoaked Chardonnay are all useful options. Then adjust for the strongest flavour in the complete bite: the fish, vinegared rice, soy sauce, wasabi, chilli, creamy sauce or fried coating.

## Highlights

- Sushi is a broad category, so there is no single best wine for every plate.
- Fresh whites and sparkling wines are the safest starting points for many sushi styles.
- Acidity helps a wine stay lively alongside salty, rich or fried elements.
- A touch of sweetness can work well with chilli or spicy mayonnaise.
- Soy sauce, seaweed and other umami-rich ingredients can make firm tannins seem more bitter.
- Heavy oak, high alcohol and powerful reds can overwhelm delicate fish.
- For a mixed platter, prioritise versatility rather than trying to match every piece separately.
- Match the wine to the complete bite, not simply to the fact that the fish is raw.

## Quick answer: what wine goes with sushi?

Start with a **fresh, relatively light white or sparkling wine**. Australian sparkling wine, dry Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Gris/Grigio and light unoaked Chardonnay are practical choices.

For simple nigiri with delicate fish, look for freshness and restraint. For oily fish or richer rolls, choose a wine with more acidity and enough flavour to avoid seeming thin. For chilli or spicy mayonnaise, try a wine with some sweetness and freshness, such as an off-dry Riesling. For tempura or fried rolls, sparkling wine and fresh whites provide useful contrast to the richness.

A light, low-tannin Pinot Noir or young Grenache can sometimes work with strongly flavoured fish, such as fatty tuna, especially when soy sauce is involved. It is a conditional option, not the safest general answer.

The best choice depends on the preparation. As with any food pairing, [how wine pairing works](/how-wine-pairing-works/) matters more than memorising a list of varieties.

## Why sushi needs more than one pairing rule

Sushi usually combines seasoned vinegared rice with ingredients such as fish, seafood, vegetables, egg, seaweed and sauces. In Australian restaurants and takeaway shops, the menu may also include sashimi, rolls, tempura and fried or mayonnaise-based preparations.

That means “raw fish” is only one part of the picture. Vinegar in the rice can make a wine’s freshness especially noticeable. Fish may be delicate or oily. Soy sauce adds salt and savoury umami, while wasabi and chilli add pungency and heat. Tempura adds batter, oil and crunch.

The strongest flavour or texture can change from one piece to the next. This is why a wine that feels perfect with plain nigiri may seem less comfortable beside a sweet, spicy or fried roll.

## The best wine styles to try

### Sparkling wine

Sparkling wine is one of the most versatile choices for a mixed sushi platter. Its acidity keeps the wine fresh, while its bubbles add texture alongside salty, rich or fried elements.

It can suit simple sushi, sashimi and tempura, although a sweet sauce or very spicy filling may still become the dominant flavour. Australian sparkling wine is a useful local style to look for without needing to choose a particular producer.

### Riesling

Dry Riesling is a strong choice when you want brightness and freshness. Its acidity can work with vinegared rice, seafood and salty accompaniments, while its aromatic character gives it enough flavour to remain present.

If the sushi includes chilli or spicy mayonnaise, an off-dry Riesling—with a little noticeable sweetness—may be more comfortable. Slight sweetness can soften the impression of chilli heat, while acidity stops the wine from feeling heavy. Remember that Australian Riesling is not automatically sweet: check the individual wine’s style.

### Sauvignon Blanc

Sauvignon Blanc can work particularly well when the sushi includes herbs, vegetables, fried elements or assertive sauces. Choose a fresh style rather than one dominated by oak or alcohol.

Its aromatic character may be more noticeable than that of a delicate white, so it is worth matching the intensity of the wine to the food. A very powerful Sauvignon Blanc can distract from subtle fish.

### Pinot Gris or Pinot Grigio

Pinot Gris/Grigio offers a useful middle ground: generally fresh enough for seafood, but with enough texture for rolls and fried elements. The style varies considerably, so use the wine’s actual weight and freshness as your guide rather than relying only on the name on the label.

### Light, unoaked Chardonnay

A light, unoaked Chardonnay can suit sushi when you want a little more body without strong oak flavours. This can be helpful with richer fish, creamy sauces or a platter that includes several different textures.

Heavily oaked Chardonnay is a risk with delicate sushi because oak and richness can dominate the meal. That does not make all Chardonnay unsuitable; it means the wine’s style matters.

## Match the wine to the sushi

### Delicate nigiri and simple maki

For delicate white fish or simple rolls, choose a clean, fresh white or sparkling wine with moderate alcohol. Dry Riesling, light Chardonnay and sparkling wine are sensible starting points.

Keep the wine’s intensity in proportion to the food. Very rich, heavily oaked or highly alcoholic wine can make a subtle piece of fish seem smaller by comparison.

### Oily fish and fatty tuna

Oily fish needs a wine with enough acidity and flavour to stay lively. Sparkling wine, a fresh fuller-bodied white or a light, low-tannin red can all be considered.

A soft, savoury Pinot Noir or young Grenache may work with strongly flavoured red sashimi and soy sauce. The red should be light and low in tannin; firm, drying tannins are more likely to clash with savoury umami.

### Soy sauce, seaweed and umami

Soy sauce and seaweed bring salt and umami—the savoury taste associated with foods such as soy and seaweed. Umami can make wines with noticeable tannin seem more bitter and less fruity.

That is why fresh whites, sparkling wines and low-tannin reds are usually more comfortable than powerful, structured reds. Wine does not chemically “neutralise” soy sauce or umami; rather, the food and wine can change how each is perceived.

For more on this interaction, see [umami and wine](/umami-and-wine/).

### Wasabi, chilli and spicy mayonnaise

Wasabi and chilli are both hot, pungent ingredients, but they are not identical. The strongest evidence for sweetness helping concerns chilli heat, so apply the idea to wasabi cautiously.

With chilli or spicy mayonnaise, try a wine with slight sweetness, freshness and moderate alcohol. Avoid assuming that a very dry, high-alcohol or tannic wine will be the most refreshing choice.

With wasabi, keep the wine fresh, light to medium in body and moderate in alcohol. How much wasabi is used matters greatly.

### Tempura and fried rolls

Tempura changes the pairing because batter and oil add richness. Sparkling wine, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Gris/Grigio and fresh Chardonnay are useful places to start.

The sauce may matter as much as the fried coating. A sweet or spicy sauce can shift the ideal wine towards a style with more sweetness and freshness, while a simple fried piece may favour acidity and bubbles.

## What to choose for a mixed platter

No single bottle will perfectly match every piece on a mixed sushi platter. Instead, choose a wine that is:

- fresh and high in acidity;
- moderate in alcohol and body;
- not strongly tannic;
- lightly oaked or unoaked;
- flavourful enough for the richest piece without overwhelming the delicate ones.

Sparkling wine, dry Riesling or a fresh Sauvignon Blanc are practical defaults. If the platter contains a lot of chilli or sweet sauce, consider a wine with some sweetness. If it is mostly delicate nigiri, choose restraint.

A useful restaurant question is: **“Which fresh white or sparkling wine would work with a mixed sushi platter that includes both raw and fried pieces?”** That gives the sommelier or server the information they need without requiring you to know every wine term.

## Serving the wine

Serve fresh whites and sparkling wines chilled, but not so cold that their aroma disappears. If the wine is too cold, let it warm slightly in the glass.

A regular white-wine glass is sufficient. You do not need specialised glassware to enjoy wine with sushi. For more detail, see [wine serving temperature explained](/wine-serving-temperature-explained/).

Sake is another beverage commonly considered with Japanese food, but it is not the subject of this guide. The useful wine lesson is the same: preparation, intensity, seasoning and texture matter more than a single universal rule.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is white wine always best with sushi?

No. Fresh whites and sparkling wines are the safest general starting points, but a light, low-tannin Pinot Noir or young Grenache can work with strongly flavoured or oily fish. The preparation and the wine’s structure matter more than its colour alone.

### Does Riesling with sushi need to be sweet?

No. Dry Riesling is a useful choice for many sushi styles. A slightly sweet or off-dry Riesling may be more comfortable with chilli or spicy mayonnaise, but not every sushi dish calls for sweetness.

### Can red wine work with sushi?

It can, particularly with richer fish, but choose a light, fresh, low-tannin red. Avoid making a firm, heavily tannic red the default for delicate sushi, soy sauce or wasabi.

### What is the safest choice for a sushi platter?

Choose sparkling wine, dry Riesling or another fresh white with moderate alcohol, moderate body and restrained oak. These styles are versatile, though no wine will match every piece equally.

### Should wine be served very cold with sushi?

Fresh whites and sparkling wines benefit from being chilled, but extreme cold can mute aroma and flavour. Serve them chilled and allow the wine to warm slightly if it seems closed.
