# What is green harvesting?

## Highlights

- Green harvesting means removing unripe grape bunches before the main harvest.
- It is a way to manage crop load and yield in the vineyard.
- The timing can range from after fruit set to around veraison, or later for selective removal.
- It is different from pruning, shoot thinning and picking ripe grapes.
- Removing bunches does not automatically produce better wine.
- Results depend on the vine, variety, site, season, water status and growing objectives.

## Quick answer

Green harvesting is the deliberate removal of some immature grape bunches from a vine during the growing season, before the remaining grapes are harvested. It is also commonly called crop thinning, cluster thinning or bunch thinning.

Growers may use it to reduce yield, adjust the amount of fruit carried by the vine, improve ripening uniformity or remove bunches that are damaged, diseased or unlikely to mature with the rest of the crop. It is a management decision, not a guaranteed recipe for more concentrated or higher-quality wine.

## When does green harvesting happen?

There is no single calendar date for green harvesting. It generally takes place after **fruit set**, when young berries have formed, and before or around **veraison**, the stage when grapes begin to ripen. In red grapes, veraison is usually marked by softening and colour change.

Earlier removal can give the remaining crop more time to develop with fewer competing bunches. Later removal may be used to fine-tune the crop or take out clusters that remain green, uneven, rotten or otherwise unsuitable. The useful timing depends on the variety, climate, vine growth, season and production goal.

## Why remove unripe bunches?

A vine has a limited amount of leaf area and growing capacity to support its fruit. Green harvesting changes the amount of fruit carried by the vine in relation to that growth.

The intended effects might include:

- reducing total yield;
- balancing fruit with the vine’s growth;
- making ripening more even;
- removing fruit from crowded areas;
- meeting a particular yield or fruit-composition target.

Those effects are not automatic. Earlier thinning can increase berry size in some conditions, while later thinning may have less effect on berry size but still remove fruit that is lagging behind.

## Does green harvesting make better wine?

Not necessarily. Research and technical guidance support possible changes in yield, berry size, ripening and grape composition, but the result varies with how much fruit is removed and when.

Green harvesting does not guarantee more sugar, flavour, colour, tannin or concentration. Nor does it automatically improve wine quality. A vineyard carrying too much fruit may respond differently from one that is already well balanced. Variety, site, season, vine vigour, leaf area and water status all matter.

In Australia, growers may consider green harvesting as one option for managing crop load in variable conditions, including hot or dry seasons. Australian research on Semillon and Shiraz in a hot climate shows why the practice needs to be judged in relation to variety and conditions rather than treated as a universal rule.

## How is it different from other vineyard work?

- **Pruning** cuts back parts of the vine, often during dormancy, to shape the vine and manage buds.
- **Shoot thinning** removes unwanted or excess shoots.
- **Green harvesting** removes developing grape bunches.
- **Selective harvesting** picks ripe grapes at harvest, rather than removing immature bunches earlier in the season.

In short, green harvesting is best understood as crop-load management. It can be useful in some vineyards, but it is not a shortcut to better wine.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is green harvesting the same as cluster thinning?

Usually, yes. “Cluster thinning” and “bunch thinning” are commonly used for removing developing grape bunches. Local terminology can vary.

### Is green harvesting sustainable?

The practice has potential labour, cost and material trade-offs, so it should not be described as inherently sustainable. Its value depends on the vineyard’s purpose and results.

### Does every vineyard need green harvesting?

No. It is not a universal requirement or standard across Australian vineyards. Growers decide whether it is useful for their vines, season, market and production goals.
