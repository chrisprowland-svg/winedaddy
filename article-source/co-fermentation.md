# What Is Co-fermentation? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- **Co-fermentation means fermenting two or more grape varieties or grape materials together.**
- The alternative is to ferment each variety separately and blend the finished wines later.
- A wine made from several grape varieties is **not automatically** co-fermented.
- Co-fermentation may affect colour, aroma, texture, extraction and perceived integration.
- The result depends on the varieties, proportions, ripeness, fruit condition, yeast and fermentation conditions.
- Co-fermentation is a winemaking method—not a quality guarantee, Australian GI or synonym for natural wine.
- It is different from whole-bunch fermentation, carbonic maceration and malolactic fermentation.

## Quick answer: what does co-fermentation mean?

Co-fermentation is the process of fermenting two or more grape varieties, or other grape-derived materials, together in the same fermenting batch rather than making separate wines and blending them later.

Imagine two paths. In the first, Shiraz and Viognier grapes enter a shared ferment and ferment together. In the second, each variety is fermented separately, and the winemaker combines the finished wines afterwards. Both methods can produce a multi-variety wine, but only the first is co-fermentation.

That timing matters because grapes, juice, skins, seeds and yeast interact during fermentation. Co-fermentation can therefore influence extraction, colour, aroma, acidity, texture and the way the components seem to fit together. But it does not automatically make a wine better or more complex. The outcome depends on the fruit and the winemaking conditions.

## How does co-fermentation work?

The grape varieties may be combined before fermentation begins, or one material may be added while another is already fermenting. The exact practice varies, so it is useful to ask two questions:

1. **What materials were combined?**
2. **When did they begin fermenting together?**

The materials might be two grape varieties, such as a red and a white variety, or other grape-derived materials in more specialised work. The varieties do not necessarily have to be harvested on the same day. In practice, their maturity, fruit condition and readiness for processing all influence whether a shared ferment is suitable.

Other factors also matter, including the proportions of each variety, sugar and acidity, yeast, fermentation temperature, skin contact and how much extraction takes place. This is why the same varieties can behave differently in different ferments.

A useful background introduction to fermentation is available in [What Is Fermentation?](/what-is-fermentation/), while [How Wine Is Made](/how-wine-is-made/) explains where alcoholic fermentation fits in the broader winemaking process.

## Co-fermentation versus blending

Co-fermentation and blending can both create a wine made from multiple varieties, but they happen at different points.

| Co-fermentation | Blending |
|---|---|
| Varieties are combined before or during alcoholic fermentation. | Wines are fermented separately and combined later. |
| The components share a fermenting environment. | Each component develops in its own ferment. |
| Grape material, juice, skins, seeds and yeast interact during fermentation. | The main interaction occurs after the component wines already exist. |
| There may be less opportunity to adjust each variety independently later. | The winemaker can assess and adjust separate wines before making the final blend. |

A label listing several varieties cannot, by itself, prove that they were co-fermented. Production information or an explicit winemaking description is needed.

This is also why co-fermentation is not the same as a **field blend**. A field blend contains varieties grown together, but those grapes may still be fermented together or separately. The vineyard arrangement does not establish what happened in the winery.

## Why might a winemaker choose co-fermentation?

A winemaker may choose co-fermentation to influence the balance or character of the finished wine. Possible aims include:

### To influence colour and extraction

In red winemaking, fermentation with skins extracts colour and phenolic compounds. Combining varieties can change the composition of the ferment and therefore influence how pigments and tannins are extracted or retained.

Research comparing Tempranillo and Graciano treatments found differences in pigment extraction and colour behaviour when the varieties were combined before fermentation rather than blended later. That result applies to the varieties and conditions studied; it is not a universal promise for every co-fermented wine.

### To shape aroma and texture

Different grape varieties contribute different compounds, and fermentation changes how those compounds are expressed. Yeast, skins, juice and the surrounding grape material can interact in ways that affect aroma and mouthfeel.

A winemaker may be seeking a particular texture, aromatic relationship or sense of integration. “More integrated” is best understood as a stylistic aim or tasting observation, not a guaranteed result.

### To pursue a particular style

Co-fermentation can be part of a producer’s chosen style or a recognised regional tradition. In Australia, Wine Australia documents examples of Shiraz co-fermented with Viognier. This is an Australian example, not evidence that the practice defines Australian Shiraz or is used throughout the country.

## What can co-fermentation change in a wine?

Co-fermentation **may** affect:

- **Colour**, through changes in pigment extraction and stability.
- **Tannin and phenolics**, because each variety contributes different compounds.
- **Aroma**, through the interaction of grape compounds and yeast during fermentation.
- **Acidity and alcohol balance**, because the combined fruit has its own overall composition.
- **Texture**, through phenolic extraction, alcohol, acidity and other fermentation effects.
- **Perceived integration**, because the components develop together rather than meeting only after separate fermentation.

These are possibilities, not fixed outcomes. Co-fermentation does not inherently increase or decrease acidity, soften tannins, lower alcohol or guarantee complexity. Variety, maturity, fruit condition, proportions, yeast, temperature and skin contact all influence the result.

## What co-fermentation is not

Co-fermentation is often confused with other winemaking terms:

- **Whole-bunch fermentation:** whole bunches, including stems, are fermented. This describes fruit handling, not whether several varieties are present.
- **Whole-berry fermentation:** intact berries are fermented. One variety or several varieties may be involved.
- **Carbonic maceration:** intact berries undergo a distinctive fermentation environment with limited oxygen and carbon dioxide. This is a different technique.
- **Semi-carbonic maceration:** combines elements of carbonic and conventional fermentation. It is not another name for co-fermentation.
- **Malolactic fermentation:** bacteria convert malic acid into lactic acid. This is different from the alcoholic fermentation of grape varieties.
- **Native- or cultured-yeast fermentation:** describes the yeast choice. Co-fermentation describes what materials are fermented together.
- **Natural wine:** a broad and inconsistently defined style term. Co-fermentation does not tell you whether a wine uses native yeast, low sulphur or minimal intervention.

## Is co-fermentation a quality claim?

No. Co-fermentation describes a production method. It is not, by itself, an Australian Geographic Indication, legal quality designation or guarantee of style.

A co-fermented wine can be successful, distinctive, balanced or enjoyable—but those qualities depend on the fruit and the complete winemaking approach. The word tells you something about how the wine was made, not whether you will automatically prefer it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a blend always co-fermented?

No. A blend may be made by fermenting varieties separately and combining the finished wines later.

### Can red and white grapes be co-fermented?

Yes, the broad term can include different combinations of grape varieties, including red and white materials. The result depends on the varieties, proportions and process.

### Does co-fermentation mean natural wine?

No. Co-fermentation concerns the materials being fermented together. It does not specify yeast, sulphur dioxide or the level of intervention.

### Can you tell from a wine label whether a wine was co-fermented?

Not necessarily. A multi-variety label tells you the wine contains multiple varieties, but not always whether they were fermented together. An explicit producer statement or production record is stronger evidence.

### Is co-fermentation the same as blending?

No. Co-fermentation happens during alcoholic fermentation. Blending combines wines that have usually fermented separately.
