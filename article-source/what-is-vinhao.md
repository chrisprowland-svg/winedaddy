# What is Vinhão?

## Highlights

- Vinhão is a dark-skinned Portuguese wine grape.
- It is strongly associated with northern Portugal, especially the Vinhos Verdes region.
- In the Douro, the same grape is commonly known as Sousão.
- Its heavily pigmented skins can produce intensely coloured wines.
- Vinhão wines often combine fresh acidity with firm tannin or grip.
- Flavours may include dark or wild berries, vinous notes and savoury characters.
- The finished style depends on ripeness, region, blending and winemaking.

## What is Vinhão?

Vinhão is a Portuguese red wine grape variety. It is not a generic name for Portuguese red wine, and it is not the name of a wine region.

The name is properly written **Vinhão**, with a tilde. You may also see the grape described as **Vinhão grape** for clarity.

Vinhão is best known for the deep colour it can give to wine. Its skins contain substantial pigment, which can be extracted during fermentation when the grape juice remains in contact with the skins. The result may be a wine that looks deeply ruby, purple or almost blue-black, depending on the fruit and the winemaking.

## Where is Vinhão grown?

Vinhão is particularly associated with northern Portugal. It is one of the authorised black grape varieties used in the protected **Vinho Verde** wine region, also called the Vinhos Verdes region.

Vinho Verde is a protected wine region in north-west Portugal, not simply a synonym for white wine. Although the region is widely known for fresh white wines, it also produces red wines from grapes such as Vinhão.

Vinhão is also associated with the Portuguese regions of Lafões and the Douro. The Douro is a separate wine region from Vinho Verde. If you encounter the name **Sousão** on a Douro label, it may refer to Vinhão under this regional name.

This does not mean that every wine labelled Sousão is identical in style, or that every bottle from a particular region is made entirely from this grape. Regional names, grape names and blends can all appear differently on labels.

## Why are Vinhão wines so dark?

The answer is in the grape skins.

Red wine gets much of its colour from pigments in dark grape skins, known as anthocyanins. During red-wine fermentation, the juice can remain in contact with the skins, allowing colour and other compounds to move into the wine.

Vinhão has strongly pigmented skins and a high capacity to contribute colour. That is why a Vinhão-based wine may look darker than a casual drinker expects from a relatively unfamiliar Portuguese grape.

Deep colour does not automatically mean heavy, sweet or high in alcohol. Colour, acidity, tannin, alcohol and body are different parts of a wine’s structure. Vinhão can look powerful while still tasting fresh because its natural acidity may provide energy and lift.

## What does Vinhão wine taste like?

Vinhão wines are often described as fresh, deeply coloured and firmly structured. Broad tendencies can include:

- high or pronounced acidity;
- noticeable tannin or astringency;
- medium to full body;
- vinous or savoury aromas;
- dark, wild or berry-like fruit characters.

Some examples may show raspberry or other wild-fruit impressions. More traditional or less ripe examples can seem rustic, sharp or drying. A wine made from fully ripe fruit with careful extraction may feel more balanced and polished.

These descriptions are tendencies, not a tasting guarantee. The final wine also depends on where the grapes were grown, the vintage, how ripe they were picked, whether Vinhão was blended with other grapes and how the wine was fermented and matured.

A useful way to think about Vinhão is as a grape that can contribute **colour, freshness and grip**. The winemaker and growing conditions determine how those elements come together in the glass.

## Vinhão and Sousão: are they the same?

For practical label reading, **Sousão is a major regional name for Vinhão, especially in the Douro**. In the Vinhos Verdes context, the grape is generally presented as Vinhão.

You may therefore encounter:

- **Vinhão** on a wine connected with northern Portugal or Vinho Verde;
- **Sousão** on a Douro wine;
- a regional designation without the grape name;
- a blend in which the grape is not prominently listed.

The name change can be confusing, but it reflects regional naming. It is still sensible to check the wine’s region and blend rather than assume that a grape name tells you everything about the finished style.

The related form **Sousón** is also documented in Galicia, across the border in north-west Spain. That is useful background, but Australian drinkers do not need to memorise every historical synonym to recognise the main names.

## How should you approach a bottle?

If you see Vinhão or Sousão on a label or wine list, expect the possibility of a deeply coloured red with refreshing acidity and firm structure. Do not assume that every example will be heavy or harsh.

For serving, follow the finished wine rather than the grape name alone. A fresher, lighter example may show better with a little chill, while a fuller, more structured wine may suit normal red-wine service. The bottle’s alcohol, body and winemaking style are better guides than colour alone.

For an Australian drinker, the most useful information is label awareness: Vinhão is a Portuguese grape, and Sousão is the name you are especially likely to encounter in a Douro context. It should not be confused with a claim that the grape is widely grown or commonly produced in Australia.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Vinhão a grape or a wine?

Vinhão is a grape variety. Wines made from it may be labelled with the grape name, a regional designation or a blend name.

### Is Vinhão the same as Sousão?

Sousão is a major regional synonym for Vinhão, particularly in the Douro. The names should still be understood in their regional and labelling context.

### Is Vinhão a white or red grape?

Vinhão is a dark-skinned red wine grape. It is used to make red wines.

### Why is Vinhão wine so dark?

Its skins are strongly pigmented and can release substantial colour during fermentation and skin contact.

### Is Vinhão always tannic or rustic?

No. Vinhão can have firm tannin and rustic or astringent character, especially in some less ripe or traditionally made examples, but ripeness and winemaking can produce a more balanced style.

### Where does Vinhão come from?

It is a Portuguese grape strongly associated with northern Portugal, including the Vinhos Verdes region, Lafões and the Douro, where it is commonly called Sousão.
