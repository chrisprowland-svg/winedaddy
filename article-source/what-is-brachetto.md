# What is Brachetto?

## Highlights

- **Brachetto is a black-skinned Italian wine-grape variety**, not one single universal wine style.
- It is strongly associated with **Piemonte**, especially the area around **Acqui** in north-west Italy.
- The best-known wine made from it is **Brachetto d’Acqui DOCG**, a protected regional designation.
- Brachetto d’Acqui is usually aromatic, red-fruited and sweet, and may be still, sparkling or made in a passito style.
- The word **Brachetto alone does not guarantee** that a wine will be sweet, sparkling, red or low in alcohol.
- In Australia, you are most likely to encounter it on an imported Italian bottle, restaurant list or specialist wine shelf.

## The short answer

Brachetto is an aromatic Italian grape variety with black berries. It is best known for wines with floral and red-fruit character, particularly the sweet wines made in Piemonte under the name **Brachetto d’Acqui DOCG**.

That distinction matters. **Brachetto is the grape; Brachetto d’Acqui DOCG is a protected wine designation with its own regional boundaries and production rules.** A bottle that simply refers to Brachetto should not automatically be assumed to have the same style as Brachetto d’Acqui.

The familiar Brachetto style is often lightly coloured, sweet, fragrant and gently or fully sparkling. But those are typical associations, not a definition of every wine made from the grape.

## Where does Brachetto come from?

Brachetto is principally associated with **Piemonte**, in north-west Italy. Its strongest historical association is with south-eastern Piemonte, around **Acqui Terme**, including parts of the provinces of **Asti and Alessandria**.

The protected designation **Brachetto d’Acqui DOCG**, also known as **Acqui**, refers to wine made within a defined area under regulated conditions. It is not simply another name for the grape in every context.

So, when reading a label:

- **Brachetto** generally points to the grape variety.
- **Brachetto d’Acqui DOCG** identifies a specific protected regional wine designation.
- **Acqui** may be used as part of the regulated wine name, rather than describing every Brachetto wine made elsewhere.

Town names and regional names can therefore tell you something different from the grape name. A wine made from Brachetto is not automatically a Brachetto d’Acqui DOCG.

## What does Brachetto wine taste like?

The most recognisable Brachetto profile comes from Brachetto d’Acqui. Typical examples may show:

- ruby-red, garnet or rosé-leaning colour;
- floral and muscat-like fragrance;
- red-fruit flavours such as strawberry and raspberry;
- a soft, delicate palate;
- noticeable sweetness;
- some freshness from acidity; and
- either no bubbles, a light fizz or more persistent sparkling character, depending on the style.

The word **aromatic** is useful here. It means the wine’s fragrance is especially prominent, so you may notice its perfume before you taste much of it.

Brachetto wines can also vary considerably. Production method, the specific designation, the level of sweetness and the amount of carbonation all affect the finished wine. Do not assume every bottle will be pale, sweet, sparkling or low in alcohol simply because it says Brachetto.

## Still, sparkling and passito styles

Brachetto d’Acqui can be made in several forms.

A **still** version has no noticeable bubbles. A **sparkling** version has more persistent carbonation, while a lightly sparkling wine may feel softer and less effervescent. A **passito** version is made using partly dried grapes, producing a different and generally more concentrated style.

These categories are important because the same grape can create quite different drinking experiences. Carbonation can make sweetness feel brighter, while a still wine may feel rounder and more obviously dessert-like. Alcohol can also differ substantially between styles, so the bottle’s alcohol-by-volume figure is more useful than assuming a universal Brachetto level.

## How should you serve Brachetto?

Serve a sweet, aromatic Brachetto **well chilled**. The research sources give different recommendations, so there is no single temperature that should be treated as a universal rule. A practical starting range is around **6–10°C**, adjusted to the style and the producer’s guidance.

A small white-wine glass or a suitable sparkling-wine glass will work. The main goal is to preserve the chill while leaving enough room to smell the wine.

Brachetto is especially suited to relaxed, lighter occasions: an aperitif, a picnic dessert, afternoon tea or the end of a meal. Its sweetness and fragrant fruit character can make it approachable for people who do not usually choose a full-bodied dry red.

## What food goes with Brachetto?

Match Brachetto with food that can meet its sweetness rather than making the wine taste thin or sharp. Good starting points include:

- strawberries and other fresh berries;
- fruit salad;
- fruit tarts;
- sorbet;
- dry biscuits; and
- light baked sweets.

The red-fruit and floral character works naturally with fruit-based desserts. As with any pairing, the exact balance depends on the wine’s sweetness, acidity and sparkle.

## Brachetto is not Moscato or Lambrusco

Brachetto can be confused with other Italian wines because Moscato and Lambrusco may also be aromatic, sweet or sparkling.

They are not the same thing:

- **Moscato** refers to different grape varieties and wine traditions, not to Brachetto.
- **Lambrusco** refers to a separate group of grapes and associated wines.
- **Brachetto** has its own identity, even when the finished wine shares qualities such as sweetness, red colour or bubbles.

Nor should **Braquet** be treated as a confirmed synonym for Brachetto on the evidence available here.

## How might an Australian drinker encounter Brachetto?

In Australia, you may see Brachetto or Brachetto d’Acqui on an imported Italian label, a restaurant wine list or a specialist retailer’s shelf. Current evidence does not establish substantial Australian plantings, production or consistent retail availability, so it is better understood as a wine you may encounter occasionally rather than a mainstream Australian variety.

If you see **Brachetto d’Acqui DOCG**, you are looking at a named Italian regional designation. If you see **Brachetto** used as a grape reference, read the rest of the label for the country, region, style, sweetness and alcohol level. The grape name alone is not a complete tasting description.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Brachetto a sweet wine?

It is best known for sweet wines, especially Brachetto d’Acqui. However, Brachetto is first a grape name, so the word alone should not be treated as a guarantee of sweetness.

### Is Brachetto always sparkling?

No. Brachetto d’Acqui may be still, sparkling or passito. Check the style description and the label.

### Is Brachetto a red wine?

Brachetto has black berries and is commonly associated with lightly coloured red, ruby, garnet or rosé-leaning wines. But the finished colour depends on how the wine is made.

### Is Brachetto the same as Moscato?

No. They are different grape identities, although some wines made from each can be aromatic, sweet and lower in alcohol.

### Should Brachetto be served cold?

Yes, a sweet aromatic example is generally best served well chilled. Follow the producer’s guidance where available rather than treating one exact temperature as mandatory.
