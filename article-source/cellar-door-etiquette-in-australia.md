# Cellar door etiquette in Australia: what to expect and how to behave

Visiting an Australian cellar door should feel welcoming, not intimidating. You do not need to be a wine expert or know a secret set of rules. The basics are simple: check the venue’s arrangements, arrive on time, listen to the host, taste at your own pace and make a safe plan for getting home.

## Highlights

- A cellar door is a winery’s public-facing space for tasting, hospitality and often wine sales.
- Every venue is different. Bookings, tasting fees, group limits, children, pets, photography and accessibility arrangements can vary.
- Arrive on time for a booked or seated tasting, and contact the venue if you are delayed.
- Ask questions. Staff expect visitors to be curious, including beginners.
- Spitting is normal during a structured tasting, particularly if you are visiting several venues.
- You are allowed to decline another pour or decide not to buy wine.
- Arrange transport before you start tasting. The venue’s current instructions always take priority.

## What is a cellar door?

In Australia, a cellar door is generally the visitor-facing part of a winery. It may combine a tasting area, hospitality space and retail shop. The experience might be a casual tasting at a counter, a seated appointment or a hosted session with a set format.

It is not necessarily the whole winery. Production areas can contain vehicles, equipment, chemicals and other hazards, so stay in public areas and follow signs and staff directions. Do not enter vineyards, sheds, cellars or work areas unless you are invited.

## Check the details before you go

A few minutes of preparation can make the visit much more relaxed. Check the venue’s current website or contact details for:

- opening hours and public-holiday closures;
- whether bookings are required or encouraged;
- the tasting format and approximate duration;
- tasting fees and whether they are redeemable;
- group-size limits;
- accessibility arrangements;
- policies for children and pets;
- photography expectations; and
- parking and transport options.

Bookings and tasting fees are common in Australian cellar-door practice, especially for seated or hosted tastings, but neither is universal. Wine Australia reported that 73% of surveyed wineries charged for tastings and 71% encouraged bookings for all tastings in its 2020–21 direct-to-consumer survey. Those figures describe industry practice at that time, not a rule that applies to every venue today.

If you are bringing a large group, a child or a dog, do not rely on assumptions. Ask first. The same applies if you use a wheelchair, have particular access needs or need information about seating, paths or facilities.

## Arrive on time and follow the format

For a booked tasting, arriving a few minutes early is considerate. A late arrival can interrupt a host or affect a timed session. If you are delayed, contact the venue rather than assuming your place will be held.

When you arrive, listen to the host’s explanation. They may describe the tasting order, where to stand or sit, which areas are open to visitors and how to use the spittoons. These instructions are more useful than any general etiquette guide because they reflect that venue’s current setup.

A cellar door is both a hospitality space and a working winery. Keep clear of equipment and restricted areas, and avoid touching barrels, vines, bottles or other objects unless invited.

## Taste at a comfortable pace

You do not have to finish every pour. Taste slowly, drink water and pause when you need to. If you are no longer enjoying the experience, it is fine to stop.

Spitting is also perfectly acceptable. A spittoon is there so visitors can assess wines without swallowing every sample. It can be especially useful when tasting several wines or visiting more than one cellar door. If you are unsure where or how to spit, ask the host.

Spitting does not remove all alcohol intake, so it is not a reason to drive after drinking. DrinkWise notes that small tasting pours can accumulate; its cellar-door material uses approximately six 15-millilitre tastings as a practical estimate of one standard drink. Treat that as a planning guide, not a licence to drive.

## Ask questions — even simple ones

Questions are part of the cellar-door experience. You might ask:

- “What should I notice in this wine?”
- “Is this fruit grown here?”
- “How would you describe the style?”
- “Is there a less expensive or lighter option?”
- “How long might this wine keep?”
- “Could I taste that one again before deciding?”

You do not need specialised vocabulary. Saying “I’m new to wine — how would you explain this?” is a useful way to begin.

Be aware that staff may be serving several groups at once. Give them room to finish with another visitor, and avoid monopolising the conversation during a busy session.

## You do not have to buy

Buying wine is a normal part of a cellar-door visit, but tasting does not automatically mean you must purchase something. A tasting fee may or may not be redeemable against a purchase, so check the venue’s terms rather than assuming.

If you are not buying, a simple response is enough:

> “Thank you — I enjoyed the tasting, but I’m not buying today.”

You can also say, “I’d like to think about it” or ask for the wine details so you can decide later. Recommendations are an invitation to learn, not an obligation.

## Be considerate in a group

Groups can make a cellar-door visit enjoyable, but they can also quickly fill a small tasting space. Keep voices and movement considerate, allow staff to serve others and supervise children closely where children are permitted.

Pets are not automatically allowed, particularly near production areas, food service or livestock. Photography may also be venue-specific. Ask before photographing staff, other visitors, private areas or commercially sensitive equipment.

There is no single Australian rule for children, pets or photography. The venue’s policy is the one to follow.

## Plan a safe way home

A wine-region visit often involves more than one tasting, and several small pours can add up. Arrange a designated driver, tour, rideshare, public transport or accommodation before drinking. Do not rely on feeling unaffected as a measure of whether you are safe or legally permitted to drive.

Water, food and a slower pace can make the visit more comfortable, but they do not make drink-driving safe. If you are visiting multiple venues, consider reducing the number of tastings or using spitting facilities.

Responsible-service rules apply to venues and staff, and details can differ between states and territories. They are not the same thing as a visitor etiquette rule. As a visitor, your practical responsibility is to drink cautiously, follow staff directions and organise safe transport.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to book a cellar door in Australia?

Not always. Some venues welcome walk-ins, while others require or strongly encourage bookings, particularly for seated tastings, large groups or busy periods. Check before travelling.

### Do I have to pay for a tasting?

Not always. Tasting fees are common but vary by venue and experience. Ask whether the fee is redeemable before you begin.

### Do I have to buy wine?

No general etiquette rule requires a purchase. You can thank the host and decline politely.

### Is it rude to spit?

No. Spitting is a normal part of wine tasting and can be sensible when tasting several wines. Use the provided receptacle and follow the venue’s guidance.

### Can I bring children or a dog?

Possibly, but policies vary. Ask the venue first and follow its supervision and access requirements.

### What is the most important rule?

Treat the cellar door as both a hospitality space and a working winery: be prepared, considerate and responsive to the venue’s instructions. Those current instructions override general etiquette.
