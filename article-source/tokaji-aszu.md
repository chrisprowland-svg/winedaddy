# Tokaji Aszú: Hungary’s Sweet Wine Explained

## Highlights

- Tokaji Aszú is a traditional sweet wine from Hungary’s Tokaj wine region.
- “Tokaji Aszú” is a protected, place-linked name—not a synonym for every sweet or botrytised wine.
- Its defining ingredient is individually selected, botrytised grapes called aszú berries.
- Furmint is the leading grape associated with the wine, but Tokaji Aszú is not necessarily a single-variety wine.
- Sweetness is balanced by acidity, concentration and freshness.
- Tokaj is the broader wine region and protected designation system; Aszú is one wine type within it.
- Older labels may mention puttonyos, but this term should not automatically be read as a current official sweetness scale.

## Quick answer: what is Tokaji Aszú?

Tokaji Aszú is a regulated sweet wine type from the Tokaj wine region in north-eastern Hungary. It is protected under the European Union’s Tokaj/Tokaji Protected Designation of Origin, or PDO.

The wine is made using individually selected grapes affected by *Botrytis cinerea*, commonly called noble rot when it develops beneficially. These concentrated grapes are known as aszú berries. They are combined with qualifying must or wine, then fermented and matured under the rules of the Tokaj designation.

That makes Tokaji Aszú different from a generic sweet wine, dessert wine, late-harvest wine or botrytised wine. Those terms describe broad styles or production techniques. Tokaji Aszú describes a particular wine type with a protected geographical and regulatory identity.

## Where does Tokaji Aszú come from?

Tokaj is in north-eastern Hungary, around the foothills of the Zemplén Mountains and the Bodrog–Tisza river area. The Tokaj wine region contains a defined group of localities, including Tokaj, Mád, Tarcal, Tállya, Tolcsva, Sárospatak and Sátoraljaújhely.

These town names help place the region, but a town name does not automatically mean a wine is Tokaji Aszú. The broader name **Tokaj/Tokaji** refers to a protected designation system covering several wine types. **Tokaji Aszú** is one specific type within that system.

For broader Hungarian context, see our guide to [Hungary](/hungary/).

## Why are botrytised grapes important?

Noble rot is a form of *Botrytis cinerea* that can develop on grapes in suitable conditions. It reduces water in the berries and concentrates their sugars, acids and flavour compounds. The result can be intensely flavoured grapes with the concentration needed for a rich sweet wine.

For Tokaji Aszú, suitable berries are picked separately and selected individually—grape by grape. They are then combined with must or wine from the qualifying Tokaj production context.

This is the key idea to remember: Tokaji Aszú is not simply wine made from grapes that happen to have botrytis. It is a regulated wine type in which aszú berries and the place-linked production rules are central.

You can learn more about the process in [Botrytis and noble rot](/botrytis-and-noble-rot/) and [botrytised wine](/botrytised-wine/).

## Which grapes are used?

Furmint is the leading grape associated with Tokaji Aszú. It is important partly because its naturally lively acidity can help a sweet wine taste fresh rather than heavy.

However, Tokaji Aszú is not a Furmint-only category. Tokaj materials also recognise Hárslevelű, Sárgamuskotály, Zéta, Kövérszőlő and Kabar. The exact blend can vary, so it is more accurate to say that Furmint is the principal associated variety than to call Tokaji Aszú a single-grape wine.

## What does Tokaji Aszú taste like?

Tokaji Aszú is generally sweet, concentrated and full-flavoured, with acidity that can provide balance and freshness. Depending on the grapes, vintage and maturation, you may encounter aromas and flavours suggesting dried or stone fruit, citrus, honey, spice, flowers and botrytis-derived notes.

The wine can be rich without tasting flat. That is the useful beginner’s lesson: sweetness is only one part of its structure. Residual sugar creates sweetness, while acidity helps keep the wine lively. Concentration adds weight and persistence.

Not every bottle will taste identical. Variety, harvest conditions, the proportion and condition of aszú berries, fermentation and maturation can all influence the final wine.

## What does “puttonyos” mean?

Puttonyos is a traditional Tokaj term historically linked to the quantity of aszú material used and, by extension, sweetness. Older bottles may display a puttonyos number.

Current labelling treatment has changed, so puttonyos should not automatically be treated as a complete modern sweetness scale or a universal mandatory classification. If you see the term, regard it as useful historical and label context rather than relying on it alone to predict the wine.

The exact current legal thresholds, ageing rules and labelling provisions should be checked against the amended Tokaj/Tokaji specification.

## How is it different from other sweet wines?

- **Sweet wine:** a broad category for wine with noticeable residual sugar. Tokaji Aszú is sweet, but most sweet wines are not Tokaji Aszú.
- **Dessert wine:** a consumer-friendly term for rich or sweet wines. It does not carry the same protected geographical meaning.
- **Late-harvest wine:** wine made from grapes picked later than usual. Tokaj includes a separate late-harvest type; late harvesting alone does not make a wine Aszú.
- **Botrytised wine:** wine made from grapes affected by noble rot. Tokaji Aszú is botrytised, but botrytis alone does not grant permission to use the Tokaji name.

For Australian drinkers, these are helpful comparisons—not legal equivalents under Hungarian or Australian wine rules.

## How should you read the label?

Look for the specific wine type **Tokaji Aszú**, rather than assuming that every bottle marked Tokaji is Aszú. A label may also include a producer name, vintage, locality or, particularly on older or transitional bottles, a puttonyos reference.

The practical rule is simple: **Tokaj/Tokaji tells you about the protected regional system; Aszú identifies the particular wine type.**

Tokaji Aszú is usually served chilled in modest pours. Its sweetness and acidity can work well with rich cheeses, pâté, fruit desserts and some lightly spiced dishes. It is also commonly regarded as capable of ageing, although its development depends on the wine, vintage, storage and bottle condition.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Tokaji Aszú the same as Tokaj?

No. Tokaj is the wine region, while Tokaj/Tokaji is the broader protected designation system. Tokaji Aszú is one regulated wine type within that system.

### Is Tokaji Aszú always made from Furmint?

No. Furmint is the leading associated variety, but other recognised Tokaj varieties include Hárslevelű, Sárgamuskotály, Zéta, Kövérszőlő and Kabar.

### Is every botrytised wine Tokaji Aszú?

No. Botrytised describes a production ingredient or style. Tokaji Aszú also requires the protected place-linked identity and qualifying production methods.

### Is Tokaji Aszú a dessert wine?

It can be described as a dessert wine in everyday Australian conversation, but “dessert wine” is a broad category. Tokaji Aszú has a more specific geographical and regulatory meaning.

### Is puttonyos still a current sweetness scale?

Treat it cautiously. It is a traditional term, and current labelling rules have changed. Do not use a puttonyos number as a complete modern guide to sweetness without checking the current specification.
