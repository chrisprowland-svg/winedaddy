# What Is Blending Wine? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Wine blending is the deliberate combination of separately made wine components.
- The components may come from different grape varieties, vineyard blocks, regions, vintages, vessels or maturation lots.
- Winemakers blend to shape balance, structure, aroma, texture, colour, complexity, consistency or style.
- A blend is not automatically lower quality than a single-variety wine.
- Blending is different from co-fermentation and field blending.
- In Australia, a single-variety label does not necessarily mean the wine is made from 100% of that grape.

## Quick answer: what is wine blending?

Wine blending is the deliberate combination of two or more separately made wine components, usually before bottling, to achieve a particular result in the finished wine.

A component might be a wine made from Shiraz, a parcel from a different vineyard block, a barrel-aged portion, or a lot with notably fresh acidity or firm tannin. Winemakers assess these components separately, test them in different proportions and select the combination that best suits the intended style.

Blending can help shape a wine’s balance, structure, aroma, texture, colour, complexity and consistency. It is a normal winemaking tool, not automatically a sign of lower quality. But blending is not a guarantee of improvement: a poor combination can make a wine less balanced, and sound winemaking does not use blending to conceal serious microbiological or chemical faults.

## What can be blended?

### Different grape varieties

The most familiar example is a wine made from more than one grape variety. Shiraz–Cabernet is a useful Australian example. A GSM-style wine may combine Grenache, Shiraz and Mourvèdre.

These names describe possible combinations, not fixed recipes. The proportions and labelling treatment can vary according to the wine, producer and applicable rules.

### Separate lots of the same variety

Blending does not require different grape varieties. A winemaker may combine separate lots of the same grape, such as fruit from different vineyard blocks, harvest dates, fermentation vessels or barrels.

One lot might contribute freshness, while another provides body, colour or tannin. The final wine can still be presented as a single-variety wine, provided it meets the relevant labelling requirements.

### Different regions, vintages or vessels

Components may also differ by geographical indication, vintage, fermentation method or maturation vessel. For example, a winemaker might assess stainless-steel and oak-matured portions separately before deciding whether to combine them.

This does not mean every wine contains all these components. They are possible bases for a blend, and practices vary between wines and wineries.

## Why do winemakers blend wine?

### To improve balance

One component may have lively acidity, while another feels rounder or fuller. Combining them can create a more harmonious relationship between freshness, fruit, alcohol, body and texture.

Balance is a sensory judgement rather than a single number. More of one component is not automatically better.

### To build structure and texture

Structure is the way elements such as acidity, tannin, body and alcohol give a wine shape. A firm, tannic lot may contribute grip, while a softer lot can make the finished wine feel more approachable.

Blending may also change the wine’s mouthfeel, including its weight, freshness, softness and finish.

### To add aroma, flavour and colour

Different components can contribute different characters. One may bring red-fruit aromas, another dark fruit, spice or savoury notes. A darker component may also influence a wine’s colour.

The result is not necessarily more complex or better. The aim is to create a combination that fits the intended style.

### To create complexity

A carefully chosen blend can bring together complementary characters that are less noticeable in the individual components. But complexity is not guaranteed simply because more varieties or lots have been added.

### To improve consistency

Blending can help a winery maintain a recognisable style when vineyard parcels or seasons vary. It may also allow separate lots to be assembled into a practical finished volume.

Consistency does not mean a wine lacks character. It is one possible objective among many.

## How does the blending process work?

There is no single universal sequence, but a high-level workflow usually looks like this:

1. **Separate components are made or matured.** Lots may be separated by variety, vineyard, vintage, vessel or treatment.
2. **The components are assessed.** Winemakers may consider sensory observations and laboratory information.
3. **A goal is defined.** The aim might be balance, structure, aroma, complexity, style or consistency.
4. **Small blend trials are prepared.** Different proportions are tested on a small scale.
5. **The trials are tasted and compared.** The preferred combination is judged against the intended wine style.
6. **The selected blend is assembled.** The chosen lots are combined in a suitable vessel.
7. **The wine is finished as appropriate.** It may be settled, stabilised, clarified, filtered or otherwise prepared for bottling.
8. **Label claims are checked.** Any variety, vintage or geographical-indication claims must meet current Australian requirements.

Blending does not always happen immediately before bottling, and it does not always happen at the same point in maturation. Timing depends on the wine and the winemaker’s approach.

## Blending is not the same as co-fermentation or a field blend

These terms describe different points at which components come together.

- **Blending:** separately made or matured wine components are combined.
- **Co-fermentation:** two or more components are fermented together, rather than being kept entirely separate until after fermentation.
- **Field blend:** different varieties are grown together and commonly harvested or processed together in the vineyard and winery.
- **Finished wine blend:** a broad description of a wine made from multiple components.

A finished wine may involve more than one of these ideas. For example, a wine can be described broadly as a blend even if some components were co-fermented. The important distinction is whether you are talking about the winemaking action or the finished wine category.

## What does blending mean on an Australian wine label?

Australian rules set minimum requirements for claims about grape variety, vintage and geographical indication. In general, a single claimed variety, vintage or GI must meet the relevant 85% requirement, subject to the detailed rules. When multiple varieties or geographical indications are claimed, they are listed in descending order of proportion, with additional requirements applying to multiple-GI claims.

That means an Australian wine labelled with one grape variety does not necessarily contain 100% of that variety. Wine Australia’s educational material explains that a single-variety wine may contain up to 15% other grapes under the 85% rule.

The label may therefore tell you about a principal variety, vintage or place without describing every component, lot, vessel or winemaking decision. Exact compliance depends on the type of claim and the current regulations. For more background, see [how to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/).

## Can blending improve a wine?

It can, when the components are sound and the proportions are carefully chosen. A successful blend may soften excessive tannin, add freshness, broaden aroma, increase body or bring the wine closer to a planned style.

But blending is a tool, not a guarantee. Components can clash, and a proportion that works in a small trial may dominate when used at a larger scale. Blending cannot reliably turn every flawed wine into a good one, and it should not be treated as a legitimate way to hide serious faults.

The most useful beginner takeaway is simple: a blend is not defined by how many varieties it contains. It is defined by the deliberate way its components are selected and combined.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does blending mean mixing different grape varieties?

Not necessarily. Different varieties can be blended, but separate lots of the same variety can also be combined.

### Are blended wines lower quality?

No. Blending is a normal winemaking decision and does not, by itself, indicate quality.

### Is blending the same as co-fermentation?

No. Blending combines separately made components, while co-fermentation involves fermenting components together.

### Does a single-variety Australian wine contain only that grape?

Not necessarily. Under the relevant Australian rules, a single-variety claim generally requires at least 85% of the named variety, subject to the detailed requirements.
