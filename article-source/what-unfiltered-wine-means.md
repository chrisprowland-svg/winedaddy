# What unfiltered wine means

## Highlights

- **Unfiltered wine is usually bottled without a final filtration step.**
- It may still have been clarified by settling, racking or fining.
- It can look clear, hazy or develop sediment in the bottle.
- “Unfiltered” does not automatically mean natural, organic, healthier or better.
- It may taste or feel different, but there is no guaranteed flavour profile.
- Sediment is usually a serving consideration, not an automatic sign of faulty wine.
- The word alone cannot tell you whether a wine is stable, sound or spoiled.

## Quick answer: what is unfiltered wine?

Unfiltered wine is wine bottled without a final filtration step intended to remove suspended particles and, depending on the filtration method, microorganisms.

Filtration is only one way to make wine clearer or more stable. Before bottling, a wine may also become clearer through natural settling, racking, fining or other cellar practices. That means an unfiltered wine can still be bright and clear. It may also be slightly hazy, visibly cloudy or develop a deposit of sediment over time.

The important point is that **“unfiltered” describes one processing choice, not the whole philosophy or quality of the wine**. It does not prove that a wine is natural, organic, biodynamic, low-intervention, more authentic, healthier or superior. It also does not, by itself, tell you whether the wine is safe, stable or likely to taste a particular way.

## Filtration is not the same as clarification

These terms are related, but they do not mean the same thing.

**Clarification** is the broader process of reducing suspended material so wine becomes clearer or more stable. Some clarification happens naturally: particles can settle under gravity during fermentation and storage.

**Racking** means moving clearer wine away from settled solids. It can reduce the amount of sediment, but it does not necessarily remove every suspended particle or microorganism.

**Fining** involves adding a substance that gathers or precipitates suspended material so it can settle out. It is a different process from physically passing wine through a filter.

**Filtration** passes wine through a filter that retains material. Depending on the method, filtration may target visible particles, smaller colloidal material or microorganisms.

So, a wine can be **fined but not filtered**, or filtered without being fined. The word “unfiltered” does not reveal every step taken in the winery.

## What does unfiltered wine look like?

There is no single appearance you should expect.

An unfiltered wine may be:

- bright and clear;
- lightly hazy;
- visibly cloudy;
- dotted with floating particles; or
- clear at first but later show sediment at the bottom or side of the bottle.

A clear unfiltered wine may have been clarified through settling or another method. Conversely, haze may come from retained yeast, grape material, colloids, crystals or other substances. Some causes are stylistic or harmless; others may indicate instability or a fault.

Appearance alone cannot identify the cause. Cloudy wine is not automatically faulty, and sediment is not automatically proof of quality or a sign that something has gone wrong.

## Does unfiltered wine taste different?

It may, but there is no guaranteed “unfiltered taste”.

Differences in clarification and retained particles may influence how a particular wine feels, smells or develops. Some drinkers notice a different texture or a greater sense of weight. Others notice little difference, or simply prefer the brightness of a fully clear wine.

The result depends on the wine, its composition, the producer’s choices, packaging and storage. It is safer to think of “unfiltered” as information about processing rather than a promise of fuller flavour, extra complexity or better aroma.

A filtered wine is not automatically characterless, and an unfiltered wine is not automatically more expressive.

## Is unfiltered wine natural or organic?

No. Unfiltered is not a synonym for natural, organic, biodynamic or low-intervention wine.

Those terms relate to different aspects of farming, certification or winemaking. A producer may use one, several or none of them. An unfiltered label only tells you—or generally intends to tell you—that the wine was bottled without a final filtration step. It does not establish whether the wine was fined, settled, racked or made using any broader production philosophy.

## Is unfiltered wine safe to drink?

The word alone cannot answer that question.

Filtration can help reduce microorganisms and improve microbiological stability before bottling. Its importance depends partly on the wine’s composition and intended storage. For example, wines with residual sugar can present particular stability considerations.

However, “unfiltered” does not prove that a wine is unsafe, just as “filtered” is not a complete guarantee that every bottle will remain sound. Consumers cannot assess microbiological stability from haze or sediment alone.

If a bottle has an unusual smell, unexpected pressure, leakage, a damaged closure or an obviously abnormal condition, stop drinking it and contact the retailer or producer. That is sensible consumer advice, not a diagnosis from appearance.

## How should you buy and serve it?

When buying wine in an Australian bottle shop, restaurant or cellar door, treat “unfiltered” as useful but incomplete information.

Ask the retailer or producer what the term means for that particular bottling if you want to know whether it is likely to show haze or sediment. This can be especially useful when brilliant clarity matters for a formal dinner or restaurant service.

At home:

1. **Avoid unnecessary agitation** if you expect sediment.
2. **Stand the bottle upright before serving** so loose material can collect at the bottom. The time needed varies.
3. **Pour slowly and steadily.**
4. **Use a light behind the bottle** if it helps you see the deposit.
5. **Decant only when useful.** Decanting can separate wine from substantial sediment, but it is not required for every unfiltered wine.

If the wine is clear or only lightly hazy, you may prefer to pour it normally. The aim is to enjoy the wine, not follow a ritual simply because the label says “unfiltered”.

## What can Australian drinkers infer from the label?

Australian wine labels must describe the product truthfully and must not mislead consumers. In the reviewed Wine Australia guidance, “unfiltered” is not presented as a complete statutory quality category with a detailed standalone definition.

In practical terms, treat it as a producer-used process description or voluntary claim. Do not assume the label tells you the entire cellar history, and do not infer that the wine was never clarified in another way.

If the process matters to your choice, ask a retailer, sommelier or cellar door about that specific bottle.

## Common questions

### Should I decant every unfiltered wine?

No. Decanting is optional. It can help when a bottle contains substantial sediment, but unnecessary decanting may not improve a clear or delicate wine.

### Does sediment mean the wine is faulty?

Not necessarily. Sediment can have several causes, and its presence alone does not diagnose a fault. Unusual smells or flavours are more useful reasons to seek advice than appearance alone.

### Does unfiltered mean unfined?

No. Fining and filtration are separate practices. A wine may be fined but not filtered.

### Is unfiltered wine better?

Not automatically. Whether it is enjoyable depends on the particular wine and your preference. “Unfiltered” is not a quality ranking.

## The simplest way to remember it

**Unfiltered tells you what was not done at the final stage—not everything that was done before it.**

It may affect clarity, sediment and stability, but the word is not a guarantee of style, quality or taste. Read it alongside the rest of the label, and ask about the particular bottling when the detail matters.
