# Wine with Soft Cheese: How to Choose a Good Match

Soft cheese is not one single pairing target. A fresh, tangy goat’s cheese, a creamy Brie-style cheese and a pungent washed-rind cheese can all be soft, but they do not taste alike.

The best starting point is to match the wine to the cheese’s **creaminess, salt, acidity, rind character and intensity**. Then consider what else is on the board.

## Highlights

For a soft-cheese board, start with a wine that has freshness and enough flavour to stand up to the cheese without overwhelming it.

- **Fresh, mild cheeses:** try Australian sparkling wine, a crisp white or dry rosé.
- **Fresh goat’s cheese:** try Sauvignon Blanc, another fresh high-acid white, sparkling wine or dry rosé.
- **Brie-style and Camembert-style cheeses:** try sparkling wine or a fresh white. A more textured Chardonnay can work with a ripe, rich cheese and substantial accompaniments.
- **Washed-rind cheeses:** look for more aromatic or textural presence, while retaining freshness. Sparkling wine, aromatic whites, fuller fresh whites or a lighter red may work.
- **Honey, chutney and sweet fruit:** consider a fruitier or slightly off-dry white rather than the driest, leanest wine.
- **Mixed boards:** choose a versatile, fresh wine with moderate intensity and low-to-moderate tannin.

These are sensible starting points, not universal rules. Soft cheese varies too much for one grape variety or wine colour to work every time.

## What counts as soft cheese?

“Soft cheese” is a broad practical description covering several styles. Cheese categories can overlap: a cheese can be soft, made from goat’s milk, surface-ripened and quite intense at the same time.

### Fresh and unripened soft cheese

Fresh cheeses have not undergone extended ripening. Examples include cream cheese, mascarpone, ricotta, quark and fromage blanc.

They are often mild, moist and creamy, so a delicate, fresh wine is a useful place to begin. Australian sparkling wine, crisp white wine and dry rosé can provide lift without taking over the cheese.

Fresh does not always mean bland, however. Fresh goat’s cheese usually brings more tang and lactic freshness than mild cream cheese, which changes the pairing.

### Bloomy-rind cheese

Brie and Camembert are examples of soft-ripened, bloomy-rind cheeses. Their soft white rind and creamy interiors make richness and texture central to the pairing.

Sparkling wine is a useful first choice because its freshness and carbonation can make a creamy mouthful feel lighter. A fresh high-acid white can do something similar. If the cheese is particularly ripe and rich, or the board includes bread and nuts, a more textured Chardonnay may be worth trying.

“Bloomy rind” describes a ripening and rind style. It does not mean that every soft cheese is Brie or Camembert, and products sold in Australia may not all have identical traditional production methods.

### Fresh soft goat’s cheese

Goat’s cheese is not automatically fresh: it may be young, moderately aged or firm. Fresh examples are often moist, refreshing and tangy, with a recognisable goat-milk character.

That tang makes high-acid wines especially logical starting points. Try Sauvignon Blanc, another crisp white, dry rosé or fresh sparkling wine. These styles can echo the cheese’s brightness rather than making it seem heavy.

If the goat’s cheese is older and more pronounced, move towards a wine with more flavour and texture rather than assuming the youngest, lightest style will always be best.

### Washed-rind soft cheese

Washed-rind cheese is ripened by washing the cheese during maturation with brine, beer, wine, brandy or a similar liquid. It can be creamy and soft, but it may also have a sticky rind, pungent aroma and savoury intensity.

For this style, a very delicate wine may disappear. Consider an aromatic white, a fresh but fuller-bodied white, sparkling wine or a lighter-bodied red. Keep the recommendation flexible: the cheese’s salt, ripeness and pungency matter more than the label “washed rind” alone.

## Why these pairings can work

### Acidity and creaminess

Rich, creamy cheese can coat the mouth. A wine with noticeable acidity can make the combination feel fresher and less heavy. This is why sparkling wine and crisp whites are such reliable starting points for many creamy cheeses.

### Salt and sweetness

Salt can change how wine tastes, making it seem softer and less acidic. That can help explain why fresh whites, sparkling wines and some lighter reds can work with salty cheese.

Sweet accompaniments create a different challenge. Honey, sweet chutney, dried fruit and ripe sweet fruit can make a dry wine seem less fruity, drier or more bitter. With those additions, try a wine with more fruit character or a little perceptible sweetness.

A dry wine can still be flavourful and aromatic; “dry” describes sweetness, not a lack of flavour.

### Carbonation and texture

The bubbles in sparkling wine provide lift against a creamy texture, while its acidity keeps the pairing lively. Australian sparkling wine is a practical option for a cheese board because the style is familiar and available in a range of expressions, including dry brut styles.

### Body, aroma and tannin

Match the wine’s intensity to the cheese. Mild cream cheese can be overwhelmed by a powerful wine, while a pungent washed-rind cheese can make a very delicate wine seem thin.

Some lighter-bodied, lower-tannin reds can work with savoury, mature or washed-rind cheeses, particularly when the board also includes cured meat or roasted ingredients. Firm, highly tannic red wine is not the safest default for delicate, creamy cheese.

## How accompaniments change the match

A cheese board is a collection of pairings, not one fixed combination.

- **Honey, chutney and sweet fruit:** try a fruitier or slightly off-dry white.
- **Fresh fruit and herbs:** fresh whites, sparkling wine and dry rosé are useful starting points.
- **Nuts:** a richer cheese and a more textured wine may be able to handle the added weight.
- **Cured meat:** salt and savoury intensity may make a fuller fresh white or lighter red more suitable.
- **Crackers and bread:** these add texture and starch, but the topping and cheese usually have the greater influence.

If one wine must cover a mixed board, prioritise freshness, moderate flavour intensity and restrained tannin. Sparkling wine, a fresh white or dry rosé will often give you more flexibility than an extremely delicate wine or a very powerful red.

## A simple way to choose

Before opening the bottle, ask three questions:

1. **Is the cheese mild, tangy, creamy or pungent?**
2. **Is it salty, and are there sweet accompaniments?**
3. **Will the wine be lighter, similar in intensity or much stronger?**

For a mild and creamy board, begin with sparkling wine, a crisp white or dry rosé. For tangy goat’s cheese, look towards high-acid whites. For a ripe bloomy rind, add textured Chardonnay to the possibilities. For pungent washed rind, increase the wine’s aromatic or textural presence while keeping freshness.

Serve the wine and cheese according to their normal style guidance rather than treating every soft cheese as identical. Very cold wine can show less aroma, while wine served too warm can feel heavier. ## FAQs

### Is white wine always best with soft cheese?

No. Fresh whites and sparkling wines are useful starting points, but dry rosé and some lighter-bodied reds can also work. The cheese’s salt, ripeness, rind character and accompaniments matter more than a simple white-versus-red rule.

### Is Sauvignon Blanc the best wine for goat’s cheese?

It is a sensible starting point for fresh, tangy goat’s cheese because its freshness can complement the cheese. It is not a universal answer: aged goat’s cheese, herbs, fruit and other toppings may call for a different style.

### Can you serve Chardonnay with Brie?

Yes, particularly when the Brie-style cheese is ripe and rich or the board includes substantial accompaniments. Start with a fresh or moderately textured Chardonnay rather than assuming every full-bodied, oaked example will suit.

### What wine should I choose for a pungent soft cheese?

Look for a wine with enough aromatic or textural presence to avoid being overwhelmed, while retaining freshness. Sparkling wine, aromatic whites, fuller fresh whites and lighter reds are possible starting points.

### Does dry wine mean it will taste plain with cheese?

No. Dryness refers to the wine’s level of perceptible sweetness. A dry wine can still be fruity, aromatic and full of flavour.
