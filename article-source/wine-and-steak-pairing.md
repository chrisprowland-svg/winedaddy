# Wine and Steak Pairing: What Wine Goes Best with Steak?

There is no single best wine for every steak. The most reliable approach is to match the wine to the **whole dish**: the cut, its richness, how it was cooked, the amount of char or smoke, and the sauce.

As a starting point, rich or heavily grilled steaks can suit structured reds such as Australian Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon. Leaner, more delicately flavoured cuts may be better with a fresher, softer red such as Pinot Noir. But sauce and seasoning can change the answer just as much as the steak itself.

## Highlights

- Rich, fatty steak can work well with a wine that has body, acidity and tannin.
- Tannin is the drying, grippy sensation found in many red wines.
- Acidity adds freshness and can stop a rich dish feeling heavy.
- Char, smoke, pepper, chilli and sweet barbecue sauce all affect the pairing.
- Australian Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon come in many styles; neither is always the right choice.
- Lean steak can suit moderate-bodied red wine, fuller Chardonnay, rosé or sparkling wine.
- Personal preference is part of a successful pairing.

## The quick answer: which wine should you choose?

Start by asking three questions:

1. **Is the steak rich or lean?**
2. **Is the dominant flavour beef, char, smoke or sauce?**
3. **Does the wine need to provide freshness, structure or restraint?**

A marbled rib eye, also called scotch fillet in Australia, has enough richness to support a fuller red with noticeable but integrated tannin. Tannin gives wine grip and a drying texture. Australian Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon can be good starting points, particularly when the steak is grilled or barbecued.

A lean fillet or tenderloin has a more delicate texture and flavour. A very tannic, heavily oaked or highly alcoholic wine may overwhelm it. Pinot Noir or another fresher, moderate-bodied red can allow the steak to remain the focus.

For a simply cooked steak, the cut matters more. Once you add pepper sauce, mushroom cream, béarnaise, herb butter, chimichurri or a sweet barbecue-style glaze, the accompaniment may become the dominant pairing factor.

Think of these as useful directions, not rules. Wine styles vary considerably, even within the same grape variety.

## Match the wine to the steak cut

### Rib eye or scotch fillet

Rib eye is richly marbled, full-flavoured and often served grilled, pan-seared or barbecued. Its fat and browned crust can work with a wine that has enough body and concentration to stand alongside it.

Try a structured Australian Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon or red blend. Look for freshness as well as tannin: a wine with too much concentration, oak or alcohol can make the meal feel heavy.

### Sirloin, striploin or porterhouse

Cut names can vary between Australian butchers, states and countries, so these terms are not always exact equivalents. In general, these steaks are substantial but often leaner than rib eye.

Medium- to full-bodied Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon can work, especially with a robust jus, pepper sauce or charred crust. A medium-bodied red may be more versatile when the steak is simply cooked or served with fresh herbs.

### Rump

Rump often has a savoury, beefy character and can be grilled, pan-fried or prepared on the barbecue. Its texture and richness vary with the particular cut and preparation.

A medium-bodied red is a flexible choice. Choose a more structured wine when there is heavy char or a strong sauce; choose something fresher and softer when the steak is simply prepared.

### Fillet or tenderloin

Fillet is lean, tender and more delicately flavoured than a richly marbled cut. Pinot Noir can be a good starting point, particularly with mushrooms or a light herb accompaniment.

That does not mean fillet must be paired with Pinot Noir. A balanced Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon can work with a rich sauce, while fuller Chardonnay or sparkling wine may suit a lean steak with béarnaise or herb butter.

## How cooking method changes the pairing

### Grilled or barbecued steak

Grilling and barbecue can add browned crust, char, smoke, salt and rendered fat. These flavours often support a more intense wine, including Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon.

However, barbecue does not automatically require the biggest red in the cupboard. A sweet sauce or chilli-heavy marinade can make a high-alcohol, highly tannic wine feel harsh. A fruit-forward, medium-bodied or fresher style may be more comfortable.

### Pan-seared or butter-basted steak

Pan-searing creates a savoury browned crust without necessarily adding the same smoky character as a barbecue. Butter, garlic, herbs and pan juices add richness and aroma.

A medium- to full-bodied red can suit a rich preparation. Pinot Noir may be more appropriate when the steak is lean and served with mushrooms or delicate herbs. Fuller Chardonnay can also work when butter, cream or mushrooms dominate the dish.

### Slow-cooked beef

Slow cooking often produces tender meat, concentrated savoury flavours and a rich sauce or jus. A substantial Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon or structured blend can be a useful starting point.

Here, the sauce may matter more than the beef. A wine that is too firm or austere can seem hard beside a sweet or sharply reduced sauce.

Doneness can influence texture and browned flavour, but it should not be used as a rigid rule. Cut, seasoning, cooking method and sauce remain important.

## Let the sauce guide the choice

### Pepper sauce

Pepper adds pungency and warmth. Rather than automatically choosing the most tannic red, try a fruit-forward wine with moderate tannin and enough freshness. Very high alcohol or firm tannin can feel more aggressive alongside strong pepper.

### Mushroom sauce

Mushrooms bring savoury, earthy flavours, while cream or butter adds richness. Pinot Noir can complement a lighter mushroom preparation. A medium-bodied Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon may work with a richer sauce. Fuller Chardonnay is another option when cream or butter is the main feature.

### Béarnaise

Béarnaise is buttery, aromatic and lightly acidic. Freshness is useful here. A medium-bodied red can work with a substantial steak, while sparkling wine or fuller Chardonnay may suit a lean steak where the sauce is the dominant feature.

### Herb butter

Herb butter adds fat, salt and aromatic freshness. A wine with acidity can refresh the palate. Pinot Noir may suit lean steak and delicate herbs; Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon can suit a more heavily grilled steak with robust seasoning.

### Barbecue-style sauce

Barbecue sauce may combine smoke, salt, spice and sweetness. A fruit-forward Shiraz is one possible match, but avoid assuming every Shiraz is equally ripe, full-bodied or high in alcohol. Cabernet Sauvignon can work when the sauce is savoury and the steak is strongly charred.

If the sauce is noticeably sweet, a very austere wine may taste harder and more bitter by contrast.

### Chimichurri

Chimichurri brings herbs, acidity, oil, garlic and sometimes chilli. Freshness and moderate alcohol are useful qualities. Pinot Noir, a medium-bodied Shiraz, fresh rosé or sparkling wine can all be considered, depending on the steak and the strength of the sauce.

## Australian wine styles to consider

Australian Shiraz is not one fixed style. It can be fresh and savoury, or rich and full-bodied, depending on region, climate, vintage and winemaking. A fuller style may suit rib eye, barbecue or slow-cooked beef, while a fresher style can be more flexible with rump or sirloin.

Australian Cabernet Sauvignon also varies. It is often associated with firm structure and tannin, but regional expression and winemaking make a significant difference. A structured Cabernet can suit charred steak or slow-cooked beef; a more elegant style may suit a leaner cut.

Pinot Noir is useful when you want freshness, moderate body or restrained tannin. Chardonnay, rosé and sparkling wine are not forbidden with steak: they can work with lean cuts, buttery sauces, fresh herb sauces or lighter preparations.

## A simple decision framework

When choosing a bottle, use this sequence:

1. **Choose the cut:** fatty and rich, or lean and delicate?
2. **Consider the cooking:** grilled, barbecued, pan-seared or slow-cooked?
3. **Identify the strongest accompaniment:** pepper, mushroom, béarnaise, herb butter, barbecue sauce or chimichurri.
4. **Choose the wine’s response:**
   - richness needs freshness and/or suitable structure;
   - delicate meat needs restraint;
   - char and smoke need sufficient intensity;
   - sweet sauce needs fruit and moderate tannin;
   - sharp herb sauce benefits from freshness;
   - buttery sauce can work with acidity or bubbles.
5. **Taste the combination:** does the wine seem fresher, flatter, hotter, more bitter, sharper or more balanced with the food?

That final observation matters. A pairing is not a test with one correct answer. If you enjoy the combination, it is working for you.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is red wine always best with steak?

No. Red wine is common because many red styles provide body, fruit, tannin and savoury character, but white, rosé and sparkling wines can work with particular cuts, sauces and preparations.

### Is Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon better with steak?

Neither is universally better. A rich, structured Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon can suit a fatty or charred steak, but a fresher or lighter style may be better with a lean cut or delicate sauce. The individual wine matters more than the variety name alone.

### What wine goes with fillet steak?

Pinot Noir is a useful starting point because its freshness and moderate structure may suit fillet’s lean, delicate character. Fuller Chardonnay, sparkling wine or a balanced red can also work, especially with béarnaise, mushrooms or herb butter.

### What wine goes with barbecue steak?

A fruit-forward Australian Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon can suit char, smoke and rich barbecue flavours. If the sauce is sweet or hot, consider a wine with moderate tannin, freshness and fruit rather than simply choosing the most powerful red.

### Can you drink white wine with steak?

Yes. Fuller Chardonnay or sparkling wine can work with lean steak, béarnaise, herb butter, mushrooms or a fresh sauce. It is a less conventional choice, but pairing depends on the whole dish rather than a red-meat rule.
