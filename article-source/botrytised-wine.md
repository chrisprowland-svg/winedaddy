# What Is Botrytised Wine? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Botrytised wine is made from grapes affected by *Botrytis cinerea* in its beneficial “noble rot” form.
- Noble rot partially dehydrates grapes, concentrating their juice and changing their aromas and flavours.
- Botrytised wines are usually sweet, but “botrytised” does not guarantee one sweetness level or taste.
- The same fungus can also cause destructive grey rot, which is a different and undesirable condition.
- Late-harvest wine, icewine and fortified wine can all be sweet, but they are made in different ways.
- In Australia, botrytised Sémillon is associated with humid growing areas including the Riverina and Hunter Valley.

## Quick answer: what is botrytised wine?

Botrytised wine is made from grapes affected by *Botrytis cinerea* in the specific form of infection called noble rot. Under suitable conditions, the fungus causes the grapes to lose some water and become concentrated. It also changes the grapes’ chemistry and contributes distinctive aromas and flavours.

The resulting wines are commonly sweet, rich and aromatic, often with noticeable acidity to help balance the sweetness. However, botrytised wine is not defined simply by being sweet. The final character depends on the grape variety, how extensively the grapes are affected, when they are harvested and how the wine is fermented and matured.

The key idea is this: **“botrytised” describes the involvement of noble rot in the grapes, not one fixed sweetness level or flavour profile.**

## What does “noble rot” mean?

Noble rot is the beneficial form of *Botrytis cinerea* infection on ripe grapes. Instead of quickly destroying the fruit, the infection develops in a way that partially shrivels the berries while leaving them suitable for winemaking.

Water loss concentrates the remaining grape juice. Fungal activity also changes the berry’s composition and helps create aromas that ordinary ripening does not produce in quite the same way. This is why botrytised wines can taste unusually concentrated and complex.

The process is not automatic. Noble rot depends on several interacting factors, including grape maturity, moisture, drying conditions, variety, berry condition and vineyard microclimate. A simple phrase such as “moist mornings and dry afternoons” can be a useful introduction, but it is not a universal formula.

For a deeper explanation of the fungus and the noble-rot process, see [Botrytis and noble rot](/botrytis-and-noble-rot/). This page focuses on the finished wine and how to understand it.

## Noble rot is not the same as grey rot

The name *Botrytis cinerea* does not automatically mean something good for wine. Under unsuitable conditions, the fungus can cause Botrytis bunch rot, also called grey mould or grey rot. This destructive infection can soften and damage grapes and may contribute unwanted effects in wine.

Noble rot is therefore a specific outcome, not a synonym for any mould on grapes. The difference depends on how the infection develops and whether the fruit remains concentrated and suitable for harvest and fermentation.

That distinction matters when reading wine descriptions. “Botrytised” refers to grapes affected by noble rot. It should not be understood as “wine made from spoiled grapes”.

## How do botrytised grapes become wine?

The broad pathway is easier to understand than the technical details:

1. Ripe grapes remain on the vine long enough for noble rot to develop.
2. *Botrytis cinerea* affects the berries.
3. Under suitable conditions, the berries partially shrivel rather than rapidly decay.
4. Water loss concentrates sugars and other grape components.
5. The infection changes the grapes’ aroma and chemical profile.
6. Grapes are selected and harvested as they reach a suitable condition.
7. The concentrated juice is fermented into wine.

Noble rot can develop unevenly, even within the same bunch. This means harvest selection can be important: grapes may not all be ready at the same time. Fermentation can also be slow or difficult because the juice is highly concentrated and its composition has changed.

These production choices help explain why botrytised wine is often made in relatively small amounts, but the amount made varies by place, season and winemaking approach. It is safer to focus on the method than to assume that every botrytised wine is produced in exactly the same way.

## What does botrytised wine taste like?

Many botrytised wines combine sweetness with acidity and concentration. Acidity can make a sweet wine feel fresher and more balanced rather than simply heavy.

Possible aroma and flavour descriptions include:

- honey;
- apricot and other stone fruit;
- peach;
- pear or quince;
- citrus;
- raisin and dried fruit; and
- spice or savoury, earthy notes.

These are reference points, not a checklist. Not every botrytised wine will show all of them, and the balance between sweetness, acidity, alcohol, texture and aroma can vary considerably.

A wine can also be intensely aromatic without being defined only by sweetness. This is where the distinction between flavour intensity and dryness is useful: a wine’s fruit or honeyed character does not, by itself, tell you how sweet it is.

## Is botrytised wine always sweet?

No. Most botrytised wines are sweet because noble rot concentrates sugar in the grapes, and many are made as dessert wines. But “botrytised” identifies the grape condition and production pathway. It does not guarantee one particular level of residual sugar.

The finished wine depends on how much noble rot developed, how the grapes were selected and how fermentation proceeded. For that reason, “usually sweet” or “commonly sweet” is more accurate than “always sweet”.

You can learn more about the difference between sweetness and other aspects of wine in [What Is Sweetness in Wine?](/what-is-sweetness-in-wine/) and [What Is Sweet Wine?](/what-is-sweet-wine/).

## Botrytised wine compared with other sweet wines

Botrytised wine is often discussed alongside dessert and sweet wines, but the terms are not interchangeable.

| Wine or term | What creates the concentration or sweetness? | Is noble rot required? |
|---|---|---|
| **Botrytised wine** | Noble-rot infection, dehydration and biochemical change | Yes |
| **Late-harvest wine** | Grapes remain on the vine longer and become riper | No |
| **Icewine** | Grapes are frozen on the vine or harvested frozen, depending on the style and rules | No |
| **Fortified wine** | Grape spirit is added during production | No |
| **Dessert wine** | A broad style or serving category, commonly associated with sweetness | No |
| **Straw wine or passito** | Grapes are dried after or around harvest | No |

A late-harvest wine may also be botrytised, but late harvest alone does not prove that noble rot was involved. Icewine achieves concentration through freezing rather than fungal infection. Fortified wine is defined by spirit addition, not noble rot.

These methods can produce wines that share sweetness or concentration, but they are not the same production story. For further comparison, see [What Is Late-Harvest Wine?](/late-harvest-wine/) and [What Is Icewine?](/icewine/).

## Botrytised wine in Australia

Australian learners may encounter botrytised Sémillon in discussions of late-harvest and sweet wine. Wine Australia’s educational material identifies humid growing areas including the Riverina and Hunter Valley in connection with Australian Botrytis Sémillon, and associates the style with descriptors such as apricot, citrus and honey.

This is useful context, but it should not be read as a claim that all Australian botrytised wine comes from those areas or that production is consistently prominent there. Wine Australia also discusses *Botrytis* as a serious bunch-rot disease, which is an important reminder that the fungus is not normally beneficial simply because it is present.

The words “botrytised”, “botrytis” and “noble rot” may be used descriptively in wine discussions and on particular labels. There is not enough evidence here to present “botrytised” as one universal protected Australian legal category.

## How should a beginner approach the term?

When you see “botrytised” or “noble rot”, ask three simple questions:

1. **Is the wine being described as sweet, dry or somewhere between?** The word botrytised alone does not answer this.
2. **What other information is given about the grapes or harvest?** Late harvest may overlap with noble rot, but it is not the same thing.
3. **Are the tasting notes describing concentration, acidity and aroma separately?** Honeyed or dried-fruit aromas do not automatically tell you how sweet the wine will taste.

The most useful summary is that botrytised wine is a wine of **noble-rot origin**. Its sweetness, acidity, texture and aromas are results of that origin combined with the decisions made during harvest and winemaking.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is botrytised wine a dessert wine?

Often, yes. Many botrytised wines are sweet and served as dessert wines. But dessert wine is a broad category, and not every dessert wine is botrytised.

### Is botrytised wine the same as late-harvest wine?

No. Late harvest describes when grapes are picked. Botrytised describes the involvement of noble rot. A wine can be late-harvest, botrytised, or both.

### Is botrytised wine the same as icewine?

No. Icewine is concentrated through freezing. Botrytised wine is concentrated and transformed through noble-rot infection.

### Does botrytised wine taste like mould?

It can have distinctive earthy, savoury or fungus-related notes, but these should not automatically be confused with destructive grey rot or spoilage. Honey, stone fruit, citrus and dried-fruit descriptors are also associated with the style.

### Is all Botrytis bad for grapes?

No, but it is not all beneficial either. *Botrytis cinerea* can produce noble rot under suitable conditions, or destructive grey mould and bunch rot under unsuitable ones.
