# Wine with pizza: how to choose the right style

## Highlights

- There is no single best wine for every pizza.
- For a typical tomato-and-cheese pizza, start with a fresh, medium-bodied red such as Sangiovese.
- Tomato sauce usually benefits from freshness and acidity.
- Cheese, oil, salt and meat can support a wine with more body, fruit and moderate tannin.
- Chilli can make high-alcohol wine feel hotter, so fruit-forward, moderate-alcohol styles are often safer.
- Dry rosé, sparkling wine and fresh white wine can work beautifully with lighter pizzas.
- Choose for the pizza’s dominant ingredient, not simply because it is pizza.

## Quick answer: what wine goes with pizza?

For a typical tomato-and-cheese pizza, try a fresh, medium-bodied red such as Sangiovese, Grenache or a lighter-bodied Shiraz. Sangiovese is a particularly useful starting point because its tangy acidity can stay lively alongside tomato sauce.

If the pizza is lighter, dry rosé or sparkling wine may be even more refreshing. Fresh white wine can also work with seafood, herbs or a white pizza without tomato.

The important idea is that pizza is not one flavour. A Margherita, pepperoni, mushroom, seafood or chilli pizza can all need different wine. Look first at the sauce, cheese, salt, oil, toppings and overall intensity. For a broader explanation of these principles, see [how wine pairing works](/how-wine-pairing-works/).

## Why tomato pizza usually likes fresh wine

Tomato sauce has noticeable acidity. Beside it, a soft, low-acid wine can taste flat. A wine with freshness—an appetising, mouth-watering quality—usually keeps the pairing lively.

That is why Sangiovese is a useful reference for tomato-based pizza. It is not the only answer, and the grape name does not guarantee one fixed style. A fresh Australian Sangiovese may be quite different from a richer example.

Fresh Grenache, lighter Shiraz, dry rosé and sparkling wine can follow the same general principle. The best choice depends on how much cheese, oil, meat and seasoning the pizza carries.

## Match the wine to the pizza style

### Margherita

Tomato, mozzarella and basil make a relatively moderate-intensity pizza. Try Sangiovese, light-to-medium-bodied Pinot Noir, dry rosé, fresh Sauvignon Blanc or sparkling wine.

A very tannic or high-alcohol red may overwhelm the pizza. A lively wine with moderate body usually lets the tomato, cheese and herbs remain distinct.

### Pepperoni or salami

Cured meat brings salt, fat, spice and savoury flavour. Fruit-forward Grenache, medium-bodied Shiraz or Sangiovese are useful red starting points. Dry rosé and sparkling wine can also refresh the palate.

Moderate tannin may work well with salty, oily toppings, but the biggest red in the bottle shop is not automatically the best match. Too much tannin can feel drying, particularly if the pizza itself is not very rich.

### Meat-heavy or smoky pizza

Sausage, bacon, barbecue-style toppings and smoky flavours can support more body and intensity. Consider a medium-bodied Shiraz, a fresh Grenache-Shiraz blend or another moderately structured red.

Pay attention to the sauce. Sweet barbecue sauce and chilli can make the pizza taste quite different from one topped with roasted meat and herbs.

### Mushroom or vegetarian pizza

Mushrooms bring savoury, earthy and umami flavours. Pinot Noir, Sangiovese and other fresh, medium-bodied reds may suit roasted mushroom toppings. Chardonnay or sparkling wine can be better when the pizza is lighter, herb-led or simply more delicate.

Strong umami can make firm tannins seem more bitter, so avoid assuming that a heavily structured red is needed. The cheese, garlic, herbs and cooking method matter too. For a deeper look at the ingredient, see [wine with mushrooms](/wine-with-mushrooms/).

### Ham and pineapple

This combination brings salt, savoury flavour, acidity and sweetness. Dry rosé, sparkling wine, fresh Riesling or Sauvignon Blanc can all be useful starting points. A fruit-forward light red may work too.

The wine does not need to be sweet. A dry wine can taste fruity without containing noticeable sweetness. If the pineapple or sauce is particularly sweet, a gently off-dry style may feel more comfortable than a very austere wine.

### Spicy pizza

Chilli changes the pairing. Alcohol can make heat feel stronger, while high tannin may seem more aggressive. Try a fruit-forward red with moderate alcohol, dry rosé, sparkling wine or a dry-to-slightly off-dry Riesling.

A little sweetness can soften chilli, but spicy pizza does not always require sweet wine. The amount of heat and your own preference matter. Our guide to [wine and spicy food](/wine-and-spicy-food-pairing/) explains the principle in more detail.

### Seafood pizza

Seafood pizza may be delicate, salty, herb-led, tomato-based or spicy. Fresh Sauvignon Blanc, unoaked or fresh Chardonnay, Riesling, sparkling wine and dry rosé are useful options.

If the pizza includes a strong tomato sauce, smoked seafood or chilli, a more substantial wine may be appropriate. Start with the preparation rather than treating all seafood pizzas alike.

### White or cream-based pizza

A white pizza usually has no tomato sauce and may be built around cheese, oil, garlic, herbs or cream. Fresh Chardonnay, sparkling wine, dry rosé and Pinot Noir can work. A fresh, textured white may also suit a richer version.

The wine needs enough freshness to refresh the richness, but not so much intensity that it hides the cheese and toppings. See [wine with creamy sauces](/wine-with-creamy-sauces/) for the related pairing principle.

## Red, white, rosé or sparkling?

Red wine is a natural starting point for tomato, meat and strongly seasoned pizza, but it is not a rule. White wine can be excellent with seafood, herbs, lighter vegetables and white pizza. Rosé offers freshness with enough fruit for many toppings, while sparkling wine is useful when salt, oil and cheese make the palate feel heavy.

Think about style rather than grape name alone. Australian Shiraz ranges from medium- to full-bodied, and Australian Chardonnay ranges from crisp and fresh to richer and riper. “Fresh Chardonnay” and “medium-bodied Shiraz” tell you more about the likely pairing than the variety by itself.

## Three simple adjustments

- **Pizza feels oily or rich?** Choose more freshness, such as sparkling wine, dry rosé or a lively red.
- **Pizza is salty and meaty?** Consider fruit and moderate tannin, rather than an extremely firm red.
- **Pizza is very spicy?** Look for moderate alcohol, plenty of fruit and, if you enjoy it, a touch of sweetness.

Australian readers may encounter Italian-inspired, meat-heavy, barbecue-style, seafood and contemporary pizzas at home, restaurants or as takeaway. There is no need to follow a rigid rule. Choose a wine style that matches the strongest feature of the pizza, serve it in a way you enjoy and adjust next time if the balance feels off.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is red wine the best wine with pizza?

Not always. Fresh red wine is a useful default for tomato and meat, but white, rosé and sparkling wines can be better with lighter, seafood or white pizzas.

### Is Sangiovese the perfect pizza wine?

No wine is perfect for every pizza. Sangiovese is a sensible starting point for tomato-and-cheese pizza because its freshness can work well beside tomato sauce.

### Can I drink Chardonnay with pizza?

Yes. Choose a fresh or moderately rich Chardonnay with white pizza, seafood pizza or lighter toppings. A very rich style may overwhelm a delicate pizza.

### Should spicy pizza be paired with sweet wine?

Not necessarily. Slight sweetness can soften chilli, but fruit-forward dry wines, rosé and sparkling wine may also work. Avoid assuming that high alcohol will help: it can intensify the sensation of heat.

### What should I choose at a bottle shop?

Describe the pizza rather than asking for “a pizza wine”. For example: “I’m having a spicy pepperoni pizza—could you suggest a fruit-forward, moderate-alcohol red?” That gives the staff a useful starting point.
