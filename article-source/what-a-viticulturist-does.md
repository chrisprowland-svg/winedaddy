# What does a viticulturist do?

## Highlights

- A viticulturist works with grapevines and vineyards.
- Viticulture is the cultivation and management of grapevines, especially for grape production.
- The role covers decisions across the growing season, from pruning and vine health to harvest timing.
- A viticulturist responds to the site, weather, water, soil, vines and intended grape use.
- A grape grower, vineyard manager and viticulturist may be different people—or the same person.
- Vineyard decisions can influence the grapes and the wine’s possibilities, but they do not determine the finished wine alone.

## In short

A **viticulturist grows and manages grapevines**. Their work may include planning vineyard activities, monitoring vine health, managing the canopy and crop, responding to water and weather conditions, watching for pests and disease, assessing grape maturity and helping decide when to harvest.

The role is mainly on the **grape-growing side** of wine production. A winemaker works primarily with harvested grapes in the winery, turning them into wine. The two roles collaborate, particularly around grape condition and harvest timing.

A viticulturist does not simply follow the same checklist every year. Vineyard decisions change with the site, season, grape variety, water availability, vine growth and intended use of the grapes.

## What is viticulture?

**Viticulture** is the cultivation, management and harvesting of grapevines. In plain English, it is the work and knowledge involved in growing grapes and caring for the vineyard.

A viticulturist applies that knowledge. Depending on the workplace, they may make technical decisions, advise a grower, supervise vineyard work, analyse vineyard conditions or take part in hands-on activities. They do not necessarily perform every task personally.

The work begins well before harvest. Healthy, appropriately developed grapes are the result of many observations and decisions made throughout the vine’s annual cycle.

## What does a viticulturist do through the year?

The exact timing varies with Australia’s climate, grape variety, site and season, but the broad cycle looks like this.

### Dormancy and winter planning

When vines are dormant, vineyard teams prepare for the coming growing season. This can include planning priorities and making pruning decisions that help shape the vine and its future growth.

Pruning is only one part of the role. A viticulturist may also review the previous season, consider vineyard variation and plan how work will be organised.

### Budburst, flowering and fruit set

As vines begin growing, the focus turns to development and risk. A viticulturist observes budburst, shoot growth and vine health, while considering weather conditions such as frost.

Flowering and fruit set are important stages in the formation of the crop. Cold or unsettled conditions at these times can affect yield, so the season’s progress may require close monitoring rather than assumptions based on last year.

### Canopy and crop management

The **canopy** is the vine’s arrangement of leaves and shoots. Canopy management is the broad process of managing leaves, shoots and fruit from pruning through to harvest.

A viticulturist may consider how much foliage and fruit a vine is carrying, how evenly the vineyard is developing, and how much exposure and airflow the grapes receive. This is sometimes described as managing **vine balance**.

**Crop load** means the amount of fruit a vine is carrying. Too much or too little is not automatically good or bad in every situation. The appropriate balance depends on the vineyard, variety, season and intended grape use.

These decisions can influence how evenly grapes ripen and may affect grape composition. They are not guarantees of a particular flavour or wine style.

### Water, soil and vineyard health

Vineyards differ in soil, topography, vine vigour, water availability and fruit development. A viticulturist observes these differences and helps decide how the vineyard should be managed.

Water may be a significant consideration where rainfall is unreliable or seasonal demand is high, but Australian vineyards do not all face the same conditions. Soil moisture, vine growth and signs of stress can all contribute to decisions.

Pests and diseases are also monitored. The aim is to identify risks early and coordinate suitable vineyard responses within the property’s management system. The role is not simply reacting to problems after they appear; regular observation matters.

### Veraison, ripening and harvest

**Veraison** is the stage when grapes begin changing as they ripen. In dark-skinned varieties, berries begin to develop colour; in all varieties, it marks a noticeable shift towards ripening.

At this stage, a viticulturist may monitor grape maturity, vine stress, heat exposure, disease risk and fruit condition. Heat can contribute to sunburn, shrivel and water stress, depending on the conditions.

Harvest preparation involves sampling and observing grapes, assessing the vineyard’s readiness and coordinating with the winemaking team. The question is not simply “Are the grapes ripe?” It may also be “Are they in the condition and style of maturity needed for this particular wine?”

## How are vineyard decisions made?

A viticulturist combines experience, observation and measurements. Useful observations may include:

- recent and forecast weather;
- soil moisture and water availability;
- vine growth and canopy density;
- flowering, fruit set, veraison and ripening;
- crop load and vine balance;
- signs of pests, disease or heat stress;
- grape maturity and fruit condition;
- differences between parts of the same vineyard.

This is why two sections of one vineyard may receive different attention. Soil, slope, exposure, drainage and vine vigour can vary over a relatively small area.

Australian vineyards span cool and warm growing conditions, and sites with very different rainfall and water patterns. Depending on the vineyard and season, viticulturists may also need to consider frost, heavy rainfall, disease pressure, drought, heat, bushfire or smoke-related risks. No single Australian vineyard calendar or response applies everywhere.

## Viticulturist, grape grower, vineyard manager or winemaker?

These terms overlap, and employers do not always use them in exactly the same way.

- **Grape grower:** the person or business responsible for producing grapes. A grower may own a vineyard, manage one or sell grapes under contract.
- **Viticulturist:** someone whose work or expertise focuses on cultivating and managing grapevines. They may be a specialist, adviser, grower or member of a vineyard team.
- **Vineyard manager:** generally the person coordinating the practical operation of a vineyard, including timing, people and day-to-day work. The role may overlap substantially with viticulture.
- **Winemaker:** the person primarily responsible for making wine from harvested grapes and managing winery decisions.

In a small vineyard, one person may be the grape grower, viticulturist and vineyard manager. In a larger business, technical advice, operational management and physical vineyard work may be shared among several people, including an agronomist and seasonal vineyard teams.

So the useful distinction is not a rigid list of job titles. It is the difference between **growing the grapes** and **making the wine**, while recognising that the people involved may work across both sides.

## How can a viticulturist’s work affect wine?

Vineyard decisions can influence grape ripeness, flavour development, acidity, tannin-related composition, disease risk and consistency. For example, managing the relationship between foliage and fruit may affect ripening and grape exposure to sunlight and airflow. Water status, heat and crop level can also influence grape development.

But a viticulturist does not control the flavour of the finished wine. Vineyard work helps create the grapes and their potential. Harvest handling, fermentation, maturation, blending and other winery decisions also shape what eventually reaches the bottle.

The best way to think about it is this: the viticulturist helps determine the condition and possibilities of the raw material, while the winemaking team works with that material to produce a particular wine.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a viticulturist the same as a grape grower?

Not always. A grape grower is responsible for producing grapes, while “viticulturist” more often highlights specialist knowledge or professional work in grape cultivation. One person may be both.

### Does a viticulturist make wine?

Usually, the viticulturist works mainly in the vineyard and the winemaker works mainly in the winery. They may collaborate, especially when discussing grape condition and harvest timing.

### Does every vineyard employ a viticulturist?

No. Some vineyards use a specialist viticulturist, while others rely on a grower, vineyard manager or adviser who combines several responsibilities.

### What is the simplest definition of viticulture?

Viticulture is the cultivation and management of grapevines.

## The takeaway

A viticulturist manages the living, changing part of wine production: the vineyard. By observing vines, weather, soil, water, crop development and grape maturity, they help create healthy grapes suited to their intended use. Their decisions matter, but the final wine is the result of both vineyard and winery work.
