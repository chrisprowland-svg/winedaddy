# Marsanne and Roussanne in Australia

Marsanne and Roussanne are two different white grape varieties that are often discussed together. They have a long-standing blending relationship: Marsanne tends to bring body, weight and texture, while Roussanne can contribute fragrance, freshness and definition.

Those are broad tendencies, not rules. In Australia, both grapes are made as single-variety wines and used in blends. Their final style depends on the region, vintage, harvest timing, blending proportions and winemaking choices.

## Highlights

- Marsanne and Roussanne are separate grape varieties, not two names for the same grape.
- They are often blended because their broad strengths can complement each other.
- Australian examples may be varietal, Marsanne–Roussanne blends or broader white Rhône-style blends.
- Marsanne has its clearest documented Australian association with the Goulburn Valley, with production also recorded elsewhere.
- Roussanne is grown on a smaller scale, and its Australian geography is less clearly mapped in the available national evidence.
- Neither grape has one fixed Australian flavour. Site, climate, vintage and winemaking all matter.

## Quick answer: what should Australian wine learners know?

Marsanne and Roussanne are related Rhône white grapes with overlapping flavours but different typical roles.

Marsanne is often associated with a fuller, rounder palate and a sense of weight or generosity. It can show pear, melon, citrus, stone fruit, floral, honeyed or waxy characters.

Roussanne is often associated with more pronounced fragrance and lift. It can show floral, herbal, pear, stone-fruit, honeyed or savoury notes, alongside a textured palate.

These descriptions are useful starting points, not tasting tests. Oak, lees contact, skin contact, bottle age and serving temperature can make the differences less obvious. A Roussanne wine is not always more aromatic, and Marsanne is not always fuller-bodied or lower in acidity.

In Australia, the most useful way to understand the pair is as **related but not interchangeable varieties**. You may find them separately, together, or in a broader blend with other white Rhône varieties.

## Why are Marsanne and Roussanne discussed together?

The relationship is historical, practical and sensory.

Both varieties are traditionally associated with the northern Rhône, where they have been used as partners in white wines. Their broad blending roles are complementary: Marsanne can provide palate weight and texture, while Roussanne can add perfume, freshness or finer definition.

That does not mean every blend follows the same recipe. A winemaker may use different proportions, harvest each variety at a different time or ferment them in different vessels before combining them. The resulting wine might be rich and rounded, fragrant and savoury, or fresher and more restrained.

The varieties can also be made separately. For a deeper explanation of each grape’s general identity, see our guides to [Marsanne](/marsanne/) and [Roussanne](/roussanne/).

## Where are they grown in Australia?

Both grapes are small-volume varieties nationally. Wine Australia’s 2024 National Vintage Report recorded 737 tonnes of Marsanne and 492 tonnes of Roussanne crushed across Australia.

Marsanne has the clearest documented regional pattern. Wine Australia’s 2025 Marsanne snapshot reported the largest shares of Marsanne crush from:

- Goulburn Valley, Victoria
- Riverina, New South Wales
- McLaren Vale, South Australia
- Barossa Valley, South Australia
- Heathcote, Victoria

The Goulburn Valley accounted for the largest reported share in that snapshot. This means it is a significant documented location for Australian Marsanne production; it does not mean Marsanne dominates the region’s vineyards or wines.

Roussanne is also grown in Australia, but current national evidence gives a less complete regional picture. Dated production evidence records Roussanne in McLaren Vale, while documented Australian blend examples occur in places including the Yarra Valley and Margaret River. These are useful examples, not proof that any one region is Australia’s principal Roussanne home.

For broader orientation, you can explore [Australian wine regions](/australian-wine-regions/). Remember that a region’s reputation for a variety is not the same as a guarantee that every bottle from that region will taste alike.

## What might you see on an Australian label?

Australian labels can identify a single variety, multiple varieties, a geographical indication (GI), or a combination of these.

A bottle labelled **Marsanne** is not necessarily 100 per cent Marsanne. Wine Australia’s blending rules require at least 85 per cent of the named variety for a single-variety claim.

When two or more varieties are named, they are listed in descending order of proportion. So **Marsanne–Roussanne** indicates a blend in which Marsanne makes up at least as much of the named composition as Roussanne.

A regional name communicates origin, while a variety name communicates grape composition. A GI claim generally requires at least 85 per cent of the fruit to come from that GI. These are different kinds of information, so a label might tell you both where the fruit came from and which grapes are in the wine.

For more on the idea of combining grape varieties, see [wine blends](/wine-blends/) or [blending wine](/blending-wine/).

## How might the wines taste?

Think of the following as a comparison of tendencies rather than a checklist.

### Marsanne

Marsanne can feel rounded, generous or moderately to fully bodied. Common descriptions include pear, melon, citrus, stone fruit, flowers and honey. With development, some examples can take on waxy or nutty characters.

### Roussanne

Roussanne can be noticeably fragrant, with floral, herbal, pear, stone-fruit or honeyed notes. It may also bring savoury detail and a textured palate.

The two profiles overlap. Both can be aromatic, textured and capable of developing richer flavours with age. Their differences may be clearest in a simple comparison of young, similarly made wines, but Australian wines can vary considerably.

## How do Australian examples vary?

Climate and site influence the grapes, but they do not dictate a single flavour. Harvest timing, fruit condition, vintage, crop level, fermentation vessel, lees work, skin contact and blending decisions can all change the result.

A warmer inland site may produce a richer or riper expression in some years. A cooler site or earlier-picked wine may retain more freshness. Oak or lees contact may add texture, while skin contact can alter both colour and structure. These are possible influences, not promises.

That is why two Australian Marsanne wines—or two Marsanne–Roussanne blends—may taste quite different while both being genuine examples of the varieties.

## What should you look for when choosing or tasting one?

Start with the label:

1. Is it Marsanne, Roussanne, or a blend?
2. Is a region named as well as the variety?
3. If both varieties are listed, which appears first?
4. Does the description suggest a fresh, textured, aromatic or more developed style?

Then taste for structure rather than trying to identify every aroma. Notice whether the wine feels light or weighty, crisp or rounded, softly textured or firm. Look for the combination of fragrance and palate weight that can make a Marsanne–Roussanne blend interesting.

Most importantly, treat variety descriptions as useful signposts, not guarantees. The bottle in your glass reflects grapes, place, season and winemaking together.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are Marsanne and Roussanne the same grape?

No. They are distinct white grape varieties that are often grown and blended together.

### Are they always blended?

No. Australian examples include single-variety wines, Marsanne–Roussanne blends and broader white Rhône-style blends.

### Which is sweeter?

Neither grape is inherently sweet. Fruit aromas such as pear or honey do not necessarily mean the wine contains noticeable sugar.

### Is Marsanne more important in Australia than Roussanne?

The available evidence records more Marsanne than Roussanne crushed nationally in 2024, and provides a clearer regional pattern for Marsanne. That does not make either grape nationally dominant.

### Does a Marsanne–Roussanne label guarantee a particular taste?

No. The blend can vary with grape proportions, region, vintage and winemaking.
