# What Is Wine Finish?

## Highlights

- **Wine finish** is what remains after you swallow or spit the wine out.
- It includes lingering flavour, aroma, acidity, sweetness, tannin and mouthfeel.
- A finish can be short, medium or long—but there are no universal time limits.
- Finish describes both **how long** sensations last and **what** those sensations are like.
- A long finish is not automatically better than a short one.
- A simple pause after swallowing or spitting can help you recognise a wine’s finish.

## Quick answer: what does “finish” mean in wine?

Wine finish is the collection of flavours, aromas, tastes and mouthfeel sensations that remain after wine leaves your mouth.

You might notice fresh citrus, red fruit, spice, savoury notes, oak, sweetness, tannin, bitterness or alcohol warmth. The finish describes both the character of those sensations and how long they persist.

For example, a wine might have a **short, clean finish** if its flavours fade quickly but neatly. Another might have a **long, spicy finish** if recognisable spice and fruit remain well after the sip.

When wine professionals talk about finish, they may also use the words **length**, **persistence** or **aftertaste**. These terms overlap in everyday wine writing. Finish is often the broader term because it can include flavour, aroma and mouthfeel—not just the last taste.

## How do you taste a wine’s finish?

Finish is assessed after you swallow or spit the wine out. Try this:

1. Take a small sip and hold it briefly across your palate.
2. Swallow or spit.
3. Pause before taking another sip.
4. Notice what remains.
5. Consider how quickly those sensations fade.
6. Describe their character: fruity, fresh, spicy, savoury, sweet, drying, warm or bitter.
7. Ask whether the persistence is pleasant, neutral or distracting.

You do not need a stopwatch. The aim is to make a clear observation, not to produce a laboratory measurement.

Ask yourself:

- What is the last recognisable flavour?
- Does freshness or acidity remain?
- Is there lingering fruit or spice?
- Does tannin leave a drying grip?
- Does the wine feel hot, bitter or overly sweet?
- Does the finish feel clear and tidy, or muddled?

Taste again if you are unsure. A repeatable impression is more useful than trying to sound technical.

## Short, medium and long finish

Tasting notes commonly describe finish as **short**, **medium** or **long**.

- **Short:** the recognisable sensations fade relatively quickly.
- **Medium:** the sensations remain for a noticeable but moderate period.
- **Long:** recognisable sensations continue for a relatively extended period.

These are useful descriptive categories, not universal scientific scores. There is no agreed rule that a short finish lasts a particular number of seconds or that a long finish must exceed a fixed threshold. Different tasting systems and research methods may assess persistence differently.

The category is only part of the description. A long finish dominated by harsh bitterness or excessive alcohol is not necessarily a positive feature. A shorter finish may be entirely appropriate for a light, fresh style.

## What can contribute to a wine’s finish?

Finish is a whole-wine impression, so it rarely has one simple cause. Several parts of the wine may contribute:

- **Acidity** can leave freshness, tartness or a mouth-watering sensation.
- **Tannin** can leave drying, gripping or textural sensations, particularly in red wine.
- **Sweetness** can leave a rich or sweet impression.
- **Alcohol** can contribute warmth or heat.
- **Flavour and aroma compounds** can remain as fruit, floral, herbal or spicy impressions.
- **Oak** may contribute lingering toast, vanilla, cedar, coconut or spice-like notes.
- **Concentration and texture** may give the wine more sensory presence after the sip.

These elements interact. High alcohol, abundant tannin, oak or concentration does not guarantee a long or enjoyable finish. The wine’s overall composition—and how it is perceived by the individual taster—matters.

## Finish is not the same as intensity or body

These wine terms are related, but they answer different questions.

**Intensity** asks: *How strong is the sensation?*  
**Finish** asks: *What remains after the sip, and how long does it last?*

A wine can be powerful and aromatic but fade quickly. Another can seem restrained at first yet leave a persistent, precise impression.

**Body** describes the wine’s perceived weight or fullness in your mouth. Finish describes what remains afterwards. A full-bodied wine does not automatically have a long finish, and a lighter-bodied wine can still be persistent.

Finish is also different from **balance** and **complexity**. Balance describes how the wine’s components work together. Complexity describes the range and interaction of its sensory characteristics. A long finish may contribute to an impression of quality, but it is not the same thing as balance or complexity.

## Does a long finish mean better wine?

Not necessarily.

A long finish can be a positive feature when pleasant, clear and well integrated sensations continue after the sip. But persistence alone is not a complete measure of quality.

Bitterness, excessive alcohol warmth, coarse drying tannin, intrusive oak or cloying sweetness can also linger. The useful question is not simply, “How long does it last?” It is also, “What is lingering, and do I enjoy it?”

Likewise, a short finish does not automatically mean a wine is poor. Some wines are designed to be light, fresh and immediately drinkable. Quality depends on the whole experience, including balance, concentration, texture, freshness and suitability to the style.

A long finish also does not reliably prove that a wine will improve with age, cost more or receive higher scores.

## How Australians may see finish described

Australian tasting notes may use phrases such as:

- “good length”;
- “persistent”;
- “lingering”;
- “clean finish”;
- “long aftertaste”.

You might find that an Australian Riesling leaves a fresh citrus or mineral-like impression, that a Chardonnay finishes with acidity, fruit, oak or texture, or that a Shiraz leaves fruit, spice, tannin or warmth. These are possible observations, not guarantees. No Australian variety or region has one fixed finish profile.

The same wine may also seem different depending on its serving temperature, the food you have eaten, the glass, tasting order and your own sensitivity.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is finish only the last flavour in wine?

No. Finish can include flavour, retronasal aroma, taste and mouthfeel. The last recognisable flavour is one part of the experience.

### Is finish the same as aftertaste?

The terms overlap. “Aftertaste” usually refers to a taste or sensation that remains, while “finish” is often used more broadly for the whole post-swallow or post-spit experience.

### Can white wine have a long finish?

Yes. White wine can leave persistent acidity, citrus, floral, mineral-like, oak, texture or other sensations. Finish is not limited to red wine.

### How can I describe a finish?

Combine a duration with a character: “short and fresh”, “medium with savoury spice” or “long with fruit and fine tannin”. This is more useful than calling every persistent sensation “good length”.

## The simplest way to remember it

Finish is what remains after the wine is swallowed or spat out. Judge both **duration** and **character**. A long finish can be rewarding, but the best finish is not necessarily the longest one—it is the one that remains pleasant, clear and suited to the wine.
