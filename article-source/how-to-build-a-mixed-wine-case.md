# How to build a mixed wine case

A good mixed wine case is not a random collection of bottles or a fixed split between red, white and sparkling wine. Build it around the people who will drink it, the occasions it needs to cover, the styles they enjoy, your budget and how you will store and serve the wines.

Start with familiar choices, add a little exploration, and only buy as much as your household is likely to use.

## Highlights

Before choosing individual wines, ask:

- Who will drink them?
- What occasions and meals do they need to cover?
- Which styles are already reliable favourites?
- How much variety will be useful?
- What is the budget?
- When will the wines be opened?
- Can the bottles be stored somewhere cool and dark?
- Can chilled wines be cooled before serving?

Then choose broad styles rather than working from a long list of grape varieties. A flexible case might include sparkling wine, fresh whites, fuller whites, rosé, lighter reds, fuller reds, or a sweet or fortified wine if someone will genuinely enjoy it.

There is no universally correct case. A useful case is one the household will actually open, enjoy and finish within its intended timeframe.

## 1. Start with the drinkers

The case should suit real people, not an imaginary “complete” wine collection.

Consider whether the drinkers prefer white, red, rosé, sparkling, sweet or fuller-bodied wines. In a mixed household, one person may prefer crisp, refreshing whites while another reaches for lighter reds. That is a reason to vary the case—not to force every drinker into the same style.

It is also worth separating familiar bottles from exploratory ones. Familiar wines give the case a dependable foundation. Exploratory bottles can introduce a new region, variety, style or sweetness level without making the whole purchase a gamble.

How much exploration feels right is a preference, not a rule. Adventurous drinkers may want more surprises; a case for guests or a gift may need more familiar options.

## 2. Match the case to occasions

List the situations in which the wine is likely to be opened:

- casual drinks at home;
- weeknight or weekend meals;
- entertaining;
- celebrations;
- warm-weather gatherings;
- gifts;
- bottles intended to open soon rather than later.

This changes the shape of the case. A household that mostly drinks outdoors in summer may value more refreshing whites, rosé and sparkling wine. A household that cooks substantial meals may want more medium- or full-bodied wines. A case for occasional entertaining may need versatile styles that can suit different guests.

You do not need to predict every meal. The aim is simply to avoid buying a case that suits only one narrow occasion.

## 3. Choose broad styles before varieties

Grape varieties and regions can be useful clues, but broad style is often the more practical starting point. Think about colour, body, acidity, sweetness, tannin and bubbles.

| Style category | Why it might belong in a case |
|---|---|
| Sparkling | Celebrations, aperitif occasions and lively gatherings |
| Fresh whites | Light, crisp or refreshing drinking |
| Fuller whites | More texture or weight with richer meals |
| Rosé | A flexible option for warm weather and casual meals |
| Lighter reds | Red-wine drinkers who prefer less weight or tannin |
| Fuller reds | Richer meals or people who enjoy more body and structure |
| Sweet or fortified wines | Only when there is a genuine preference or a clear occasion |

This is a planning menu, not a required checklist. A case may reasonably contain no sweet wine, no fortified wine or no full-bodied red. The right category is the one that solves a real need.

Food can also affect the choice. Salt, sweetness, acidity, fat and spice change how a wine tastes, so a wine that works well on its own may feel different with dinner. You do not need a perfect pairing plan; simply consider whether the case will be used mostly with light meals, rich food, spicy dishes or dessert.

## 4. Make the quantity fit the household

A “case” does not have to mean exactly 12 bottles. The useful quantity depends on the number of drinkers, how often wine is opened, upcoming gatherings, storage space and the intended drinking timeframe.

A small household may be better served by a compact selection bought more often. A larger household or regular entertainer may need more bottles, but should still consider whether there is enough room to store them properly.

Avoid buying extra bottles just to make the case look balanced. A case with fewer categories can be better than a larger case containing wines nobody wants.

## 5. Use the label as a guide, not a guarantee

Australian wine labels may foreground the variety, region, brand, vintage or style. These details can help narrow your choice, but they cannot guarantee that a bottle will match your taste.

Before buying, check:

- the stated style or variety;
- region or place information;
- vintage, where shown;
- alcohol by volume;
- standard-drink information;
- bottle size;
- sweetness or other style descriptions, where provided.

Packaged alcohol in Australia must show the number of standard drinks it contains. One Australian standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol. This information helps you understand the alcohol content of different bottles, particularly because strength and bottle size vary.

It is useful information—not a prediction of quality or enjoyment.

## 6. Plan for Australian conditions

Heat matters when transporting and storing wine. Avoid leaving bottles for long periods in a hot car, direct sun or a location beside an oven, heater or hot window.

At home, choose a cool, dark and reasonably stable place. A household case does not require specialist cellaring advice for this basic step; it simply should not sit somewhere that becomes very hot or experiences large temperature swings.

Serving conditions should influence the case too. Sparkling, white and rosé wines generally need chilling before service. Limited refrigerator space may make a case dominated by chilled styles less convenient. Red wine should not automatically be served warm just because it is red; an overheated room can make it seem heavy and unbalanced.

## A final mixed-case checklist

Before you buy, ask:

- Does this suit the people who will drink it?
- Are there enough familiar choices?
- Is there useful variety in colour, body, acidity, sweetness or bubbles?
- Do the quantities reflect actual occasions?
- Have I allowed for the budget without buying random bottles?
- Can the wines be stored and chilled appropriately?
- Am I including any bottle only because it sounds impressive?
- Have I checked the label for alcohol and standard-drink information?
- Would this household actually choose these wines over the coming weeks or months?

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a mixed wine case have to contain 12 bottles?

No. The quantity should match the household, occasions, storage space and likely drinking timeframe. Twelve bottles may be convenient for some purchases, but it is not a universal rule.

### Should every mixed case include red, white and sparkling wine?

No. Include styles that suit the drinkers and occasions. A case can be balanced without containing every possible category.

### How many unfamiliar wines should I include?

There is no correct number. Keep enough familiar choices to make the case useful, then add exploration at a level the drinkers will enjoy.

### Are expensive wines better for a mixed case?

Price alone cannot tell you whether a wine will suit the household. A useful case prioritises fit, variety and occasions rather than prestige.

### Can I store the whole case in a garage?

That depends on the conditions. Avoid locations that become hot, sunny or highly variable in temperature. A cool, dark and stable indoor place is generally a more practical household choice.

### What is the most important rule?

Build the case for actual people and real occasions. The best mixed case is not the most impressive-looking one; it is the one that gets opened and enjoyed.
