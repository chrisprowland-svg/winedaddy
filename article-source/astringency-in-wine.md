# Astringency in Wine: What It Means and How to Recognise It

## Highlights

- Astringency is a **drying, puckering, rough or grippy sensation** in the mouth.
- It is tactile: you feel it rather than taste it as a basic taste.
- Tannins are a major cause of astringency, but tannin and astringency are not the same thing.
- Astringency is different from bitterness, acidity and dry wine.
- It can give wine structure and length, but excessive astringency may feel harsh.
- Astringency can occur in white, rosé and sparkling wines as well as red wines.

## Quick answer: what is astringency in wine?

Astringency is the sensation that makes your mouth feel **dry, puckered, rough or grippy** after drinking wine. You may notice it around your gums, cheeks, tongue or palate, sometimes becoming more obvious after swallowing or spitting.

It is a tactile sensation rather than a basic taste. In many wines, tannins and other phenolic compounds are the main contributors. Tannins can interact with saliva and reduce the feeling of lubrication in your mouth, creating the familiar drying or gripping effect.

That does not mean tannin and astringency are interchangeable. **Tannin is a group of compounds in the wine; astringency is what you feel.**

Astringency is often associated with structured red wines, so Australian learners may encounter words such as *tannic*, *grippy*, *drying* and *structured* when reading about Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon. But no grape variety guarantees a particular level of astringency, and Australian wine is not uniformly tannic or drying.

## Astringency, tannin, acidity and bitterness

These terms can overlap in the same wine, but they describe different things.

| Term | What it means | What you may notice |
|---|---|---|
| **Astringency** | A tactile mouth sensation | Drying, puckering, roughness or grip |
| **Tannin** | A group of phenolic compounds | Structure or drying sensations caused partly by those compounds |
| **Acidity** | The wine’s acid component and its sensory effect | Sourness, freshness and saliva flow |
| **Bitterness** | A taste sensation | A bitter flavour, often on the tongue |
| **Dry wine** | Wine with little perceived sweetness | The wine tastes not sweet |
| **Body** | The wine’s overall weight or volume | Light-, medium- or full-bodied |
| **Texture** | The wider group of tactile sensations | Smooth, silky, creamy, oily, rough or grippy |

The most important beginner distinction is between **dry wine** and **tactile dryness**. In wine language, *dry* usually refers to sweetness: a dry wine has little perceptible residual sugar. A dry wine can feel smooth, while a sweet wine can still create some astringency.

Acidity can also be confused with astringency. Acidity tends to feel sharp, sour or mouth-watering. Astringency tends to feel drying, tightening or rough. A wine may have both.

Bitterness and astringency may also appear together, especially in wines with substantial phenolic material. They are still different sensations: bitterness is a taste, while astringency is mainly something you feel.

## Where does astringency come from?

Grape skins, seeds and sometimes stems contain phenolic compounds, including tannins. During winemaking, contact with these parts can extract some of those compounds into the wine. Oak can also contribute tannins and other phenolic material.

Extraction depends on many interacting factors, including contact time, temperature, fermentation and maceration conditions, the condition and maturity of the grapes, and the wine’s wider chemical makeup. More contact or extraction can increase phenolic material in some circumstances, but it does not provide a simple guarantee of harshness.

Alcohol, acidity, pH, polysaccharides and other components of the wine can change how astringency is perceived. Food, saliva and the order in which wines are tasted can matter too. This is why a grape variety, winemaking technique or label description cannot tell you exactly how a particular wine will feel.

Astringency is usually easier to notice in many red wines because they commonly contain more grape-derived phenolic material. However, it is not limited to red wine. Skin-contact whites, rosé, sparkling wines and oak-influenced whites may also show some drying or grippy qualities, depending on their composition and production.

## Is astringency desirable?

Astringency is not automatically a problem. In the right amount, it can give wine **shape, grip, structure and length**. It may help a wine feel firm and energetic rather than soft or loose.

The same sensation can feel unpleasant when it is coarse, aggressive, intense or out of balance. A wine may then seem harsh or excessively drying. Whether astringency is positive depends on its intensity, how it fits the wine’s other components, the wine’s style and your own preference.

This is also why “more tannin” does not automatically mean “better wine”. Astringency is one part of the overall experience.

## A simple way to taste astringency

Try this with a small pour of a dry, still wine with noticeable structure. A red wine may make the sensation easiest to recognise, but the exercise is about observation rather than measuring tannin.

1. Take a small sip and move it around your mouth briefly.
2. Notice whether your gums, cheeks, tongue or palate feel less lubricated.
3. Look for drying, puckering, roughness, graininess or grip.
4. Ask when it appears: immediately, after swallowing or spitting, or after a few seconds.
5. Notice how long it lasts.
6. Separate the sensation from sourness, bitterness, sweetness and body.
7. Rinse with water and, if needed, use a plain neutral food before comparing it with a softer-feeling wine.

The point is not to decide exactly how much tannin the wine contains. It is to learn what astringency feels like and to describe it separately from other wine characteristics.

## Is astringency a wine fault?

No. Astringency is a normal sensory feature of many wines, particularly structured reds. It may be judged excessive or problematic when it overwhelms the wine, feels harsh or seems poorly balanced.

Astringency is therefore not the same as a fault, and it is not proof of quality, ageworthiness or price. It is simply one part of how a wine feels.

## Common questions

### Can white wine be astringent?

Yes. White wines usually have less grape-derived phenolic material than many red wines, but skin contact, oak and other production choices can create drying or grippy sensations.

### Is tannin the same as astringency?

No. Tannin is a group of compounds; astringency is the sensation those compounds can help create. The relationship is strong, but tannin concentration alone does not predict exactly how a wine will feel.

### Is astringency the same as dryness?

No. Dryness in wine usually refers to low sweetness. Astringency refers to tactile drying, puckering or grip.

### Which Australian wines are the most astringent?

There is no responsible universal ranking. Australian learners may encounter astringency in structured wines made from varieties such as Shiraz or Cabernet Sauvignon, but the result depends on the grapes, winemaking, maturation, wine composition and serving context.

### What should I say when I notice astringency?

You could describe the wine as “drying”, “grippy”, “puckering”, “rough” or “tannic”, depending on what you feel. If the sensation is strong, you might say it is “firm but integrated” or “harsh and drying”. Your own sensory observation is more useful than forcing the wine into a score.
