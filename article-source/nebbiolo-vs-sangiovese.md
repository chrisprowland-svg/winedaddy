# Nebbiolo vs Sangiovese: What’s the Difference?

## Highlights

- Nebbiolo and Sangiovese are different red grape varieties.
- Nebbiolo is usually paler in colour but more firmly tannic and structured.
- Sangiovese is often recognised by sour red cherry, fresh acidity and savoury flavours.
- Nebbiolo is strongly associated with Piedmont, while Sangiovese is strongly associated with Tuscany.
- Barolo and Barbaresco are Nebbiolo-associated wines; Chianti, Chianti Classico and Brunello di Montalcino are Sangiovese-associated wines.
- Both varieties can make ageworthy wine, but neither has one fixed style.
- Australian examples are local expressions, not exact copies of Italian wines.

## Quick answer

Nebbiolo and Sangiovese are both Italian red grape varieties with naturally high acidity and noticeable tannin, but they tend to feel different in the glass.

Nebbiolo is often pale or translucent-looking, yet powerful in structure. It commonly shows red cherry, rose, violet, tar, liquorice, leather and earthy notes. Its tannins can feel firm and mouth-drying when young.

Sangiovese is also fresh and structured, but it is often more immediately recognisable through sour red cherry, red fruit, violet, dried herbs, earth, pepper and savoury flavours. It can feel energetic and especially comfortable with food.

These are tendencies, not rules. Climate, site, vintage, ripeness, yields, extraction, oak, blending and bottle age can change the result considerably.

## Nebbiolo and Sangiovese are grapes, not regional names

A grape variety is the type of vine used to make a wine. A regional wine name usually tells you something about where the wine comes from and how it is classified.

Nebbiolo is the grape most strongly associated with Piedmont in north-western Italy. Barolo and Barbaresco are regulated wines associated with Nebbiolo and particular places. They are not alternative names for the grape.

Sangiovese is especially associated with Tuscany. Chianti, Chianti Classico and Brunello di Montalcino are Tuscan wine names associated with Sangiovese. They are not synonyms for Sangiovese either.

This distinction is useful when reading an Australian or Italian label: **Nebbiolo** and **Sangiovese** name grapes, while names such as **Barolo** and **Chianti Classico** identify regional wines.

## Nebbiolo vs Sangiovese at a glance

| Feature | Nebbiolo | Sangiovese |
|---|---|---|
| Colour | Often pale to medium ruby or garnet | Often light to medium ruby |
| Acidity | High and prominent | High and prominent |
| Tannin | High to very high; often firm when young | Medium-high to high; can also be firm |
| Body | Medium-full to full despite its pale colour | Medium to medium-full, sometimes fuller |
| Fruit character | Red cherry and other red-fruit flavours | Sour red cherry, red plum and sometimes darker fruit |
| Other common notes | Rose, violet, tar, liquorice, leather and earth | Violet, dried herbs, earth, pepper and savoury notes |
| Typical impression | Fragrant, powerful and structured | Fresh, savoury, energetic and food-friendly |

The table describes common patterns rather than guarantees. A warm-climate Sangiovese may be richer and fuller, while a lighter Nebbiolo expression may feel more delicate in fruit but still firm in tannin.

## How do they taste different?

### Nebbiolo: pale colour, serious structure

Nebbiolo can surprise beginners because its colour does not always predict its intensity. It may look relatively light, but high acidity and firm tannin can give the wine considerable presence.

Young Nebbiolo often feels angular or grippy. With time, its tannins may become more integrated, while its aromas can develop further savoury, earthy and leather-like complexity. Rose, red cherry and tar are familiar reference points, but not every Nebbiolo will show all of them.

### Sangiovese: bright, savoury and versatile

Sangiovese commonly combines fresh acidity with noticeable tannin. Sour red cherry is a useful shorthand, alongside red or darker fruit, violet, herbs, earth, pepper and savoury flavours.

Its many local forms and regional expressions mean that Sangiovese is not one uniform taste profile. Some wines are fresh and relatively light on their feet; others are deeper, richer and more firmly structured.

## Which one is fuller-bodied?

It is tempting to say that Nebbiolo is fuller-bodied than Sangiovese, but that is too simple. Nebbiolo can feel powerful because of its tannin and acidity even when its colour is pale. Sangiovese can range from medium-bodied and lively to fuller and more concentrated.

Think of **structure** as well as body. Body describes the weight of the wine in your mouth; acidity and tannin describe much of its shape and grip. A pale Nebbiolo can therefore feel more imposing than its appearance suggests.

You can learn more about these terms in [What are tannins?](/what-are-tannins/), [What is acidity in wine?](/what-is-acidity-in-wine/) and [What is wine body?](/what-is-wine-body/).

## Which one is better with food?

Neither is universally better. The choice depends on the dish, the preparation and the style of wine.

Nebbiolo’s acidity and tannin can work well with rich or protein-rich foods, braised meat, mushrooms, cured meats and firm or creamy cheeses. The wine’s structure can meet the richness of the dish.

Sangiovese’s acidity often suits tomato-based pasta, pizza, poultry, pork, roasted vegetables and savoury herbs. Its freshness can cut through richness and work with dishes that have tomato, salt or herbs.

These are starting points, not rigid rules. Fat, salt, sauce and cooking method may matter more than the grape name alone. [How wine pairing works](/how-wine-pairing-works/) explains the broader principle.

## What will Australian examples be like?

Both varieties are available in Australia, but they remain minor varieties compared with Australia’s best-known grapes.

Wine Australia’s 2023 Sangiovese snapshot recorded 438 hectares planted and 5,127 tonnes crushed. It identified the Murray–Darling–Swan Hill, Riverland, King Valley, Riverina and Barossa Valley among the leading production contexts.

Australian Nebbiolo has a smaller and more exploratory profile. Wine Australia identifies the King Valley as an important context, with further exploration in places including the Mornington Peninsula, Beechworth and the Yarra Valley.

Australian climate, harvest timing, site, canopy management, extraction, vessels and oak can all influence the finished wine. An Australian Nebbiolo may show the variety’s familiar structure and aromatic direction without tasting like a Barolo. An Australian Sangiovese may share freshness and savoury character with Tuscan examples while having its own local expression.

For more detail, see [Nebbiolo in Australia](/nebbiolo-in-australia/) and [Sangiovese in Australia](/sangiovese-in-australia/).

## How to choose between them

Choose Nebbiolo if you are curious about:

- firm, noticeable tannin;
- high acidity and a structured finish;
- relatively pale colour with considerable intensity;
- floral, earthy and savoury aromas;
- a wine that may change significantly with bottle age.

Choose Sangiovese if you are drawn to:

- bright, fresh acidity;
- sour red cherry and red-fruit flavours;
- herbal, earthy and savoury notes;
- a red wine that feels naturally at home with food;
- a broad range of styles, from lighter and lively to richer and more structured.

The best comparison is often side by side. Taste both with the same meal and notice which matters more to you: Nebbiolo’s tannic grip and perfume, or Sangiovese’s cherry freshness and savoury energy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Nebbiolo the same as Sangiovese?

No. They are separate red grape varieties with different typical aromas, textures and regional associations.

### Is Barolo another name for Nebbiolo?

No. Nebbiolo is the grape. Barolo is a regulated wine associated with Nebbiolo and a particular area of Piedmont.

### Is Chianti another name for Sangiovese?

No. Chianti is a Tuscan wine name associated with Sangiovese. Sangiovese is the grape variety.

### Which has more tannin: Nebbiolo or Sangiovese?

Nebbiolo is typically more firmly tannic, especially when young, but Sangiovese can also have substantial tannin. Extraction, ripeness, oak, site and age affect the final texture.

### Can both wines age?

Yes. Both can produce ageworthy wines when fruit, acidity, tannin and winemaking are in balance. Not every bottle is intended for long ageing, however.

### Are Australian Nebbiolo and Sangiovese the same as Italian examples?

No. They are made from the same grape varieties, but Australian conditions and winemaking choices can create different expressions.
