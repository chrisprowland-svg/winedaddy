# Taurasi: an Australian beginner’s guide to the Italian red wine

## Highlights

- Taurasi is both a town in southern Italy and the name of a protected red-wine denomination.
- The denomination is in inland Campania, within Avellino Province and the broader Irpinia area.
- Taurasi DOCG wines must contain at least 85% Aglianico, a black grape variety strongly associated with southern Italy.
- Taurasi is not the same thing as Aglianico: Aglianico is the grape; Taurasi is the regulated place-based denomination.
- The wines are often dry, full-bodied and structured, with noticeable acidity and firm tannin.
- Taurasi can develop from dark-fruit and spice characters towards savoury, earthy and tobacco-like notes with age.
- A standard Taurasi must mature for at least three years, including at least one year in wood.
- “Taurasi DOCG” on a label identifies the denomination, not necessarily wine made only in the town of Taurasi.

## Quick answer: what should you understand about Taurasi?

Taurasi is an Italian DOCG red-wine denomination from inland Campania. It is centred on the Irpinia area of Avellino Province, east or east-south-east of Naples. The legal production area covers the complete administrative territories of 17 municipalities, including Taurasi, Montemarano, Castelfranci, Paternopoli and several neighbouring communities.

The wine is made principally from Aglianico. Current rules require at least 85% Aglianico, allowing up to 15% other authorised grapes. This is why it is useful to remember the simple sequence:

> **Aglianico is the grape. Taurasi is the protected place and denomination. Taurasi wine is the red wine made under that denomination’s rules.**

Taurasi is usually a dry, structured red. It can show dark cherry and other dark-fruit flavours, violet, spice and savoury notes. Its acidity and tannin can be prominent, especially when young. With bottle age, some examples develop more earthy and tertiary notes such as leather, tobacco, truffle or balsamic character.

That description is a regional tendency, not a guarantee. Vineyard site, vintage, vine age, winemaking, maturation and bottle storage all influence the result.

## Where is Taurasi?

Taurasi is in Campania, one of Italy’s regions, in the inland province of Avellino. It sits within the broader historic and wine-geographic area known as Irpinia.

For an Australian traveller, Naples is the most useful major-city reference point. Taurasi lies inland from Naples, generally to the east or east-south-east, among the Apennine hills. Avellino is the nearest useful regional centre. Driving from Naples towards Avellino and the Taurasi area is roughly a one-and-a-half- to two-hour orientation, depending on the exact destination, route and traffic. That is a guide to the geography rather than a fixed travel promise.

The denomination is broader than the town. Its production area covers 17 complete municipalities:

- Taurasi
- Bonito
- Castelfranci
- Castelvetere sul Calore
- Fontanarosa
- Lapio
- Luogosano
- Mirabella Eclano
- Montefalcione
- Montemarano
- Montemiletto
- Paternopoli
- Pietradefusi
- Sant’Angelo all’Esca
- San Mango sul Calore
- Torre le Nocelle
- Venticano

A bottle labelled Taurasi DOCG may therefore come from a qualifying vineyard in any of these municipalities, not only from the town of Taurasi.

## Taurasi, DOCG and Australian GI terminology

DOCG stands for *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*, or “controlled and guaranteed designation of origin”. In practical terms, it identifies an Italian denomination with a defined geographical area and regulated production requirements.

At European level, Taurasi is also treated within the protected-origin framework as a PDO, or DOP in Italian terminology. These systems protect the connection between a product and its defined place.

Australian readers may find this broadly comparable to the idea of a wine Geographical Indication, or GI: a place name can carry recognised geographical meaning and rules. However, DOCG, DOP/PDO and an Australian GI are not exact legal equivalents. The terminology belongs to different regulatory systems.

Taurasi became a DOC denomination in 1970 and was elevated to DOCG status in 1993. On an Australian bottle-shop shelf or restaurant wine list, **Taurasi DOCG** is the clearest label wording to look for.

## Why Aglianico matters

Aglianico is the central grape variety in Taurasi. It is a black grape grown in several parts of southern Italy, so an Aglianico wine is not automatically a Taurasi.

For example, an Aglianico from Basilicata or another Campanian denomination is a different geographical and regulatory product. Likewise, an Australian Aglianico does not have to taste like Taurasi simply because it uses the same grape.

The current Taurasi rules require a minimum of 85% Aglianico. That means Taurasi is not legally required to be 100% Aglianico in every case. Some wines may use only Aglianico, but that is a bottle-specific fact rather than a rule to assume.

The distinction is useful when reading a label:

- **Aglianico** tells you about the grape variety.
- **Taurasi DOCG** tells you about the protected denomination and its production rules.
- **Irpinia** refers to a broader inland area, not automatically to the Taurasi DOCG boundary.

## What is the Taurasi landscape like?

Taurasi’s inland, hilly setting is different from Campania’s coastal areas. The denomination includes varied elevations, local climates and soils. Sources describe clay and calcareous materials alongside volcanic or pyroclastic deposits associated with ancient eruptions.

These conditions help explain why Taurasi can produce wines with freshness and structure, but they do not prescribe one fixed flavour. Soil and climate are part of the story; grape variety, farming, vintage, winemaking and maturation also matter.

It is better to say that Taurasi contains site variation than to claim that every commune or vineyard produces the same style. A wine from one part of the denomination may differ noticeably from another.

## What does Taurasi taste like?

Taurasi is commonly encountered as a dry, full-bodied red with firm structure. Its main structural features are:

- **Acidity:** a fresh, mouth-watering quality that can help the wine feel lively rather than flat.
- **Tannin:** a drying, grippy sensation, often most noticeable in young wines.
- **Body:** a sense of weight and presence on the palate.
- **Finish:** flavours that can remain after swallowing, particularly in more concentrated or mature examples.

Young Taurasi may suggest dark cherry, violet, liquorice, herbs or spice. As the wine develops, it can move towards savoury and earthy characters, including leather, tobacco, truffle and balsamic notes.

These are possibilities, not tasting requirements. Some examples may feel more polished, fragrant or oak-influenced; others may be more savoury and firmly tannic. Vintage, producer choices and bottle age make a difference.

## Ageing and maturation

Standard Taurasi must be aged for at least three years, including at least one year in wood. This is a minimum regulatory requirement before release, not a promise that every bottle will improve indefinitely in a home cellar.

Taurasi Riserva is the longer-aged category. The approved research identifies a four-year minimum, including 18 months in wood; the precise current wording remains subject to final checking against the complete consolidated specification before publication.

Ageing potential is one of Taurasi’s important themes because acidity and tannin can give a wine structural support. But “ageworthy” does not mean “must be stored for decades”. Bottle condition, storage, vintage, producer and personal taste all matter.

## How to read a Taurasi label

When you see Taurasi in Australia, look for the following distinctions:

- **Taurasi DOCG:** the protected denomination.
- **Taurasi Riserva DOCG:** a regulated category with longer minimum maturation.
- **Aglianico:** the principal grape used in the wine, though it may not be the main label name.
- **A municipality or vineyard name:** additional geographic information, but not automatically a separate legal subzone or quality tier.

Taurasi is separate from several nearby or commonly confused names:

- **Irpinia DOC:** a broader denomination associated with Avellino Province.
- **Campi Taurasini:** a separate category within Irpinia DOC.
- **Aglianico del Taburno DOCG:** another Campanian denomination, in Benevento Province.
- **Aglianico del Vulture:** a separate denomination from Basilicata.

These wines may share a grape or broad regional connection, but their names are not interchangeable.

## How to serve Taurasi

A useful starting point is **16–18°C**. Avoid serving a structured red too warm, as heat can make alcohol feel more prominent and tannin seem heavier.

A young, firmly tannic Taurasi may benefit from decanting, although decanting is not compulsory. Older bottles should be handled more carefully: extended exposure to air can sometimes diminish delicate developed aromas rather than improve them.

Taurasi’s structure suits substantial food. Try it with meat ragù, braised or roasted red meat, game, lentils, mushroom-rich dishes or aged hard cheese. The aim is to match the wine’s acidity and tannin with food that has enough flavour and texture to meet it.

## FAQs

### Is Taurasi a grape or a region?

It is neither only one nor the other. Taurasi is the name of a town and a protected wine denomination. The wine is made principally from the Aglianico grape.

### Is every Taurasi 100% Aglianico?

No. The current rule requires at least 85% Aglianico, with up to 15% other authorised grapes permitted.

### Is Taurasi the same as Aglianico del Vulture?

No. Both are southern Italian references associated with Aglianico, but Taurasi is a Campanian denomination and Aglianico del Vulture is from Basilicata.

### Does Taurasi always need ageing?

Not necessarily. It often has the acidity and tannin to develop with age, but the best time to drink a bottle depends on its vintage, producer, storage and style.

### What does Taurasi DOCG mean?

It identifies a regulated Italian denomination of origin. It indicates that the wine comes from the defined Taurasi production area and meets the applicable production rules.
