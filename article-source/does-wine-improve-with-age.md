# Does Wine Improve With Age?

## Highlights

- Some wines improve with age, but wine does not automatically become better because it is older.
- Ageing can add complexity, savoury aromas and a more integrated texture.
- Many fresh, simple and fruit-driven wines are made to drink young.
- Acidity, tannin, sweetness, concentration and balance all influence ageing potential.
- Good storage protects a wine’s potential; it cannot create potential in a wine that was made for early drinking.
- A drinking window is an estimate, not a promise.
- Older wine should be judged by its condition and maturity—not its age, price or prestige alone.

## Quick answer

Some wine improves with age, while other wine is best enjoyed soon after release.

As wine matures, fresh fruit, floral and herbal aromas may become less prominent. In their place, a suitable wine can develop more savoury, earthy, nutty, honeyed, toasted or leathery characters. Tannin may feel more integrated, and the wine may become more complex and harmonious.

But ageing is not an automatic upgrade. Many wines are made to show their freshness and primary fruit when young. If they are kept for too long, they may lose freshness and aroma without gaining enough complexity to compensate.

Whether a bottle is worth keeping depends on the individual wine, its structure, its style, its closure and—critically—how it has been stored. Every wine has a useful maturity window. It may be too young, developing, mature or past its best.

## What happens as wine ages?

Ageing is the gradual chemical and sensory development of wine, particularly after bottling. Maturation is a broader term that can include development in tank, oak, bottle or another vessel.

### Aroma

A young wine may smell of fresh berries, citrus, flowers or herbs. With time, some wines develop what are often called tertiary aromas: dried fruit, earth, leather, tobacco, mushroom, spice, toast, honey or nuts.

These are possibilities, not compulsory stages. A wine does not become mature simply because it smells earthy, and developed aromas are not always desirable if freshness has disappeared.

### Colour

Red wine commonly shifts from purple or bright ruby towards garnet, brick-red and brownish tones. White wine may deepen from pale lemon towards gold or amber.

Colour can offer a useful clue, but it cannot tell you on its own whether a wine is ready or past its best. A brownish colour may reflect maturity, oxidation, the wine’s style or poor storage.

### Tannin and texture

Tannin contributes bitterness, astringency and texture, especially in red wine. During maturation, tannin can become less aggressive or feel more integrated with the wine’s fruit, acidity and alcohol.

Tannin does not simply “melt away”, however. A wine with firm tannin will not necessarily become enjoyable just because it is old. Balance matters more than tannin level alone.

### Freshness and acidity

Acidity contributes freshness and balance. In suitable wines, it can help the wine retain energy as its youthful fruit softens. It is particularly important in many Rieslings, some Chardonnays, Hunter Valley Semillons, sweet wines and sparkling wines.

Acidity alone does not guarantee longevity. The wine also needs enough concentration, balance and sound winemaking to develop well.

## What makes a wine capable of ageing?

There is no single test that predicts how long a wine will develop positively. Ageing potential usually comes from several elements working together.

### Balance

A balanced wine has its fruit, acidity, tannin, alcohol, sweetness and other components working together. High alcohol, high tannin or high acidity by itself is not proof that a wine will age well.

### Acidity

Acidity can help preserve freshness and stop a wine becoming heavy as it develops. This is one reason some high-acid white wines can mature for many years.

### Tannin

Tannin gives many red wines structure. In a well-balanced wine, it may become more integrated over time. Excessive or harsh tannin, without enough fruit and concentration, is not a guarantee of quality or longevity.

### Sweetness and concentration

Residual sugar can help some sweet wines mature, particularly when acidity provides balance. Concentrated flavour also gives a wine more material to develop. Concentration does not necessarily mean heaviness, and sweetness alone does not mean a wine can age indefinitely.

### Winemaking and closure

Oak maturation, oxygen management, sulphur dioxide, filtration and closure choice can all influence how a wine develops. A closure affects oxygen transfer and can contribute to bottle variation, but neither cork nor screw cap is universally superior. The producer’s intention, the closure’s performance and the bottle’s storage history all matter.

## Australian wines that may age well

These are broad categories, not guarantees for every bottle, producer or vintage.

Selected Australian Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon can mature when they have sufficient structure, concentration, acidity and balance. Structured Barossa Shiraz and serious Coonawarra Cabernet Sauvignon are useful examples, but a regional reputation or grape name does not automatically make every bottle suitable for long-term cellaring.

Hunter Valley Semillon is one of Australia’s clearest examples of a white wine with ageing potential. Young examples can be light, citrusy and high in acidity. Suitable wines may later develop toast, honey, fig and savoury complexity while retaining freshness. That does not mean every Semillon—or every Hunter Valley bottle—will follow the same path.

Selected Australian Riesling and Chardonnay may also develop well. Some sweet wines, fortified styles and traditional-method sparkling wines can mature for extended periods through their own distinctive ageing pathways.

At the same time, many rosés, uncomplicated whites, light reds, fresh unoaked wines and simple sparkling wines are designed to be enjoyed while their primary fruit and freshness are prominent.

## Storage matters more than many drinkers realise

Good storage protects a wine’s potential; it does not create ageing potential.

Australian heat makes this especially important. A garage, shed, roof space, sunny room or car can expose wine to damaging heat and temperature fluctuation. Repeated warming and cooling, strong light and poor seals can affect the wine before it reaches maturity.

For detailed advice about stable temperature, darkness, movement and bottle position, see our guide to [how to cellar wine](/how-to-cellar-wine/).

When considering an older bottle, provenance matters. Provenance is the bottle’s known storage and ownership history. Confidence is higher when the wine has been kept in a suitable cellar or wine fridge, rather than in an unknown hot environment.

Also look for:

- leakage or seepage around the closure;
- an unusually low fill level;
- a damaged capsule or cork;
- staining around the neck;
- evidence of heat exposure;
- an unexplained storage history.

These are warning signs, not automatic proof that the wine is faulty. They simply reduce confidence.

## How can you tell whether a bottle is ready?

Start with the bottle’s identity: producer, style, grape variety, region, vintage, closure and any information about sweetness or alcohol. These details provide context, but the label cannot guarantee readiness.

Then consider the wine’s condition and storage history. Five years may be a long time for one fresh rosé but early in the life of a structured red or a suitable Hunter Valley Semillon. Avoid universal ageing timelines.

Finally, taste the wine. A mature bottle may show:

- developed aromas alongside remaining freshness;
- integrated tannin;
- complexity rather than simple fruit intensity;
- harmony between fruit, savoury notes, acidity and alcohol;
- a persistent, balanced finish.

A bottle may be past its best when it tastes flat, hollow or tired, with faded fruit and excessive oxidation. Bruised-apple, cooked or vinegar-like characters may indicate deterioration, while mustiness can suggest cork taint. These signs can have causes other than age, so one clue should not be treated as a complete diagnosis.

An older wine may also contain sediment. Sediment is not automatically a fault, although mature bottles may need careful handling when opened. For serving questions, see our guide to [when wine should be decanted](/when-should-wine-be-decanted/) if that page is available.

## Ageing is not the same as oxidation

Wine ageing involves many interacting chemical changes. Oxygen can play a role in some development, but beneficial maturation is not simply “controlled oxidation”.

Slow, limited oxygen exposure may contribute to complexity. Excessive or rapid oxidation can damage colour, fruit, aroma and texture. Some fortified wines deliberately develop oxidative characters, but that does not mean uncontrolled oxidation is desirable in every wine.

This distinction matters: a wine can be old without being mature, and mature without being oxidised beyond repair.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is older wine always better?

No. Older wine is simply older. It may be more complex, perfectly mature, declining or damaged. Whether it is better depends on the wine and your preference.

### Can white wine improve with age?

Yes. Selected Riesling, Chardonnay, Hunter Valley Semillon, sweet wines and sparkling wines can develop with age. Freshness and acidity are often important, but not every white wine is intended for long-term cellaring.

### How long should I keep a bottle?

There is no universal answer. Producer, style, vintage, structure, closure and storage history all matter. Treat published drinking windows as estimates rather than guarantees.

### Does a screw cap stop wine ageing?

No. Screw caps can provide a consistent low-oxygen environment, and wines under screw cap can develop over time. Closure performance varies, and storage remains important.

### Is expensive wine more likely to improve with age?

Not necessarily. Price and prestige do not guarantee ageing potential. A carefully stored, moderately priced wine may be more enjoyable than an expensive bottle with poor provenance.

### Should I keep wine in a normal cupboard?

A cupboard can be suitable for short-term storage if it is cool, dark and stable. For longer cellaring, avoid areas exposed to Australian heat, sunlight or large temperature changes. See our cellar guide for more detailed storage advice.

## The simplest rule

Keep a bottle because the wine’s style and structure suggest it may develop—and because you are interested in tasting that development. Do not keep it merely because it is old, expensive or famous.

Age can bring complexity, but it cannot guarantee pleasure.
