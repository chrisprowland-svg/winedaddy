# What Is Orange Wine? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Orange wine is generally made from white grapes with meaningful contact between the juice and skins.
- Skin contact can add colour, tannin, bitterness and texture.
- Orange wine is not automatically sweet, cloudy, unfiltered or natural wine.
- Its colour can range from pale gold to amber, copper, orange or brownish shades.
- It is different from rosé, which is usually made from dark-skinned grapes with limited skin contact.
- Australian drinkers should know that Orange is also a protected wine region in New South Wales.
- There is no single orange-wine flavour profile. Each wine depends on its grapes and winemaking choices.

## Quick answer: what is orange wine?

Orange wine is generally a white-grape wine made with extended or meaningful skin contact. Instead of separating the juice from the skins straight away, the winemaker allows them to remain together during fermentation or maceration, a period when the juice extracts compounds from the grape solids.

Seeds and sometimes stems may also be involved. This approach can give the wine a deeper colour, more tannin, a grippier texture, bitterness and savoury character than many conventionally made white wines.

The name is descriptive, not a guarantee of one recipe. Orange wines can be dry or sweet, clear or cloudy, filtered or unfiltered, and conventionally made or low-intervention. The best way to understand one is to consider its production and taste rather than relying on the colour alone.

## How is orange wine made?

The process begins with white grapes. They may be crushed, destemmed, left partly or wholly in bunches, or pressed before fermentation.

For a conventional white wine, the juice is usually separated from the skins early. With orange wine, some or all of the grape solids remain in contact with the juice for longer. Fermentation may occur on the skins, with seeds and sometimes stems present. The wine may then be pressed during fermentation, once fermentation is complete, or after a longer period of maceration.

After pressing, the wine can mature in a tank, barrel, amphora or another vessel. It may be clarified, filtered and protected with sulphur dioxide, or finished with fewer interventions. These choices are separate from the decision to use skin contact.

The length and intensity of contact matter, but they are not a simple quality scale. More skin contact does not automatically mean better wine. Excessive extraction can create harsh tannin, bitterness, oxidation or other technical problems.

## Why is orange wine orange?

White grapes do not usually produce the same red pigment as dark-skinned grapes. Instead, extended contact with skins, seeds and sometimes stems can extract phenolic compounds and other colour material. Oxygen exposure and maturation can also influence the final appearance.

As a result, orange wine may be pale gold, amber, copper, orange, pinkish or brownish. Some wines made with skin contact may look only slightly deeper than a conventional white wine.

This is why colour alone does not define the style. A brightly coloured bottle is not necessarily an orange wine, and an orange wine does not have to be vivid orange.

## What does orange wine taste like?

Orange wine can show a wide range of aromas and flavours, including:

- fresh or dried fruit;
- citrus or stone fruit;
- tea;
- herbs and flowers;
- spice;
- nuttiness;
- savoury or earthy notes.

The texture may be the most noticeable difference for a beginner. Skin, seed and stem contact can give the wine grip or a drying sensation. Some examples feel fuller or more viscous, while others remain light and fresh.

Tannin is often associated with red wine, but white grapes can also contribute phenolic structure when the juice spends time with grape solids. Orange wine may therefore taste slightly bitter, firm or drying. That does not mean every example will be strongly tannic, or that bitterness is automatically a sign of quality.

Grape variety, ripeness, skin-contact time, fermentation temperature, stems, vessel, oxygen exposure, yeast and maturation all influence the result. Think of orange wine as a family of related styles rather than one fixed taste.

## Is orange wine sweet, cloudy or natural?

Not necessarily. These are separate questions.

**Dry or sweet:** Many orange wines are dry, but skin contact does not determine sweetness. Residual sugar depends on fermentation and finishing choices.

**Clear or cloudy:** Cloudiness depends on solids, clarification and filtration. An orange wine can be bright and clear or hazy.

**Filtered or unfiltered:** Filtration is a finishing decision, not a requirement of the style.

**Natural or conventional:** Natural wine describes a broader approach to farming and winemaking. Orange wine describes a style commonly associated with skin contact. The two can overlap, but neither term automatically means the other.

For a useful comparison, see [What Is Natural Wine?](/what-is-natural-wine/) and [Wine colour explained](/wine-colour-explained/).

## Orange wine versus rosé

Orange wine and rosé can look similar in some glasses, but they are usually made differently.

Orange wine is generally made from white grapes with extended skin contact. Rosé is usually made from dark-skinned grapes with limited skin contact or by pressing the grapes quickly to produce a paler wine.

The distinction is about both grape colour and method, not simply the finished shade. A pinkish orange wine is still not automatically rosé, and a pale rosé is not orange wine.

You can explore the difference further in [What Is Rosé?](/what-is-rose/).

## Orange wine and skin-contact white wine

“Skin-contact white wine” is a closely related, broader description. It can include a short period of contact before fermentation, fermentation on skins or a much longer maceration.

“Orange wine” is commonly used for more pronounced skin-contact expressions, particularly wines with amber or copper colour and noticeable phenolic texture. However, terminology varies between producers and markets. When the production details are unclear, “skin-contact white” may be the more precise description.

## What does “orange wine” mean in Australia?

Australian drinkers need to take a little care with the name. **Orange** is also a protected geographical indication in New South Wales. In that context, Orange refers to a place, not a colour or winemaking style.

That means the word “orange” can potentially refer to a wine’s colour, a style of skin-contact white wine or the Orange GI. Label wording needs to be read in context. “Amber wine” or a clear description such as “wine with an orange hue” may avoid confusion in some situations.

Orange, NSW is a separate geographic subject from orange wine. You can read about the place in [Orange wine region: a beginner’s guide to Orange, NSW](/orange-wine-region/).

Australian orange wines are not limited to one national profile. Supported examples of varieties used for amber-style wines include Riesling, Chardonnay, Pinot Gris, Gewürztraminer, Semillon, Sauvignon Blanc, Muscat and Viognier. These are examples, not a formula for Australian orange wine.

## How should you serve orange wine?

Serve it cool rather than ice-cold. Very low temperatures can make texture and aroma harder to appreciate, so let a small pour warm slightly in the glass and see how it changes.

A versatile wine glass is enough. Taste the wine before deciding whether it needs air: some examples may open with time, while others can show oxidation or volatile aromas more clearly after extended aeration.

Approach the first sip expecting texture as well as fruit. Orange wine may feel a little firmer, more savoury or more bitter than the white wines you usually drink. That difference is part of the style, not a reason to assume the wine is faulty.

Most importantly, do not expect every bottle to taste alike. Orange wine is an invitation to explore how grape variety and skin contact can change the shape of white wine.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is orange wine made from oranges?

Usually, no. In wine education, orange wine generally means white-grape wine made with meaningful skin contact. A separate fruit wine made from oranges would need to be described differently.

### Is orange wine healthier?

The research pack does not support health claims about orange wine. It is best understood as a wine style, not a health category.

### Is orange wine always funky?

No. Some examples may be savoury, earthy or unusual, but others can be clean, aromatic, fresh or structured. The style does not guarantee one aroma profile.

### Is amber wine the same as orange wine?

The terms are closely related, but usage varies. “Amber” can be a useful Australian description because it reduces confusion with Orange, NSW.
