# What Is Saignée Method? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- **Saignée** (pronounced roughly “sen-yay”) means “bleeding” in French.
- It is a red-winemaking technique where some juice is drained from crushed red grapes or an early red ferment.
- The remaining red wine has a higher proportion of skins to liquid.
- The drained juice may be fermented separately, often to make rosé.
- Saignée rosé is not the same as direct-press rosé.
- Saignée can increase colour or extraction, but it does not automatically make a better or more concentrated red wine.
- Australian wine learners may see both **saignée** and **saignee** in technical notes and producer descriptions.

## Quick Answer: What is saignée?

Saignée is a winemaking method in which a portion of juice is “bled” or drained from a vessel containing crushed red grapes or an early red ferment. The retained red ferment then continues with more grape skins in proportion to the remaining liquid.

The drained juice can be fermented separately, often as rosé. This gives the method two possible purposes: it can alter the balance of a red ferment, and it can create a separate rosé wine.

The result depends on timing, grape variety, fruit condition, the amount of juice removed and how both portions are fermented. Saignée may lead to more colour, tannin, phenolic extraction or body in the retained red wine, but those are possible outcomes—not guarantees of quality.

## How does the saignée method work?

The basic sequence is:

1. **Red grapes enter a red-wine vessel.** They may be crushed before being placed in the tank.
2. **Juice remains with the skins.** This early maceration or fermentation allows colour, tannin and other compounds to move from the grape solids into the liquid.
3. **Some juice is drained.** This is the “bleeding” step.
4. **The red ferment continues.** Because some liquid has been removed, the remaining wine contains a greater proportion of skins relative to juice.
5. **The drained juice may ferment separately.** If it is intended for rosé, it is managed as its own wine.
6. **Both fractions are finished separately.** Fermentation, clarification, maturation and blending decisions depend on the intended style.

Saignée is not one fixed recipe. The winemaker may use it mainly to manage a red ferment, mainly to produce rosé, or for both reasons.

## When is the juice drained?

Saignée usually takes place after crushing, during early maceration or early fermentation. The exact timing varies.

The Australian Wine Research Institute notes that draining is often easiest once fermentation has begun and the cap—the layer of grape skins and solids that rises above the liquid—has formed. However, Australian trade reporting also documents juice being drained shortly after crushing, before fermentation has properly begun.

Timing creates an important trade-off:

- **Earlier drainage** removes relatively pale juice and creates a larger proportional reduction in liquid. The resulting rosé may have limited colour.
- **Later drainage** allows more colour and phenolic material to enter the juice, potentially producing a more coloured or structured rosé. However, some extracted material leaves with the drained juice, which can reduce the concentration effect in the retained red wine.

This is why it is misleading to say that saignée always produces a darker rosé or a richer red.

## What can saignée do to red wine?

Removing juice increases the ratio of grape skins to liquid in the remaining ferment. In some situations, this may contribute to:

- deeper colour;
- more phenolic extraction;
- firmer tannin;
- fuller body or denser texture;
- a different expression of fruit.

But more extraction is not automatically better. A red wine can become too tannic, too concentrated or out of balance. The final result also depends on the grape variety, ripeness, fruit condition, fermentation temperature, cap management, timing and volume removed.

The AWRI reports that saignée may remove around 10–20 per cent of the juice in some practices, but this is a reported range rather than a universal instruction. Removing more juice is not automatically more beneficial, because the retained red wine also loses volume.

The practical question is therefore not “Does saignée make better red wine?” It is “Does this particular amount of drainage improve the balance and style the winemaker is trying to achieve?”

## What is saignée rosé?

Saignée rosé is rosé made from juice drained from a red-wine lot. Because the juice may have had some contact with skins before it was removed, its colour and texture can vary according to when the drainage occurred.

Some saignée rosés may show more colour or phenolic structure than a very lightly handled rosé. Others may be pale and fresh, particularly when juice is removed early. There is no single saignée rosé profile.

Rosé itself does not tell you how the wine was made. Rosé can also be produced by direct pressing, short skin contact and other methods. A producer’s technical note or description is needed to establish whether a particular wine used saignée.

Saignée is also not the same as blending red and white wine. It begins with juice removed from a red-grape ferment.

## Saignée versus direct-press rosé

The simplest distinction is the starting point:

| Saignée rosé | Direct-press rosé |
|---|---|
| Begins as part of a red-wine maceration or ferment | Begins with grapes pressed specifically to make rosé |
| Some juice is drained from the red-wine vessel | Juice is obtained through pressing |
| A retained red-wine lot remains | No retained red ferment is created by the rosé process itself |
| Skin contact may happen before drainage | Pressing is generally used to control and limit skin contact |
| Colour and structure can vary with drainage timing | The style is designed from the outset as rosé |

Neither method is automatically superior. They simply give the winemaker different ways to manage juice, skins, colour and texture.

## What does saignée mean for Australian wine drinkers?

Saignée is a documented Australian winemaking practice. The Australian Wine Research Institute provides technical guidance on the method, and Australian trade reporting has described trials and experiences from producers in several states.

Those examples show why broad claims should be avoided. Some Australian winemakers have used saignée to pursue a richer red and a separate rosé, while other trials produced excessive tannin, concentration or a rosé style that did not suit the intended market. These are individual experiences, not evidence that saignée is nationally dominant or especially superior in one region.

When reading Australian wine descriptions, you may encounter either **saignée** or the unaccented spelling **saignee**. A bottle labelled simply “rosé” does not normally establish which production method was used.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is saignée only used to make rosé?

No. The drained juice is often fermented as rosé, but it may be used for another wine depending on the winery’s plans. Saignée can also be used primarily to alter a red ferment.

### Does saignée always make red wine more concentrated?

No. It increases the proportion of skins to liquid, which can support greater extraction. But timing, the amount removed and what happens to the extracted material all affect the result.

### Is saignée rosé always darker than direct-press rosé?

No. Saignée rosé can be more coloured or structured, but early drainage and other winemaking choices can produce a pale wine.

### Is saignée a grape variety or wine region?

No. It is a winemaking technique, not a grape variety, protected wine region or appellation.

### What is the main idea to remember?

Saignée means draining some juice from a red-wine ferment. The removed juice may become rosé, while the retained red wine continues with a higher skins-to-liquid ratio. The method can change a wine, but it cannot guarantee a particular style or quality.
