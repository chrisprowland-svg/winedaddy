# Navarra wine region: a beginner’s guide

Navarra is a wine region and protected designation in northern Spain. Its wines include red, rosé, white and sweet or liqueur styles, with Garnacha and Tempranillo especially useful varieties to know.

The key point is that Navarra is not simply “another name for Rioja”. The two are separate Spanish wine designations, although parts of Navarra overlap with the DOCa Rioja production area.

## Highlights

- **Country:** Spain  
- **Autonomous community:** Comunidad Foral de Navarra  
- **Protected wine designation:** DOP Navarra, also called DO Navarra or Denominación de Origen Navarra  
- **Useful regional centre:** Pamplona, the capital of Navarra  
- **Orientation from Madrid:** roughly north-east; about 390–400 kilometres by road, commonly around four hours by car, depending on the route  
- **Familiar localities:** Olite, Estella-Lizarra, Tafalla, Tudela, Sangüesa, Puente la Reina and Viana  
- **Main learner-facing grapes:** Garnacha, known as Grenache in Australia, and Tempranillo  
- **Main styles:** red, rosé, white and liqueur wines  

These towns are useful points of reference, but they are not interchangeable names for the whole DOP. The legally protected production area covers specified municipalities grouped into five smaller geographic units.

## What kind of wine region is Navarra?

Navarra is a geographically varied part of northern Spain. Its landscape and climate change noticeably from north to south: areas with stronger Atlantic influence give way to warmer, drier Mediterranean-influenced country around the Ebro Valley.

The DOP Navarra is divided into five internal geographic units:

- **Baja Montaña**
- **Valdizarbe**
- **Tierra Estella**
- **Ribera Alta**
- **Ribera Baja**

These are smaller geographic units within the designation, not five separate appellations. They help explain why Navarra does not have one single wine style.

The northern and central areas tend to have more Atlantic influence, while the southern areas are generally warmer and drier. The official specification also describes high sunshine, warm summers and autumns, calcareous and permeable soils, undulating terrain and the dry north-westerly **cierzo** wind.

For a beginner, think of these factors as influences rather than guarantees. A warmer part of Navarra may support fuller, riper wines, while a cooler area may support fresher acidity and more aromatic lift. Grape variety, harvest timing and winemaking still matter greatly.

## What does DOP Navarra mean?

**DOP** means *Denominación de Origen Protegida*, or Protected Designation of Origin. You may also see the traditional term **DO Navarra**.

For an Australian wine drinker, the broad idea is familiar: like an Australian Geographical Indication, a Spanish DOP connects a wine with a regulated place of origin. But the systems are not legally identical. Spanish and European Union rules define the designation’s eligible area, permitted grapes, production requirements and controls.

A bottle labelled **Navarra** therefore signals protected geographic origin. It does not automatically tell you:

- whether the wine is red, rosé, white or sweet;
- which grape or blend was used;
- whether it has spent time in oak;
- which of the five geographic units it comes from;
- how it will taste.

Australian importers and retailers may present Spanish designation terms and other label information in different ways, so not every bottle will look the same.

For a general guide to Spanish designation language, see [Spanish DO and DOCa](/spanish-do-and-doca/). You can also read about [Spain](/spain/) in its broader wine-geographic context.

## Which grapes should you know?

### Garnacha and Tempranillo

Start with **Garnacha**, called **Grenache** in Australia, and **Tempranillo**.

Garnacha has a long association with Navarra and is particularly important in rosé and red wines. Tempranillo is another major Spanish red variety and an important part of Navarra’s modern identity. Our guides to [Grenache](/what-is-grenache/) and [Tempranillo](/what-is-tempranillo/) explain the grapes in more detail.

Navarra also permits varieties such as Graciano, Mazuelo, Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon, Syrah and Pinot Noir for red wines. White varieties include Chardonnay, Viura, Sauvignon Blanc, Garnacha Blanca, Malvasía, Moscatel de Grano Menudo and Oneca.

That list tells you something useful: Navarra is not limited to one traditional grape. Its wines can draw on both Spanish varieties and international varieties.

## What wines is Navarra known for?

### Rosé

Navarra is especially associated with **rosado**, the Spanish term for rosé. Traditional Navarra rosé is strongly linked with Garnacha and may be made using **sangrado**, or bleeding. In this method, some free-run juice is drawn from red grapes before fermentation continues.

This helps explain Navarra’s reputation for vivid, fruity rosé. But “Navarra” does not mean “rosé”: the designation also covers red, white and liqueur wines.

### Red wine

Navarra reds may be based on Garnacha, Tempranillo or blends that include varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot or Syrah.

Broadly, wines from warmer southern areas may show more body, ripeness and alcohol, while wines from cooler or more Atlantic-influenced areas may feel fresher and more aromatic. Some reds are intended for earlier drinking, while others are made with ageing in mind. The regional name alone does not guarantee oak ageing or a particular level of richness.

### White wine

Navarra’s white wines can be made from Chardonnay, Viura, Sauvignon Blanc, Garnacha Blanca, Malvasía, Moscatel de Grano Menudo and Oneca.

This means a Navarra white may be fresh and aromatic, or it may have a broader style depending on the grape and winemaking choices. It is better to use the variety and producer information on the label than to assume one standard Navarra white style.

### Sweet and liqueur wines

The designation also recognises liqueur wines, including sweet wines associated with Moscatel de Grano Menudo and Garnacha. These are a smaller part of the beginner picture, but they reinforce the point that Navarra is stylistically broader than its rosé reputation suggests.

## Is Navarra part of Rioja?

No. **Navarra and Rioja are separate protected wine designations.**

The complication is geographical: some municipalities in Navarra are included in the Rioja Oriental area of DOCa Rioja. These include Viana, Aras, Bargota, Mendavia, Sartaguda, Andosilla, San Adrián and Azagra.

So a wine made in Navarra is not automatically a Navarra wine in the legal labelling sense, and it is not automatically Rioja either. The designation on the bottle depends on the applicable rules, vineyard and winery registrations, and how the wine is presented within the relevant system.

For Australian learners, the simplest rule is: treat **Navarra** and **Rioja** as distinct regional names, even where their mapped areas overlap.

## How to approach a Navarra wine

When choosing or discussing a Navarra wine, start with three questions:

1. **What colour or style is it?** Red, rosé, white or sweet?
2. **Which grape is listed?** Garnacha and Tempranillo are useful starting points.
3. **Does the label say Navarra?** That indicates protected origin, not a guaranteed flavour profile.

Navarra is best understood as a varied northern Spanish wine region with a strong Garnacha and rosé tradition, but with much more to explore. It is a regional identity, not a synonym for one grape, one colour or Rioja.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Navarra a wine region or a grape variety?

Navarra is a place and a protected wine designation. It is not a grape variety.

### Is Garnacha the same as Grenache?

Yes. Garnacha is the Spanish name commonly translated as Grenache in Australia.

### Is Navarra wine always rosé?

No. Navarra produces red, rosé, white and liqueur wines.

### Is Navarra wine the same as Rioja?

No. They are separate protected designations, although some municipalities in Navarra also fall within the DOCa Rioja area.

### What does Navarra on a wine label mean?

It indicates protected geographic origin under the DOP Navarra framework. It does not, by itself, guarantee the grape, colour, sweetness, ageing method or flavour.
