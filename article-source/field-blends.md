# Field blends: what Australian wine drinkers should know

## Highlights

- A field blend generally comes from a vineyard block where two or more grape varieties are planted together.
- Traditionally, the mixed grapes are harvested together and often fermented together.
- The term describes vineyard composition and production practice—not a guaranteed wine style or quality level.
- A field blend is different from a winery blend, where separate wines or parcels are combined after some part of production.
- Field blends are not automatically red, old-vine, natural, organic, complex or superior.
- In Australia, “field blend” is best understood as descriptive language. Read the rest of the label for regulated claims about variety, vintage or Geographic Indication.

## Quick answer: what is a field blend?

A field blend is generally a wine made from fruit grown in a vineyard block where different grape varieties are planted together. In the traditional approach, the grapes are harvested together and often fermented together, rather than being kept separate and blended later in the winery.

That core idea is important: **the blend begins in the vineyard**.

However, “field blend” does not have one universal definition that fixes every step of production. Some contemporary wines use the term for fruit from a mixed planting even when varieties are harvested or fermented separately. If you are curious about a particular bottle, check the producer’s description or ask whether the grapes were planted, harvested and fermented together.

The phrase alone also does not tell you whether a wine is red or white, dry or sweet, light- or full-bodied, old-vine, natural, organic or high quality.

## How a field blend is made

Imagine a vineyard row containing several grape varieties rather than one variety planted in a separate block. The vines may have been planted together historically, inherited as an existing mixed block or chosen because the varieties work well—or perform differently—on the site.

At harvest, the grower or winemaker has a decision to make. The whole block may be picked together, bringing the varieties into the winery in one lot. The fruit can then be crushed and fermented together. This is the traditional field-blend idea.

But grape varieties do not necessarily ripen at the same rate. Picking the block on one date may mean some fruit is less ripe while other fruit is more advanced. The harvest decision is made for the combined lot rather than for each variety at its individual ideal point.

A winemaker may instead pick varieties selectively or handle them separately, then combine the resulting wines later. Some producers still call the finished wine a field blend because it comes from a mixed planting; others use the term more narrowly for shared harvesting and co-fermentation.

## Field blend versus winery blend

The simplest distinction is:

- **Field blend:** the mixture starts with different varieties planted together in the vineyard.
- **Winery blend:** separate fruit, musts or wines are combined during or after production.

A winery blend does not require the varieties to come from the same vineyard. They may come from separate blocks, vineyards or other sources, subject to the claims made on the label.

A wine can also involve both approaches. Fruit from a mixed block might be fermented together and then adjusted with separately made wine.

## Field blend versus co-fermentation

Co-fermentation describes a winemaking process: two or more varieties ferment together in the same vessel.

That can happen with fruit from a genuine mixed planting, but it can also happen when separately planted varieties are harvested and combined before fermentation. So co-fermentation and field blending overlap, but they are not synonyms.

A traditional field blend is often co-fermented. A co-fermented wine is not necessarily a field blend.

## How it differs from other familiar terms

### Single-varietal wine

A single-varietal wine is identified principally by one grape variety. It answers a question about the wine’s grape composition, while “field blend” answers a question about how varieties were planted and possibly processed.

In Australia, a variety claim generally requires at least 85 per cent of the claimed variety under Wine Australia’s blending guidance. That rule concerns the variety named on the label; it does not create a fixed percentage rule for the phrase “field blend”.

### Single-vineyard wine

“Single vineyard” describes the source of the fruit. It does not tell you whether that vineyard contains one variety or several, or whether the grapes were harvested together.

A field blend can also be a single-vineyard wine, but the terms describe different things.

### Estate-grown wine

“Estate-grown” describes a relationship between the vineyard and the wine business. It does not establish mixed planting, shared harvesting or co-fermentation.

A field blend may be estate-grown, but neither term guarantees the other.

## Why make a field blend?

Mixed plantings may survive because they are inherited from an older vineyard, reflect historical planting practices or preserve a block that would otherwise be replanted. Growers may also value varieties that perform differently in a site or contribute different elements to a final wine.

There are trade-offs. Mixed varieties can make vineyard management, harvest timing and varietal identification more complicated. Inter-planted varieties combined with non-selective harvesting can also result in unintended mixing, affecting the wine’s aroma, flavour or colour and potentially creating label-integrity issues.

These are possibilities, not promises. The final wine is also shaped by the site, season, fruit condition, planting proportions, sorting, harvest decisions and winemaking.

## What does “field blend” mean on an Australian label?

In the Wine Australia guidance reviewed for this article, “field blend” is not identified as a defined Australian Geographic Indication, variety category or quality category. It is therefore best treated as descriptive language rather than a legally fixed guarantee.

That does not make the phrase meaningless. It can be a useful clue that the wine is connected to a mixed vineyard planting. But the phrase alone does not establish:

- how many varieties are included;
- the percentage of each variety;
- whether they were picked together;
- whether they were fermented together;
- the age of the vines;
- the wine’s quality or style.

Look separately at any variety, vintage or Geographic Indication claim on the label. Those claims have their own requirements. If the production story matters to you, check whether the producer explains the planting, harvest and fermentation method.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are field blends always red?

No. “Field blend” concerns vineyard composition and production, not wine colour.

### Are all varieties in a field blend harvested together?

No. Shared harvesting is traditional and common in the narrower meaning of the term, but contemporary usage can include mixed plantings whose varieties are harvested or vinified separately.

### Does a field blend guarantee quality?

No. A field blend is not automatically more complex, authentic, natural, age-worthy or superior. Its quality and character depend on the vineyard, vintage, fruit and winemaking.
