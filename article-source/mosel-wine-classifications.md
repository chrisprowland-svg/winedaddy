# Mosel wine classifications: how to read German wine labels

## Highlights

- **Mosel** tells you where the wine comes from.
- **Qualitätswein** and **Prädikatswein** are regulated German wine categories.
- **Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Eiswein** and **Trockenbeerenauslese** describe grape ripeness and particular harvest or selection conditions.
- A Prädikat is **not a simple quality ranking**.
- A Prädikat does not automatically tell you whether the finished wine is dry or sweet.
- **Trocken** and **halbtrocken** describe the finished wine’s taste and should be read separately.
- For an Australian buyer, the safest approach is to read the whole label rather than rely on one German term.

## Quick answer

Mosel is a German wine-growing region. The classification terms found on Mosel labels come from Germany’s statutory wine system, which regulates categories such as **Qualitätswein** and **Prädikatswein**.

The six Prädikat terms—**Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese, Beerenauslese, Eiswein** and **Trockenbeerenauslese**—principally communicate information about grape ripeness, must weight and, for some categories, harvesting or selection conditions. They are not a direct code for sweetness and are not a universal “bad to good” ladder.

To understand the finished wine, look for a separate taste indication. **Trocken** means dry, while **halbtrocken** indicates an off-dry or medium-dry style. Terms such as **feinherb**, **lieblich** and **süß** may also appear. The producer, vintage, alcohol and any available residual-sugar information add further context.

A useful shortcut is:

> **Mosel = place; Prädikat = grape ripeness or harvest category; trocken or halbtrocken = finished-wine taste.**

## Mosel is the place, not the classification

Mosel is one of Germany’s defined wine-growing regions. It may appear on a bottle alongside a grape variety such as Riesling and a classification term such as Kabinett or Spätlese.

That means the label is communicating several different things at once:

- **Geographic origin:** Mosel.
- **Grape variety:** for example, Riesling, where stated.
- **Statutory category:** Qualitätswein or Prädikatswein.
- **Prädikat:** such as Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese.
- **Finished-wine taste:** such as trocken or halbtrocken.

This is different from describing Mosel as though it had a separate classification system from the rest of Germany. The terminology is German statutory wine terminology, understood here through Mosel wines.

For broader regional context, see [Mosel](/mosel/). For the wider system, see [the German Prädikat system](/german-pradikat-system/).

## Qualitätswein and Prädikatswein

**Qualitätswein** is a regulated quality-wine category from one of Germany’s defined wine-growing regions. It must meet regulatory requirements and pass the relevant quality-wine examination.

**Prädikatswein** is a quality wine with an officially recognised predicate. German legislation links the use of the Prädikat terms to official examination and an official examination number. Prädikatswein may not be enriched before fermentation, whereas Qualitätswein may be enriched within legal limits.

For a beginner, the important point is that Qualitätswein is not a synonym for poor wine, and Prädikatswein is not a guarantee that every bottle will be superior. These are legal categories that provide information about production and grape ripeness—not a complete judgement of taste or value.

## What are must weight and Oechsle?

The Prädikat system is principally based on the ripeness of the grapes, measured through the weight or density of the grape juice before fermentation. This is called **must weight** and is commonly expressed in degrees **Oechsle**.

Must weight is not the same as residual sugar. During fermentation, yeast converts grape sugar into alcohol. A winemaker may also leave some sugar in the finished wine. As a result, a wine made from grapes with higher must weight can still be dry, off-dry or sweet, depending on how it is made and labelled.

Minimum must-weight requirements vary according to factors including grape variety and wine-growing region. It is therefore safer to understand the principle than to memorise one universal table of numbers.

## The six Prädikat terms

The traditional sequence runs from Kabinett through to Trockenbeerenauslese. It is best understood as an ascending sequence of grape ripeness and special harvest conditions—not as a universal quality score.

### Kabinett

**Kabinett** is made from ripe grapes meeting the requirements for that predicate, without enrichment. It is often associated with fine, lighter wines and relatively moderate alcohol, but those are typical descriptions rather than guarantees.

Kabinett does **not** automatically mean dry.

### Spätlese

**Spätlese** is associated with a later harvest of fully ripe grapes. The name is often translated as “late harvest”.

A Spätlese can be dry, off-dry or sweet. The term describes the ripeness and harvest category; it does not, by itself, state how much residual sugar remains in the bottle.

### Auslese

**Auslese** is made from selected, fully ripe grapes, with less-ripe berries removed. Selection is central to the term.

Auslese may be made in different finished styles. It should not automatically be treated as dessert wine or assumed to be sweet.

### Beerenauslese

**Beerenauslese** is made from selected overripe berries. It is commonly associated with noble rot, also called **botrytis** or *Edelfäule*, when suitable conditions allow the fungus to shrivel and concentrate the grapes.

That association should not be turned into an absolute claim that every Beerenauslese must be botrytised in every circumstance. The key idea is selected, overripe berries and concentration.

### Eiswein

**Eiswein** is made from grapes harvested and pressed while frozen. The Deutsches Weininstitut specifies harvesting and pressing below minus 7°C. Freezing concentrates the grape juice before fermentation.

Eiswein is therefore not simply another name for late-harvest sweet wine. The frozen-harvest condition is essential to the term. Its predicate requirements include a minimum must weight corresponding to Beerenauslese.

### Trockenbeerenauslese

**Trockenbeerenauslese** is made from shrivelled, raisin-like berries, traditionally associated with noble rot and high concentration.

It is traditionally placed at the top of the Prädikat sequence. That does not mean it will be the best choice for every drinker, meal or occasion. “Highest” in this context refers to the traditional predicate sequence, not a universal guarantee of quality or preference.

## Prädikat is not the same as sweetness

This is the most useful distinction to remember:

> **Prädikat describes the grapes and harvest classification. Taste terms describe the finished wine.**

A higher must weight gives the winemaker more grape sugar and concentration to work with. Fermentation can convert much of that sugar into alcohol, or some sugar may remain. Acidity can also affect how sweet a wine feels.

That is why these assumptions are unreliable:

- Kabinett always means dry.
- Spätlese always means sweet.
- Auslese is always dessert wine.
- A higher Prädikat is always better.

Instead, look for the separate taste indication and consider the rest of the label.

## Trocken, halbtrocken and related terms

**Trocken** is the German dry taste category. It generally means up to 4 grams per litre of residual sugar, with a permitted limit up to 9 grams per litre where the acidity relationship allows it.

**Halbtrocken** is an off-dry or medium-dry category. It may reach 12 grams per litre, or 18 grams per litre under the relevant acidity relationship.

Other terms may appear:

- **Feinherb:** widely used, but not legally defined as an exact residual-sugar category.
- **Lieblich:** sweeter than halbtrocken within the relevant legal range.
- **Süß:** the sweet category above the relevant threshold.

These terms concern the finished wine. They should not be confused with Kabinett, Spätlese or Auslese.

## How to read a Mosel label in Australia

When you encounter an imported German wine in an Australian bottle shop, restaurant or specialist retailer, read the label in this order:

1. **Find the origin:** Mosel tells you the geographic source.
2. **Find the grape:** Riesling may be stated on the label.
3. **Identify the legal category:** Qualitätswein or Prädikatswein.
4. **Read the Prädikat:** Kabinett, Spätlese, Auslese or another term tells you about ripeness or harvest conditions.
5. **Look for the taste term:** trocken, halbtrocken, feinherb, lieblich or süß provides information about the finished style.
6. **Check the remaining details:** alcohol, vintage, producer and any residual-sugar information can help you form a fuller picture.

Australian wine labels generally do not use the German Prädikat system in the same way. Do not treat Kabinett or Spätlese as equivalents of Australian geographical indications, grades or sweetness labels. The systems have different purposes and terminology.

[How to read a wine label](/how-to-read-a-wine-label/) provides a broader label-reading approach.

## What about VDP terms?

You may also encounter VDP terms such as **VDP.GUTSWEIN**, **VDP.ORTSWEIN**, **VDP.ERSTE LAGE** and **VDP.GROSSE LAGE**.

These belong to the private classification rules of the Verband Deutscher Prädikatsweingüter. They are not the same as the statutory German Prädikat terms, and VDP classification is not German wine law.

For this reason, a VDP term and a term such as Spätlese should not be treated as interchangeable labels.

## FAQs

### Does Spätlese mean sweet?

No. Spätlese describes a later harvest of fully ripe grapes. The finished wine may be dry, off-dry or sweet, so check the separate taste indication.

### Is Kabinett always dry?

No. Kabinett does not determine the finished wine’s residual sugar or taste category.

### Is Auslese dessert wine?

Not necessarily. Auslese describes selected, fully ripe grapes. Read the taste term and the rest of the label.

### Is Eiswein made from grapes frozen in a freezer?

The relevant condition is that the grapes are harvested and pressed while naturally frozen, below the specified temperature. Eiswein is not simply any sweet wine made by artificial freezing.

### Are German Prädikate directly comparable with Australian wine categories?

No. They belong to different regulatory and labelling systems. Use the German terms as information about the imported wine itself, rather than trying to convert them into an Australian grade.

## The takeaway

For Mosel wines, keep three ideas separate: **place, grape ripeness and finished-wine taste**. Mosel identifies the origin. A Prädikat such as Spätlese or Auslese describes a regulated ripeness or harvest category. Trocken or halbtrocken helps describe the wine in the glass.

Once you read those terms as separate pieces of information, German wine labels become much less mysterious.
