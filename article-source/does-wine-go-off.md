# Does wine go off?

Yes. Wine can go “off”, but that usually means its quality has deteriorated rather than that it has reached a universal safety expiry date.

It may lose its freshness, become flat, develop oxidation-related flavours, smell vinegary or simply no longer taste right for its style. An opened bottle generally changes faster than an unopened one because more oxygen can reach the wine.

The practical rule is simple: if wine smells or tastes unpleasant, it is no longer worth drinking. If there is mould, contamination, damaged packaging or any real doubt about how it was stored, discard it without tasting.

## Highlights

- Wine can deteriorate whether it is opened or unopened.
- Oxygen, heat, light and temperature changes can all affect wine quality.
- Opened wine usually changes faster because it is exposed to air.
- Refrigerating and resealing wine slows deterioration; it does not reverse it.
- There is no reliable number of days that applies to every wine.
- Sparkling wine also loses its fizz after opening.
- An old bottle is not automatically unsafe, and a normal smell is not proof that questionable wine is safe.

## What does “off” mean?

“Off” is an everyday term, not one precise technical diagnosis. It can describe several different things.

### Normal ageing

Some wines develop with time, gaining savoury, earthy or nutty characters. That change may be desirable, neutral or disappointing depending on the wine and your taste.

Age alone does not prove that a wine is spoiled.

### Oxidation and loss of freshness

Oxygen exposure can gradually dull a wine’s aromas and fruit. An oxidised wine may show bruised-apple, nutty, sherry-like or madeirised characters. It may also smell like cardboard, straw or hay.

Oxidation is a quality problem, but it is not automatically the same thing as dangerous spoilage.

### Spoilage or contamination

A wine may also smell mouldy, musty, vinegary or solvent-like, or show signs that the bottle or its contents have been contaminated. These are reasons to stop and discard it, particularly if the wine has been handled or stored questionably.

## Opened wine: what changes?

Once you open a bottle, oxygen reaches the wine. The rate of change depends on how much remains, how much air is above it, whether it is resealed, the temperature, the wine’s style and the wine’s condition before opening.

A mostly empty bottle contains more air relative to the amount of wine, so it may fade more quickly than a nearly full bottle. Moving a small remaining amount into a clean, smaller, tightly sealed container can reduce that headspace.

There is no universal “three-day rule”. A delicate wine may lose its appeal quickly, while another may remain enjoyable for longer. Treat any time estimate as a guide to quality, not a safety guarantee.

### Still red wine

Red wine can also be refrigerated after opening if your aim is to slow its deterioration. Let it move back towards serving temperature before drinking. Some reds may hold their character reasonably well, but the result depends on the individual wine and its storage history.

### White and rosé

Fresh whites and rosés can show fading aroma and brightness quite clearly. Reseal them promptly and keep them cold.

### Sparkling wine

Sparkling wine has an additional problem: it loses dissolved carbon dioxide, so it can become flat even when other aromas have not changed dramatically.

For Champagne, Comité Champagne advises that an opened bottle may be kept for one to two days when it is immediately sealed with a suitable airtight sparkling-wine stopper and stored somewhere cool. This is specific guidance for Champagne, not a universal deadline for every sparkling wine.

A spoon in the neck of the bottle is not a reliable substitute for a proper sparkling-wine stopper.

### Fortified wine

Fortified wines vary widely in alcohol, sweetness, style, closure and intended ageing. Some may remain enjoyable longer after opening than ordinary table wine, but there is no single timeframe that fits them all. Follow producer guidance where it is available.

## Unopened wine can deteriorate too

An unopened bottle is protected from direct air exposure, but it is not permanently stable. Poor storage, closure performance, heat, light and repeated temperature swings can all affect its quality.

In an Australian home, avoid leaving wine for long periods in a hot car, shed, garage, sunny room or other place that becomes very warm. The risk varies between homes and seasons, so there is no single temperature threshold that tells you whether every bottle is drinkable.

A bottle that has leaked, has a pushed cork, shows seepage or has other signs of heat or closure damage deserves caution. These signs do not provide a complete diagnosis, but they suggest the wine may not be in good condition.

## How to store wine sensibly

### After opening

1. Reseal the bottle promptly.
2. Refrigerate it where practical, including red wine if slowing change is the priority.
3. Use a purpose-built stopper for sparkling wine.
4. Transfer a small remaining amount to a clean, smaller, tightly sealed container if suitable.
5. Keep the wine away from heat and sunlight.
6. Remember that storage slows deterioration; it cannot restore freshness once the wine has changed.

### Before opening

Keep unopened wine in a cool, dark and reasonably stable place. Avoid prolonged heat, direct sunlight and repeated large temperature fluctuations.

## Should I drink this wine?

Use this quick check:

1. **Was it opened?** If yes, expect faster change.
2. **Was it promptly resealed and kept cool?** If yes, it may still be enjoyable, depending on the style and storage history.
3. **Is there mould, contamination, abnormal packaging or questionable handling?** Discard it without tasting.
4. **Does it smell clearly stale, vinegary, solvent-like, mouldy or otherwise wrong?** Discard it.
5. **Is the only concern that it is old or looks darker?** Age or colour change alone does not prove that it is unsafe, but neither is a complete safety test.
6. **Are you uncertain about its safety?** Do not taste-test it. Discard it.

An unpleasant bottle may be a quality disappointment rather than a danger. But when contamination or unsafe storage is a possibility, the sensible choice is not to investigate with your palate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does wine expire?

Wine does not have one universal expiry point. Its quality depends on the style, age, closure and storage conditions. Opened wine generally changes faster than unopened wine.

### Can I drink old unopened wine?

Possibly, but old does not automatically mean better or unsafe. Storage history and the wine’s condition matter. Check for leakage, closure damage, unusual smells and other signs of deterioration. If safety is uncertain, discard it without tasting.

### Does putting wine in the fridge stop it going off?

No. Refrigeration slows many quality changes but does not stop them or reverse oxidation.

### Can wine that has gone off be used for cooking?

Only consider this when the wine has a quality problem and has been stored safely. Do not use wine with mould, contamination or questionable handling as a way to rescue it.
