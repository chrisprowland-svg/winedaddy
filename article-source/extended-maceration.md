# What Is Extended Maceration? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Extended maceration means leaving red wine in contact with grape skins, seeds and sometimes stems for longer than usual.
- It most commonly continues after alcoholic fermentation and before the wine is pressed.
- There is no universal number of days. “Extended” describes duration, not a guaranteed result.
- The process can influence tannin, colour, texture and structure.
- Longer contact does not automatically mean darker, fuller, better or more age-worthy wine.
- Extended maceration is different from cold soak, carbonic maceration, whole-bunch fermentation and oak ageing.
- Australian producers may use the technique, but it is not an Australian legal wine category or guaranteed label term.

## Quick answer: what is extended maceration?

Extended maceration is a red-winemaking practice in which wine remains in contact with grape solids—mainly skins and seeds, and sometimes stems—for longer than the usual maceration period.

In a typical sequence, red grapes ferment while their skins and seeds are present. Once alcoholic fermentation has finished, the winemaker may leave the wine with those solids for additional time before pressing or separating the wine. This is often called **extended post-fermentation maceration**.

The aim is generally to influence the wine’s phenolic compounds. These include tannins, which contribute grip, structure, bitterness and astringency, and colour-related compounds extracted from the skins. The result may be a wine with more pronounced structure or a different texture, but it can also bring bitterness, drying tannin or other problems if the fruit and conditions are not suited to prolonged contact.

There is no fixed definition such as “30 days”. Published practices range from several days to two months or more. The appropriate length depends on the grape variety, vintage, fruit condition, vessel, temperature, oxygen exposure and the style the winemaker is trying to achieve.

## How does it work?

Maceration is simply the period when grape juice or wine sits with grape solids. The solids are mostly skins and seeds. Stems may be retained, removed or added, depending on the winemaking choice; they are not automatically part of extended maceration.

During red fermentation, the liquid extracts compounds from the solids. After fermentation, the wine contains alcohol and has a different chemical environment from the original juice. Continuing contact can therefore change which compounds are extracted and how they interact.

Eventually, the wine is pressed or otherwise separated from the solids. That ends the principal period of grape-solid contact, although the wine may continue developing through later processes such as ageing.

## What can extended maceration do to wine?

### Tannin and structure

Extended maceration is often used to influence tannin extraction and composition, including tannins from grape seeds. In the glass, this may contribute to firmness, grip or a stronger sense of structure.

But more tannin is not automatically better tannin. Depending on the fruit and the length of contact, the wine may seem drying, bitter or hard. A winemaker may be seeking a firm, structured style, but the finished balance still depends on fruit flavour, acidity, alcohol and the wine’s overall concentration.

### Colour

It is tempting to assume that longer skin contact always produces darker wine. It is not that simple.

Red grape skins contain anthocyanins, important colour compounds. During extended contact, some compounds may be transformed or incorporated into other pigmented forms. A wine may gain colour stability or a different phenolic profile without becoming visibly darker. In some trials, measured colour intensity or anthocyanin levels declined during prolonged contact.

So “extended” does not mean “extra dark”. Colour outcomes depend on the grape, vintage, extraction conditions and the chemistry of the individual wine.

### Texture and mouthfeel

The process can alter how a wine feels in the mouth. It may seem more solid, firm or textural. It may also contribute to bitterness or a drying sensation caused by astringency.

The phrase “extended maceration” therefore tells you about a production decision, not exactly what the wine will taste like. It is a clue that tannin and texture may have received particular attention, rather than a promise of softness, richness or complexity.

## Why might a winemaker choose it?

The decision depends on the condition and character of the grapes, not simply on a desire to make the most powerful wine possible.

Relevant considerations include:

- grape variety and its natural phenolic profile;
- ripeness and the condition of the skins and seeds;
- seasonal or vintage variation;
- temperature and fermentation history;
- the shape and type of vessel;
- whether stems are present;
- exposure to oxygen;
- cap management and extraction during fermentation; and
- when and how the wine is separated from the solids.

Poor fruit or unsuitable conditions can make prolonged contact risky. AWRI identifies potential problems including excessive bitterness, harsh or overly astringent tannins and microbial spoilage. Oxygen exposure and spoilage organisms can contribute unwanted compounds such as acetic acid or ethyl acetate.

That is why the same number of days can produce different results in different wines. Duration is only one part of the decision.

## Extended maceration versus similar terms

### Ordinary maceration

Ordinary maceration is the general period of contact between grape solids and juice or wine. Extended maceration is a longer-than-usual version, commonly continuing beyond alcoholic fermentation.

### Cold soak

A cold soak, or cold maceration, takes place before alcoholic fermentation, usually at low temperature. Extended post-fermentation maceration takes place after fermentation has finished. Both involve grape solids, but they occur at different stages and are not interchangeable.

### Carbonic and semi-carbonic maceration

Carbonic maceration involves intact grapes undergoing an initial fermentation inside the berries in a low-oxygen environment. Semi-carbonic maceration combines elements of intact-berry and crushed-grape fermentation.

Neither is simply “extended maceration”. They describe different fermentation pathways.

### Whole-bunch fermentation

Whole-bunch fermentation means retaining some or all of the bunch structure during fermentation. Extended maceration describes prolonged contact time. Stems can be present during extended maceration, but whole-bunch fermentation and extended maceration are not synonyms.

### Skin contact in white wine

White grapes can also spend time on their skins, including in amber or orange winemaking. That is a separate application of skin contact. Extended maceration, as the term is most commonly used, refers to red-wine production.

### Oak ageing

Oak ageing is contact with oak vessels or oak products. Extended maceration is contact with grape solids. Oak can influence flavour and texture, but it is not another name for maceration.

## What does the term mean in Australia?

Australian producers may use extended maceration, particularly in red-wine production. The Australian Wine Research Institute reports its use most commonly with Cabernet Sauvignon, followed by Shiraz, Merlot and Pinot Noir. This describes documented industry practice, not a recommendation or a rule about those varieties.

“Extended maceration” is a descriptive winemaking term. It is not an Australian-specific legal category that guarantees a particular duration, flavour profile or quality level. It also does not mean that a warm Australian climate or a particular region automatically requires or favours the technique.

If you see the phrase in a producer description, read it as information about how the wine was made. It may suggest attention to tannin, structure or texture, but it does not tell you precisely how long the contact lasted or guarantee that the wine will be full-bodied, smooth or age-worthy.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does extended maceration always make wine better?

No. It can help create a desired structure or texture, but it can also produce excessive bitterness, drying tannin or instability. Its success depends on the fruit, conditions and winemaking intent.

### Does extended maceration soften tannins?

Not necessarily. It can change tannin extraction and composition, but the sensory result may be firmer, more astringent or more bitter. “Softer tannins” should not be treated as a guaranteed outcome.

### Is extended maceration the same as orange wine?

No. Orange or amber wine is generally made from white grapes with extended skin contact. Extended maceration usually refers to prolonged contact in red-wine production. The techniques can overlap in the broad idea of skin contact, but they are not the same category.

### Is extended maceration the same as oak ageing?

No. Extended maceration uses grape skins, seeds and sometimes stems. Oak ageing uses oak. Both can affect texture, but they are separate processes.

### How long is extended maceration?

There is no universal duration. Industry and research descriptions range from several days to two months or more, depending on the wine and the winemaker’s aims. The term means longer than the usual contact period for that particular wine, not a fixed number.
