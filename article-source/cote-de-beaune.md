# Côte de Beaune: A Beginner’s Guide to Burgundy’s Famous Wine Subregion

## Highlights

- Côte de Beaune is a winegrowing subregion of Burgundy in eastern France—not a grape variety.
- It forms the southern part of the Côte d’Or, with Côte de Nuits to the north.
- Chardonnay is especially important in the central and southern Côte de Beaune; Pinot Noir is central to its red wines.
- Beaune, Pommard, Volnay, Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet, Chassagne-Montrachet and Santenay are some of the names you may encounter.
- Burgundy labels usually foreground place names and appellations rather than grape varieties.
- “Premier Cru” and “Grand Cru” describe regulated places and classifications, but they are not a guarantee that every drinker will prefer the wine.
- Côte de Beaune wines can vary considerably with appellation, vineyard site, vintage and producer.

## Quick answer: what is Côte de Beaune?

Côte de Beaune is both the name of a broad Burgundy winegrowing subregion and, separately, the name of a specific French appellation associated with the commune of Beaune.

The wider Côte de Beaune sits in eastern France, within Burgundy’s Côte d’Or winegrowing corridor. It is south of the separate **Côte de Nuits**, and north of the **Côte Chalonnaise**. Beaune is the most useful regional centre and the town that gives the area its name.

The wider subregion contains many village appellations, named Premier Cru sites and Grand Cru appellations. It is particularly associated with Chardonnay-based white wines from places such as Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet and Chassagne-Montrachet. Pinot Noir is also central to the region’s red wines, including those associated with Beaune, Pommard, Volnay and Santenay.

The most important lesson is simple: **Côte de Beaune is a place framework, not one uniform wine style**. The exact appellation, site, vintage and producer all matter.

## Where is Côte de Beaune?

The geographic hierarchy is:

**France → Burgundy/Bourgogne → Côte d’Or → Côte de Beaune**

Côte de Beaune is generally south of Dijon, with Beaune as the practical orientation point. From Dijon, Beaune is roughly 40–60 minutes by road, depending on the route and traffic. That is a geographical guide rather than a precise travel promise.

Beaune is useful as a regional centre, but the town does not define the entire Côte de Beaune. The wider winegrowing area includes familiar places such as Aloxe-Corton, Pernand-Vergelesses, Savigny-lès-Beaune, Pommard, Volnay, Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet, Chassagne-Montrachet and Santenay.

The Côte de Nuits is a separate sibling subregion to the north, not a parent of Côte de Beaune. Both sit within Burgundy and the Côte d’Or. Farther south are the separate Côte Chalonnaise and Mâconnais. The Hautes Côtes de Beaune is also a separate geographical denomination, on higher ground west of the main Côte de Beaune.

For broader orientation, see our guides to [France](/france/) and [Burgundy](/burgundy/).

## Why does the name mean two things?

This is where Burgundy terminology can become confusing.

**The wider Côte de Beaune** is a winegrowing region containing many different appellations.

**The specific AOC Côte de Beaune** is a narrower, legally defined appellation associated with vineyards in the commune of Beaune. It is not a catch-all name for every wine produced in the wider subregion.

So a bottle labelled Côte de Beaune is not necessarily expressing the same geographic information as one labelled Meursault, Pommard or Puligny-Montrachet. Those are more specific appellation names, each with its own defined area and production rules.

The town **Beaune**, the specific **Côte de Beaune AOC** and the wider **Côte de Beaune winegrowing region** are related—but they are not interchangeable.

## What grapes and wine styles is it known for?

### Chardonnay

Chardonnay is especially prominent in the central and southern Côte de Beaune. Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet, Chassagne-Montrachet, Corton-Charlemagne and the Montrachet family are important reference points when learning about the region’s white wines.

Côte de Beaune Chardonnay can vary from relatively restrained and fresh to fuller, more textured or oak-influenced. It is better to treat these as possible directions than as a fixed regional tasting profile. Appellation, site, vintage and winemaking choices can all change the result.

If you are still getting comfortable with the grape, our guide to [Chardonnay](/what-is-chardonnay/) is a useful companion.

### Pinot Noir

Pinot Noir is central to the Côte de Beaune’s red-wine identity. Beaune, Pommard, Volnay and Santenay are useful names to recognise, although they should not be treated as automatic style labels.

Red wines from the wider area may show different combinations of red or darker fruit, savoury notes, floral character, tannin and structure. The exact expression depends on the appellation, the named site, the vintage, the producer and the way the wine was made and matured.

The contrast with Côte de Nuits can help beginners: Côte de Nuits is commonly associated with Pinot Noir, while central and southern Côte de Beaune are especially associated with Chardonnay. But neither statement is absolute. Côte de Beaune is not “all white”, and Côte de Nuits is not “all red”.

You can learn more in our guide to [Pinot Noir](/what-is-pinot-noir/).

## How do Premier Cru and Grand Cru fit in?

Burgundy labels can refer to several geographic and classification levels:

- **Regional appellation:** a broad Burgundy designation.
- **Village appellation:** a more specific legally defined area, often named for a commune such as Meursault, Pommard or Volnay.
- **Premier Cru:** a defined vineyard site, or *Climat*, within an eligible village appellation.
- **Grand Cru:** a separate appellation category for specifically delimited sites.

A **Climat** is not simply a description of the weather. In Burgundy, it means a precisely delimited vineyard site with its own recognised identity and history.

This structure is useful, but do not read it as a perfect quality ladder. Premier Cru and Grand Cru terms communicate regulated origin and classification. They do not guarantee that every bottle will suit your taste, nor do they remove the influence of producer, vintage or winemaking.

The label hierarchy is best understood as increasingly specific information about place—not as a promise that one wine must be better for every drinker.

## What are the landscape and soils like?

Côte de Beaune forms part of Burgundy’s limestone-based escarpment system. Its vineyards occupy different combinations of slope, aspect, elevation and drainage, with geological materials including limestone, marl and clay.

These differences can influence how vines ripen and how wines develop, but they do not work like a simple recipe. It would be misleading to say that limestone always creates one flavour, or that a particular aspect automatically produces a better wine.

A more useful way to think about the region is that **site is one part of a larger system**. Grape variety, farming, harvest decisions, vintage conditions, winemaking and maturation all contribute to the wine in your glass.

That is why two wines from the broader Côte de Beaune can taste noticeably different even when they share a broad regional identity.

## How should an Australian drinker read a Côte de Beaune label?

Australian labels often make grape variety and regional origin fairly visible, although labelling practices vary. Burgundy labels more commonly make the **place name** the main clue.

When you see a Côte de Beaune wine on an Australian restaurant list or specialist shelf, try this sequence:

1. **Find the exact place name.**  
   Côte de Beaune may refer to the specific AOC, while Meursault, Pommard or Volnay identifies a village appellation.

2. **Look for Premier Cru wording.**  
   A village name followed by “Premier Cru” and a named Climat indicates a defined site within that village appellation.

3. **Recognise Grand Cru names.**  
   Grand Cru wines often foreground the name of the Grand Cru site or appellation. Corton and Montrachet-related names are important Côte de Beaune examples.

4. **Do not expect the grape variety to be the headline.**  
   Knowing the appellation helps you understand whether Chardonnay or Pinot Noir is the likely principal grape, subject to the relevant rules.

5. **Treat the classification as a place guide.**  
   It tells you about regulated origin and specificity. It does not dictate whether you will enjoy the wine.

This is an orientation rather than a claim that French and Australian labelling systems are equivalent. They are built around different traditions and rules. The practical takeaway is simply that a Burgundy label may ask you to learn the place before the grape.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Côte de Beaune a grape?

No. Côte de Beaune is a geographic winegrowing name and also the name of a specific AOC. Chardonnay and Pinot Noir are the principal grapes associated with the wider area.

### Is Côte de Beaune the same as Beaune?

No. Beaune is a town and a village appellation. Côte de Beaune can refer to the wider winegrowing subregion or to the narrower Côte de Beaune AOC.

### Is Côte de Beaune mainly white wine?

It is especially associated with important Chardonnay-based white wines, particularly in its central and southern areas. However, Pinot Noir is also central to the region’s red wines, and the wider Côte de Beaune is not one uniform style.

### Is Côte de Beaune part of Côte de Nuits?

No. Côte de Beaune and Côte de Nuits are separate sibling subregions within Burgundy’s Côte d’Or. Côte de Nuits lies to the north.

### Does Premier Cru always mean better?

Not automatically. Premier Cru identifies a defined classified site within a village appellation. Preference still depends on the site, producer, vintage and your own taste.
