# What is free-run juice?

## Highlights

- Free-run juice is the juice that drains naturally from grapes before, or separately from, stronger pressing.
- Press juice is recovered by applying pressure to the grape skins, pulp and seeds.
- Wineries may keep the fractions separate, treat them differently or blend them later.
- Free-run does not automatically mean better, cleaner or more premium.
- The differences between fractions depend on the grapes, equipment and wine style.

## Quick answer

Free-run juice is grape juice released naturally during crushing, draining or loading grapes into a press, without substantial additional pressure. Once pressure is applied to recover more liquid from the grape solids, the resulting juice is generally called press juice, pressed juice or a press fraction.

A simple way to picture it is a basket of crushed grapes. Some juice runs out under gravity and the weight of the grapes themselves. Pressing then squeezes more juice from the skins, pulp and possibly seeds.

The two fractions can be collected together or managed separately. A winemaker might assess them independently, ferment them in different batches, treat them differently or blend them later. The choice depends on the fruit, the winery’s equipment and the style of wine being made.

## Free-run juice versus press juice

The dividing line is not identical in every winery. Some presses collect naturally draining juice and early, gentle pressings together. Other equipment allows more stages to be separated. In some production systems, such as certain decanter processes, the same discrete fractions may not be created at all.

Pressing brings the juice into greater contact with grape solids. As a result, press fractions can differ from free-run juice in suspended solids, acidity, pH and phenolic compounds. Harder pressings may contain more phenolics derived from the skins. These compounds can influence a wine’s colour, bitterness, astringency and texture.

That does not mean every press fraction tastes harsh, or that every free-run fraction is delicate. The result depends on grape variety, fruit condition, skin contact, press design, the pressure programme and the intended wine style. A white wine and a red wine may also use the terms at different stages of production.

## Is free-run juice better?

Not automatically. “Free-run” describes how the juice was obtained; it is not a universal quality rating.

There is a traditional view that naturally released juice is preferable because it has had less forceful contact with grape solids. In practice, winemakers may value different fractions for different reasons. Press juice can contribute useful structure or texture, while some fractions may be separated to reduce unwanted bitterness or coarseness. They can also be blended to achieve a particular balance.

So if you see or hear “free-run juice” in a winery, understand it as a production term—not a guarantee that the resulting wine is superior.

## What does “must” mean?

In technical winemaking language, **must** usually means unfermented grape material. Depending on the context, it can include juice along with skins, seeds and pulp. **Free-run juice** is more specific: it refers to the liquid fraction released naturally.

Australian wine-industry sources use “free-run juice”, as well as terms such as “free-run/soft pressings juice” and “hard pressings juice”, as ordinary winemaking terminology. It is not established here as a regulated Australian label claim, and wineries may handle the fractions differently.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is free-run juice unpressed?

It is juice released without substantial additional pressure. Grapes may still have been crushed, destemmed or loaded into a press before the liquid drains.

### Is press juice wasted?

No. Press juice is a useful winemaking fraction. It may be fermented separately, treated, blended with free-run juice or used according to the desired wine style.

### Does free-run juice make better wine?

It can contribute different characteristics, but the term alone does not predict quality. Grape condition, winemaking decisions and the final blend matter more than the label “free-run”.
