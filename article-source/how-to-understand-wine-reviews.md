# How to Understand Wine Reviews

## Highlights

- A wine review is an informed interpretation, not an objective verdict.
- Tasting notes describe aromas, flavours and sensations—not ingredients added to the wine.
- Acidity, tannin, body, sweetness, alcohol and finish describe how a wine is built or feels.
- Words such as “balanced” and “complex” are judgements, not precise measurements.
- A score only makes sense within the reviewer’s own scoring system.
- The useful question is not “Is this wine good?” but “Does this sound like a wine I might enjoy?”

## Quick answer: what does a wine review tell you?

A wine review describes how someone experienced a wine at a particular time and under particular tasting conditions. It usually combines what the reviewer smelled and tasted, how the wine felt in the mouth, an assessment of its overall quality and sometimes a numerical score.

That makes a review useful—but not infallible. Different people can notice different aromas, use different words or respond differently to the same level of acidity or tannin. Temperature, glassware, food, fatigue and expectations can also influence perception.

Read a review as a set of clues about a wine’s likely style. Then compare those clues with your own preferences, the wine’s price, vintage and context. A high score does not guarantee that you will enjoy the wine, just as an unfamiliar descriptor does not mean the wine is faulty.

## Read a review in four layers

### 1. Sensory descriptions: what might you smell and taste?

Reviewers often describe wine using comparisons such as lemon, cherry, plum, rose, pepper, herbs, earth or toast. These are associations that help communicate an aroma or flavour.

A wine described as smelling of cherry does not contain cherry. A note of vanilla may come from oak influence or from the way the wine’s aromas are perceived; it does not necessarily mean vanilla has been added.

Descriptors are also not perfectly fixed. One reviewer’s “blackberry” may be another reviewer’s “dark plum”. Look for the overall pattern rather than treating every adjective as a technical diagnosis.

### 2. Structure: how does the wine feel?

Structural terms describe the wine’s shape, weight and sensations in the mouth.

- **Dry:** not perceptibly sweet. A dry wine can still taste strongly of ripe fruit.
- **Acidity:** the freshness, tang or mouth-watering sensation in wine.
- **Tannin:** the drying, gripping or slightly rough sensation, especially in red wine.
- **Body:** the wine’s perceived weight or fullness in the mouth.
- **Alcohol or heat:** warmth or prickliness that may make alcohol noticeable.
- **Texture:** the tactile feel of the wine, described with words such as silky, creamy, chalky or chewy.
- **Finish:** the character and length of the aftertaste.

These terms describe different things. Tannin is not the same as bitterness, and full-bodied does not simply mean high in alcohol. A long finish may be pleasant, neutral, hot or bitter; “long” alone is not a guarantee of quality.

### 3. Judgement: how do the parts work together?

Reviewers may describe a wine as **balanced**, **fresh**, **complex**, **concentrated**, **integrated** or **typical**.

These are broader interpretations:

- **Balanced** means the components work together rather than one dominating.
- **Fresh** usually suggests an energetic impression, often linked to acidity.
- **Complex** suggests that several aromas and flavours interact—not merely that a reviewer has listed many descriptors.
- **Concentrated** suggests depth or intensity of flavour. It does not automatically mean sweet or alcoholic.
- **Integrated** often means elements such as oak, tannin or alcohol do not stand apart sharply.
- **Typical** means the reviewer sees the wine as recognisably expressing an expected variety, region or style.

A wine can be atypical yet enjoyable. It can also be technically well regarded but simply not suit your taste.

### 4. Score and context: what is the reviewer’s conclusion?

A score compresses a larger opinion into a number. Some publications use 100-point systems; others use different scales or medal categories. Even two 100-point systems may use different definitions, peer groups and score distributions.

Read the tasting note before focusing on the number. Then ask:

1. Who issued the score?
2. What does that organisation’s scale mean?
3. Was the wine assessed against similar wines?
4. Does the written description match styles you enjoy?
5. Is the score relevant to the wine’s price and vintage?

A score is shorthand within a particular system, not a universal measurement of enjoyment. It should support the written note, not replace it.

## Why two reviews can disagree

The wine itself is only part of the tasting experience. Variety, region, climate, winemaking, vintage and bottle age can all affect style. Serving temperature and glassware can change how aromas and structure appear. Food, perfume, smoke, noise, fatigue and the order in which wines are tasted may also influence perception.

Sensory research from the Australian Wine Research Institute emphasises that tasters differ in sensitivity and that controlled, repeated assessment can reduce some sources of variation. That does not make every home tasting invalid. It simply explains why disagreement is normal.

Reviews can therefore be compared without deciding that one person must be completely right. Look for repeated clues: several reviewers mentioning firm tannin, lively acidity or noticeable oak gives you more useful stylistic information than one isolated adjective.

## An Australian example: reading labels alongside reviews

In Australia, **Shiraz** is the standard label term for the Syrah grape. That does not mean all Australian Shiraz tastes the same. Region, climate, vintage and winemaking can produce very different styles, from lighter and fresher expressions to fuller-bodied wines.

A vintage tells you the year the grapes were harvested. It can provide useful weather and maturity context, but it does not guarantee quality or a particular flavour profile.

A geographical indication, or **GI**, provides information about where the winegrapes originated within a recognised Australian boundary. It offers context rather than a promise that every wine from that region will taste alike or suit every drinker. Variety, vintage and region can help you interpret a review, but none overrides the description itself.

## A practical way to use a wine review

Before buying or ordering, try this five-step method.

### Step 1: Find the context

Note the grape variety, region, vintage, style, alcohol, price and bottle age if given.

### Step 2: Separate flavour from structure

Ask what the reviewer describes in the aroma and flavour. Then look separately for sweetness, acidity, tannin, body, alcohol, texture and finish.

### Step 3: Translate the judgements

Words such as “fresh”, “balanced” or “concentrated” summarise the reviewer’s interpretation. Consider what those words usually mean to you, rather than assuming they guarantee pleasure.

### Step 4: Match the clues with your preferences

If you like crisp wines, descriptions of freshness and prominent acidity may appeal. If you prefer generous reds, ripe fruit, fuller body and softer tannin may be useful clues. If you dislike obvious oak, words such as “heavily oaked”, “toast-dominated” or “vanilla-laden” deserve attention.

These are preference clues, not universal recommendations.

### Step 5: Use the score last

Treat the number as supporting information within its own system. Let the written description, price, occasion and your own taste have the final say.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a fruity wine mean it is sweet?

No. “Fruity” describes aroma or flavour, while “sweet” describes the perception of sugar or sweetness. A wine can smell strongly of fruit and still be dry.

### Does a high wine score mean I will like the wine?

No. A high score indicates a favourable judgement within a particular reviewer’s or competition’s system. Your preferences may be different.

### What does “balanced” mean in a wine review?

It means the reviewer thinks the wine’s components—such as fruit, acidity, tannin, alcohol, sweetness and oak—work together. It does not mean every component is equal.

### Is a long finish always good?

No. A long finish means the flavour or sensation persists. The lingering character may be pleasant, bitter, hot or otherwise unattractive.

### Why do wine reviewers use words such as “pepper”, “leather” or “wet stone”?

They use familiar comparisons to communicate aromas and flavours. These words are associations, not a list of ingredients.

## The simplest rule

A wine review is most useful when you treat it as a translation, not a command. Separate what the reviewer noticed from how they judged it, understand the scoring system, and ask whether the described style matches what you enjoy. That is enough to make reviews far less intimidating—and much more useful when choosing wine.
