# Cerasuolo di Vittoria: Sicily’s DOCG Red Wine Explained

## Highlights

- **What it is:** A protected red-wine appellation from south-eastern Sicily.
- **Where it is:** In defined areas of the Sicilian provinces of Ragusa, Caltanissetta and Catania.
- **What it is made from:** 50–70% Nero d’Avola and 30–50% Frappato.
- **Its legal status:** Italian DOCG and European Union PDO.
- **Typical character:** Dry, fruity and floral, with cherry-red to violet colour, freshness and a soft, harmonious palate.
- **Important distinction:** It is a red wine—not a rosé—and it is different from Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo.
- **Classico:** A geographically and traditionally distinguished version with a later minimum release date.

## Quick answer: what is Cerasuolo di Vittoria?

Cerasuolo di Vittoria is a Sicilian red wine made from a regulated blend of two local grapes: Nero d’Avola and Frappato. Nero d’Avola must make up 50–70% of the blend, while Frappato accounts for 30–50%.

The name refers to a protected place-based wine designation, not to a grape variety. In Italy, it is classified as **DOCG**, short for *Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita*. The European Union classifies it as a **PDO**, or Protected Designation of Origin.

In practical terms, that means a wine labelled Cerasuolo di Vittoria must come from a defined Sicilian production area and follow rules covering grapes, proportions and production. DOCG is not a guarantee that every bottle will be better than every DOC or non-appellation wine. It is a legal indication of origin and regulated production.

## Where is Cerasuolo di Vittoria made?

Cerasuolo di Vittoria comes from south-eastern Sicily, the large Italian island in the Mediterranean. The appellation is contained within Sicily, which is itself part of Italy.

The legal production area crosses defined parts of three provinces:

- **Ragusa**, including the municipalities of Vittoria, Comiso, Acate, Chiaramonte Gulfi and Santa Croce Camerina, as well as part of Ragusa;
- **Caltanissetta**, including defined parts of Niscemi, Gela, Riesi, Butera and Mazzarino;
- **Catania**, including defined parts of Caltagirone, Licodia Eubea and Mazzarrone.

The wording matters. The entire province of Ragusa, Caltanissetta or Catania is not the appellation. Some municipalities are included in full, while others are included only in part.

**Vittoria** is the most useful town to remember because it gives the appellation its geographic reference point. Ragusa is a helpful nearby regional centre for orientation. Catania is a useful major-city reference when placing the area on a map, but the production zone is narrower than either the island or the provinces named above.

## What grapes are in the blend?

The blend is one of the clearest ways to understand the wine.

**Nero d’Avola** must contribute 50–70%. It commonly brings colour, ripe fruit and structural weight. **Frappato** contributes 30–50% and is often associated with perfume, red-fruited character and freshness.

Those flavour roles are useful tasting shorthand, not separate legal guarantees. Vineyard conditions, harvest timing and winemaking choices can change how much weight, acidity, tannin or oak a particular bottle shows.

The appellation’s rules also specify a maximum yield of 8,000 kilograms of grapes per hectare, minimum alcohol of 12.50% by volume and minimum total acidity of 5 grams per litre.

## What does Cerasuolo di Vittoria taste like?

The official description presents Cerasuolo di Vittoria as cherry red through violet in colour, floral to fruity in aroma, and dry, full, soft and harmonious on the palate.

For a drinker, that often translates to a red with:

- cherry and other red-fruit aromas;
- a dry finish rather than obvious sweetness;
- fresh acidity;
- a soft, rounded texture;
- moderate to fuller body, depending on the wine;
- tannin that may be present without dominating.

It is best thought of as a wine that can sit between a lighter, aromatic red and a fuller Sicilian red. Some examples may feel bright and supple, while others may be deeper and more structured. A grape blend and appellation rules create a starting point, not an identical flavour profile in every vintage.

Cerasuolo di Vittoria Classico may show more developed aromas as it ages, including dried plum, chocolate, leather or tobacco. These are possible signs of development, not compulsory flavours.

## What does “Classico” mean?

**Cerasuolo di Vittoria Classico** is the geographically and traditionally distinguished expression within the appellation. It comes from the historic core of the production area and has a later minimum release date.

Standard Cerasuolo di Vittoria may be released from 1 June of the year after harvest. Classico may be released from 31 March of the second year after harvest.

These are minimum release dates. They do not mean that every Classico wine has spent a particular period in oak, or that the word automatically means “better”. It identifies a regulated version of the appellation with different release requirements.

## How should Australians read the label?

On an imported bottle, look for wording such as:

- **Cerasuolo di Vittoria**;
- **Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG**;
- **Cerasuolo di Vittoria Classico**;
- **Cerasuolo di Vittoria Classico DOCG**.

The vintage year is required under the accessible appellation specification. “DOCG” tells you that the wine belongs to an Italian protected designation with defined origin and production rules. It should not be treated as an Australian Geographic Indication, because Australian and Italian systems are not interchangeable.

Also remember that **Vittoria** alone is not enough to establish that a bottle is Cerasuolo di Vittoria DOCG. Vittoria can also refer to a separate DOC designation.

## Cerasuolo di Vittoria is not Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo

The word *cerasuolo* can cause confusion.

- **Cerasuolo di Vittoria** is a Sicilian DOCG red made from Nero d’Avola and Frappato.
- **Cerasuolo d’Abruzzo** is a separate Abruzzo DOC rosé associated with Montepulciano.

They are different geographical designations and different wine contexts. If you see the full name Cerasuolo di Vittoria, expect a red wine with cherry-red to violet colour—not the pale pink appearance commonly associated with rosé.

## Serving and food pairing

Try a fresh example at approximately **14–16°C**, slightly cooler than a warm room. A standard red-wine glass is suitable. Decanting is not automatically necessary, although a more structured or mature bottle may open up after some time in the glass.

Its dry fruit, freshness and moderate structure make it a flexible partner for Australian meals. Consider it with:

- tomato-based pasta;
- pizza with moderate toppings;
- roast or grilled chicken;
- roast vegetables, including aubergine;
- pork;
- lighter red-meat dishes;
- savoury fish dishes with tomato, herbs or stronger seasoning.

These are starting points, not rules. The best pairing depends on the dish’s intensity, sauce and preparation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Cerasuolo di Vittoria a rosé?

No. Cerasuolo di Vittoria is a protected Sicilian red wine. Its official colour range runs from cherry red to violet.

### Is Cerasuolo di Vittoria a grape?

No. It is an appellation—the protected name for a wine from a defined place made under defined rules.

### Is Cerasuolo di Vittoria sweet?

It is officially described as dry. Fruit aromas, especially cherry, can make a dry wine seem fruity without making it sweet.

### What are the main grapes?

Nero d’Avola and Frappato. Nero d’Avola must make up 50–70% of the blend, and Frappato 30–50%.

### Is Classico a quality ranking?

Not exactly. Classico identifies a geographically and traditionally distinguished version with a later minimum release date. It should not be read as an automatic guarantee of quality.
