# How to pronounce common wine grapes

Wine grape names can look more intimidating than they sound. The practical rule is simple: say the name clearly, use an ordinary English pronunciation where that is common, and do not worry about reproducing another accent perfectly.

The pronunciation cues below are approximate Australian-English guides, not formal phonetic spellings. You may hear slightly different versions in Australia, Britain, the United States or the grape’s source language.

## Highlights

- Pronunciation cues are approximate Australian-English guides, not strict rules.
- Clear, confident speech matters more than reproducing a source-language accent perfectly.
- Regional accents can produce several acceptable pronunciations of the same grape name.

| Grape name | Approximate pronunciation |
|---|---|
| Chardonnay | **SHAR-don-ay** |
| Pinot | **PEE-noh** |
| Pinot Noir | **PEE-noh NWAHR** |
| Sauvignon | **SOH-vee-nyon** |
| Cabernet Sauvignon | **CAB-er-nay SOH-vee-nyon** |
| Merlot | **MER-loh** |
| Riesling | **REES-ling** |
| Sangiovese | **san-joh-VAY-zay** |
| Gewürztraminer | **geh-VURTS-trah-mee-ner** |

These are useful starting points for Australian wine lists, labels, classes and conversations. They are not the only acceptable ways to say the names.

## The pronunciations people most often ask about

### Chardonnay

Say **SHAR-don-ay**.

The first sound is closer to “shar” than “char”. The final vowel can vary with accent, so “SHAR-don-ay” is a practical English guide rather than a precise reproduction of every French sound.

### Pinot and Pinot Noir

Say **PEE-noh** for Pinot.

For Pinot Noir, say **PEE-noh NWAHR**. The final “r” in “Noir” may be more noticeable in some English accents than others.

### Sauvignon and Cabernet Sauvignon

Sauvignon is approximately **SOH-vee-nyon**.

Cabernet Sauvignon is approximately **CAB-er-nay SOH-vee-nyon**. The ending of “Sauvignon” is difficult to capture perfectly with English spelling, so treat the cue as a helpful approximation.

### Merlot

Say **MER-loh**.

English accents differ in the vowel of the first syllable, but “MER-loh” is an easy form to use when ordering or discussing wine.

### Riesling

Say **REES-ling**.

You may also hear a sound closer to **REES-zling** in English. This is a useful example of why pronunciation guides should not pretend there is one accent-free version.

### Sangiovese

Say approximately **san-joh-VAY-zay**.

The final syllables can vary when English speakers adapt the Italian name. Clear, steady pronunciation is more useful than trying to perform an Italian accent.

### Gewürztraminer

An English wine pronunciation is approximately **geh-VURTS-trah-mee-ner**.

The German source-language form can sound different. The English form is not automatically a mistake; it is one of the ways the name is used in English-language wine contexts.

## Source-language pronunciation versus wine-list pronunciation

Many grape names come from French, Italian, German or Spanish. A source-language pronunciation may differ from the form commonly used in English-speaking wine shops, restaurants and education.

That does not make the everyday English version wrong. Wine language travels between countries, and speakers naturally adapt unfamiliar sounds. Australian wine conversations may include both English trade conventions and pronunciations closer to the original language.

A pronunciation cue is therefore best understood as a communication tool. It helps you recognise a name and say it confidently; it does not judge your expertise.

## A note about Shiraz, Syrah and Pinot Gris

Some names need more than a pronunciation lesson.

**Shiraz** and **Syrah** are names used in different wine contexts for the same broader grape variety. They are not simply one word pronounced with an Australian accent and another pronounced with a French accent. The naming choice can reflect regional and market usage.

**Pinot Gris** and **Pinot Grigio** are also related names encountered in different language and market contexts. They should not be treated as completely unrelated grapes just because the names sound different.

For both pairs, learn the names you see on the label or list, and ask politely if you are unsure how a venue prefers to say them.

## How to handle pronunciation in real life

If you are ordering wine, a clear approximation is enough. You can also point to the bottle or list and ask, “How do you say this one?” Hospitality staff hear pronunciation variations regularly.

It is usually more helpful to repeat the name naturally than to correct another person’s accent. Wine is for communication and enjoyment, not for passing a pronunciation test.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is there one correct way to pronounce a grape name?

Not always. Dictionaries often record differences between English accents, and source-language pronunciations may differ again. Use a clear, widely understood form.

### Should I use the original French, Italian or German pronunciation?

You can, but you do not have to. An English wine pronunciation is often practical in Australian hospitality and wine education.

### Does pronunciation show how much someone knows about wine?

No. Pronunciation does not determine expertise, wine quality or authenticity. Understanding what is on the label and communicating clearly matter more.

### Why do pronunciation guides use capital letters and hyphens?

They are simple reading aids. Capital letters suggest the strongest beat, while hyphens divide the word into manageable parts. They are not formal linguistic notation.
