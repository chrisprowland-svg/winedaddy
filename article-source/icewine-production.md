# What Is Icewine Production? A Beginner’s Guide

## Highlights

- Icewine is made from grapes that freeze naturally on the vine.
- The frozen grapes are harvested and pressed while still frozen.
- Ice crystals retain much of the grape’s water, leaving a smaller amount of concentrated juice.
- The juice can be rich in sugar, acidity and flavour compounds.
- Icewine is not simply any sweet wine, frozen wine or artificially concentrated wine.
- Late-harvest, botrytised and appassimento wines use different concentration methods.
- In Australia, “Icewine”, “ice wine” and “ice-wine” are protected terms with conditions of use.

## Quick answer: how is Icewine made?

Icewine production begins when grapes are deliberately left on the vine beyond the normal harvest period. The aim is to wait for a suitable natural freeze while keeping enough fruit healthy for winemaking.

When the grapes freeze naturally, they are harvested and pressed while frozen. Ice crystals retain much of the berry’s water, while the remaining liquid juice contains a higher concentration of dissolved sugar and other grape compounds. The result is a smaller volume of concentrated juice.

That juice is then fermented. Yeast converts some of the sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, but the highly concentrated must—the grape juice before and during fermentation—can be difficult to ferment completely. Residual sugar commonly remains in the finished wine, while acidity helps give the wine freshness rather than making it taste merely sugary.

The exact legal definition of Icewine varies by jurisdiction. The central idea is consistent: recognised Icewine depends on naturally frozen grapes on the vine, not sweetness alone or artificial freezing.

## Why leave the grapes on the vine?

Icewine production involves a considerable wait beyond ordinary harvest. This extended hang-time gives the grapes time to reach a natural freezing event, but it also exposes them to risk.

Rain, disease, birds, mammals, wind and berry deterioration can reduce the crop. Grapes must remain healthy enough to meet the relevant production rules and produce sound must. A longer hang-time is therefore not automatically a quality improvement. It is a calculated decision that depends on the vineyard, variety, weather and vintage.

The vineyard also needs a workable harvest window. Labour, transport, lighting, presses and winery access all have to be organised for very cold conditions. If the grapes thaw before pressing, the production process may no longer meet the applicable requirements.

## What happens when frozen grapes are pressed?

Pressing is the defining physical stage of Icewine production.

In ordinary grape pressing, much of the berry becomes juice. With frozen grapes, some of the water is held in ice crystals. The press separates a smaller amount of liquid juice from the frozen material. That liquid is more concentrated, rather like reducing the water in a fruit mixture while leaving more dissolved material behind.

This does not mean every flavour compound increases in exactly the same proportion. The useful beginner’s explanation is simpler: **water is partly held back as ice, so the liquid juice becomes more concentrated**.

The concentrated must is assessed before and during fermentation. Winemakers may examine factors such as sugar, acidity, pH and yeast nutrition, but there is no single universal Icewine specification for sugar, alcohol, acidity or harvest temperature.

## Why can fermentation be challenging?

Yeast converts grape sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide. Icewine must can contain unusually concentrated sugar, which creates a demanding environment for fermentation. Fermentation may proceed slowly or stop before all the sugar is used.

The finished alcohol level depends on the starting juice, yeast performance and winemaking decisions. Residual sugar depends on how far fermentation progresses and what happens afterwards. This is why it is safer to describe Icewine as typically sweet and concentrated rather than promising one fixed alcohol or sweetness level.

After fermentation, the wine may be clarified, stabilised, matured in a suitable vessel and bottled. The exact finishing sequence varies.

## Icewine compared with other sweet-wine methods

Icewine is defined by its production pathway, not simply by the taste of the finished wine.

### Late-harvest wine

Late-harvest grapes remain on the vine longer than usual. They may become riper, dehydrated or shrivelled, but they do not need to freeze naturally. Late harvest and Icewine can overlap in timing, but they are not the same process.

### Botrytised wine

Botrytised or noble-rot wine relies on suitable infection by *Botrytis cinerea*. The fungus changes the berry and can concentrate its contents through dehydration. This is a biological process, whereas Icewine relies on natural freezing. Icewine should not be called botrytised unless a specific wine has evidence of both processes.

### Appassimento

Appassimento concentrates grapes by drying or raisining them, usually after harvest or in controlled conditions. It is a drying process, not naturally frozen-on-the-vine production.

### Artificial freezing

Mechanical freezing can concentrate grapes, juice, must or wine. It is not automatically equivalent to recognised Icewine production. In jurisdictions that protect the term, naturally frozen-on-the-vine grapes are central to its use.

## What should Australian wine drinkers look for?

Australia has substantial regional variation in climate, including areas with frost risk and cooler conditions. However, naturally frozen-on-the-vine production is not a routine national category in the way Icewine is associated with some colder regions.

A cold snap alone does not establish a valid Icewine production run. The grapes must meet the relevant conditions, remain sufficiently healthy, be harvested and pressed appropriately, and comply with applicable labelling rules.

Wine Australia states that “icewine”, “ice wine” and “ice-wine” are protected terms. For Australian wine, the wine must be obtained exclusively from grapes naturally frozen on the vine. This means Australian readers should not assume that a wine described as “iced”, “frozen”, “sweet” or “dessert wine” is legally Icewine.

Imported wines may be governed by the rules of their country of origin. When reading a label, check the wording, origin and production description rather than treating all concentrated sweet wines as interchangeable.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Icewine always made from white grapes?

Not necessarily. Grape varieties vary by region and producer, so no single variety should be treated as a universal requirement.

### Does Icewine have to be harvested at a particular temperature?

Some jurisdictions set specific thresholds. Ontario, for example, has a rule requiring naturally frozen grapes to be picked at minus 8°C or lower. That is an Ontario requirement, not a universal Icewine temperature.

### Is Eiswein the same as Icewine?

They are related terms, but not interchangeable legal definitions. Eiswein is a regulated German category whose grapes must be frozen at harvest and pressing. Other jurisdictions have their own rules.

### Can a winery freeze grapes in a freezer and call the result Icewine?

Not automatically. Artificial freezing and natural freezing on the vine are different production methods, and protected terms may require naturally frozen grapes.

### Is Icewine the same as a sweet wine?

Icewine is generally sweet, but sweetness alone does not make a wine Icewine. The defining distinction is the naturally frozen-grape production process.
