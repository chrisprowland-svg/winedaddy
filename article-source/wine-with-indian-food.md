# Wine with Indian food: how to choose a bottle

## Highlights

- There is no single best wine with Indian food.
- Choose for the dish, not just the word “Indian”.
- Chilli heat, sweetness, acidity, richness, smoke and aromatic intensity all change the pairing.
- Fresh, aromatic or slightly sweet wines are often useful with hot dishes.
- High alcohol and firm tannin can make very spicy food feel more intense.
- White, sparkling, rosé and lighter red wines can all work in the right context.
- Your tolerance for chilli matters as much as any pairing rule.

## Quick answer

The best wine with Indian food depends on the dish. A creamy curry, smoky tandoori dish, tangy lentil preparation and chilli-hot curry do not have the same flavour structure, so they should not be treated as one pairing category.

As a starting point, look for a wine with freshness, moderate alcohol and enough aroma to stand alongside the food. An off-dry Riesling or Gewürztraminer can be useful with chilli heat, while a dry Riesling, unoaked Chardonnay or Pinot Gris may suit tangy, herb-led or vegetable dishes. Sparkling wine or dry rosé can work well with rich, salty or shared dishes. A lighter red may suit smoky food, and a fruit-forward, moderate-tannin Shiraz can work with a richer meat curry when the chilli is not overwhelming.

These are starting points, not guarantees. The most useful question is: **what is strongest in the dish?**

For a broader introduction to the principles behind this approach, see [How Wine Pairing Works](/how-wine-pairing-works/).

## Why “Indian food” is too broad for one wine rule

Indian food includes many regional and stylistic traditions, with different combinations of spices, vegetables, meat, dairy, acidity, smoke and cooking fat. Even dishes with similar names can vary considerably.

“Curry” is therefore not a precise wine-pairing category. A creamy tomato-based dish, a sour tamarind preparation, a smoky grilled dish and a hot chilli-forward curry may all need different wine characteristics.

It is also useful to separate **aromatic spice** from **chilli heat**. Fragrant spices can make a dish smell intense without making it especially hot. Chilli heat creates a different sensory challenge for wine.

## The dish-first checklist

Before choosing a wine, consider these questions:

### How hot is it?

Chilli-hot food can make alcohol feel more prominent. A high-alcohol wine may seem warmer and can make the burn feel more pronounced.

With hot dishes, begin with a fresh, moderate- or lower-alcohol style. A wine with a little residual sweetness—often described as **off-dry**—can soften the perception of heat. It does not remove or neutralise the chilli; it changes the balance of the bite.

Aromatic whites such as Riesling or Gewürztraminer are useful places to start, particularly when they have some sweetness and enough acidity to stay refreshing. If you prefer dry wine, dry Riesling can still work by providing freshness, although it will not soften heat in the same way.

### Is the dish sweet, tangy or creamy?

Sweetness in food can make a very dry wine seem leaner and less fruity. This is one reason a completely dry, firm or bitter-tasting wine may feel less comfortable beside a sweet, fruit-forward sauce.

Acidity—the fresh, mouth-watering quality in wine—can refresh the palate between bites. It can be helpful with tangy tomato, tamarind, yoghurt or citrus flavours, and may stop a creamy or rich dish from feeling too heavy.

For creamy dishes, consider a fresh aromatic white, sparkling wine or an unoaked or lightly oaked Chardonnay. The important feature is not the grape name alone, but the combination of freshness, body and alcohol.

### Is there smoke, char or frying?

Smoke and char give a dish a more savoury, robust edge. Tandoori-style or grilled foods may be more comfortable with a dry rosé, sparkling wine or lighter red than a delicate white would be.

Firm tannin—the drying, grippy sensation often associated with red wine—can be difficult with very hot or strongly seasoned food. That does not mean red wine never works. A softer, lighter-bodied red may suit smoky food when the dish is rich enough and the chilli level is moderate.

### Is it a shared table?

When several dishes arrive together, choose for flexibility rather than trying to match every plate perfectly. Sparkling wine, dry rosé or an aromatic white can provide useful middle ground across rich, salty, fried, vegetable and moderately spicy dishes.

## Practical pathways

### Creamy tomato-based curries

Look for freshness first. An aromatic white, fresh Chardonnay or sparkling wine may help balance richness and keep the meal lively. If the dish is also very hot, consider moderate alcohol and possibly a touch of sweetness.

### Tandoori and grilled dishes

Smoke and char can make dry rosé, sparkling wine or a lighter red appealing options. A moderate amount of tannin may be more comfortable here than alongside a delicate, chilli-hot curry, but a very firm or alcoholic red can still dominate.

### Lentil and vegetable dishes

These can range from light and tangy to rich, creamy, smoky or chilli-hot. Dry Riesling, Pinot Gris, rosé or a lighter red may all be reasonable starting points, depending on the cooking fat, acidity and heat.

### Biryani and rice-based dishes

Think about the whole plate: aromatic intensity, spice fragrance, richness and any meat component. An aromatic white, sparkling wine or dry rosé can be flexible choices. If the dish is rich and the heat is moderate, a softer red may also work.

### Rich or braised meat curries

A fuller red can meet the intensity of a rich meat dish, but do not choose simply because the dish contains meat. Look for a fruit-forward style with controlled alcohol, oak and tannin. In an Australian bottle shop, that might mean a moderate-bodied Shiraz rather than the most powerful style available.

## What to look for in Australia

Australian wine labels do not always make sweetness immediately obvious. “Dry” describes the wine’s lack of noticeable residual sugar; it does not mean flavourless. A dry wine can still be strongly fruity and aromatic.

Alcohol by volume can give you a clue about warmth, but it does not tell the whole story. Body, serving temperature, sweetness, acidity and the individual wine also matter. Similarly, words such as “fresh”, “aromatic”, “crisp” and “light-bodied” are useful clues, not guarantees.

Australian Riesling is commonly made in a dry, high-acid and citrus-fruited style, although off-dry and sweeter examples also exist. That makes it a versatile variety to explore: dry versions can provide freshness, while slightly sweet versions may be worth trying with hotter dishes.

Chardonnay, Pinot Gris, Gewürztraminer, Pinot Noir, Grenache and Shiraz can all be useful starting points, but variety names do not guarantee a particular level of sweetness, alcohol, oak or body. Growing conditions and winemaking create variation.

Serve the wine cool enough for freshness to show, while avoiding the assumption that very cold wine can rescue an unsuitable pairing. If you are ordering at a restaurant, describe the dish’s heat and richness and ask for a fresh, moderate-alcohol style that suits it.

## The simplest rule

Start with the strongest feature of the dish and the heat tolerance of the people eating it.

- **Very hot:** fresh, aromatic, moderate- or lower-alcohol wine; consider off-dry.
- **Tangy or herb-led:** dry Riesling, Pinot Gris or fresh Chardonnay.
- **Creamy or rich:** acidity and freshness; aromatic white, Chardonnay or sparkling wine.
- **Smoky or grilled:** sparkling, dry rosé or a lighter red.
- **Rich meat with moderate heat:** fruit-forward red with controlled tannin and alcohol.
- **Mixed shared table:** sparkling wine, rosé or an aromatic white.

Your favourite pairing may not follow a textbook rule—and that is fine. Pairing is a way to understand what you enjoy, not a test with only one correct answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Riesling the best wine with Indian food?

Not universally. Off-dry Riesling is a strong option for chilli-hot dishes because sweetness can soften perceived heat and acidity keeps the wine fresh. Dry Riesling suits different situations, where freshness is the main goal.

### Can you drink red wine with Indian food?

Yes. Lighter, softer reds may work with smoky or grilled dishes, while a moderate, fruit-forward Shiraz can suit a rich meat curry with moderate heat. Very tannic, high-alcohol reds are less reliable with hot food.

### Is sweet wine better with spicy food?

A little residual sweetness can soften the perception of chilli heat, but it does not remove the heat. The wine also needs enough freshness, and food sweetness can change how dry or bitter the wine tastes.

### What is the safest choice for a mixed Indian meal?

A fresh aromatic white, sparkling wine or dry rosé is often a flexible starting point. Choose according to the hottest and richest dishes on the table, rather than assuming every plate will pair identically.

### Should Indian food always be paired with white wine?

No. White wine is not automatically better than red. The dish’s heat, smoke, richness and texture matter more than its cuisine label or the colour of the wine.
