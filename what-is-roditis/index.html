What is Roditis?

Roditis is a pink-skinned Greek wine grape usually made into fresh, dry white wine. Learn about its taste, style, names, origins and Australian context.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Roditis is a pink-skinned Greek wine grape, usually made into fresh, dry white wine. It can produce wines with refreshing acidity, light to medium body and citrus, floral, fruity or mineral notes.

But Roditis does not have one fixed taste. Vineyard site, crop levels, ripeness, harvest timing and winemaking can make the finished wine simple and soft, or fresher, more concentrated and textured.

Highlights

  • Roditis is a Greek Vitis vinifera wine grape.
  • Its skins are pink or rose-coloured, although it is most commonly used to make white wine.
  • It is usually made as a dry, fresh white wine.
  • Expect possible citrus, floral, fruity or mineral characters.
  • Well-made examples can have refreshing acidity and light to medium body.
  • High yields and less suitable conditions can produce simpler or dilute wines.
  • You may see the grape written as Rodítis, Rhoditis or Rodites.

Where does Roditis come from?

Roditis is principally associated with Greece and is grown widely across Greek wine regions. It is particularly important in parts of the Peloponnese and is associated with wines from Patras, among other Greek wine contexts.

Patras is a place, not another name for the grape. A bottle from Patras may contain Roditis, but Roditis is not limited to that area.

The grape’s Greek identity can also appear in different forms. Rodítis is a version with a diacritic, while Rhoditis and Rodites are recognised spelling variants. Alepou is recorded as a synonym or associated name in European and viticultural references. These names should not be confused with separate Greek varieties such as Sideritis, Assyrtiko, Moschofilero or Savatiano.

What does Roditis wine taste like?

A fresh, dry Roditis may offer:

  • lemon or other citrus characters;
  • floral aromas;
  • fresh fruit;
  • mineral notes;
  • refreshing acidity; and
  • a light to medium-bodied palate.

That is a useful starting point, not a guarantee. Think of the grape as contributing possibilities rather than prescribing a finished flavour.

Roditis can affect a wine’s potential acidity, aroma, body and texture, but it does not determine them alone. The same variety can taste noticeably different depending on where it grows and how the grapes are handled.

Why can Roditis wines vary so much?

Roditis is capable of producing both straightforward everyday wines and more distinctive examples.

When vines carry very large crops, the resulting wine can be simple, watery or flabby. Lower yields and suitable mountain sites can help produce greater freshness and concentration. Careful vinification and a short period of lees contact may add texture, while selected examples can also receive barrel treatment.

These are influences, not rules. A mountain vineyard does not automatically make a great Roditis, and a lightly made wine is not automatically lacking in character. Harvest timing, ripeness and fermentation choices also shape the balance between acidity, fruit, alcohol and texture.

Is Roditis always a white wine?

No. Roditis is pink-skinned, but most wine made from it is white because the juice is generally separated from the skins before significant colour is extracted.

Its main identity for shoppers is therefore as a white-wine grape. Roditis can also be used in blends and in other styles, including some rosé-related expressions, but dry white wine is the most useful place to begin.

How might Roditis appear in Australia?

Australian drinkers are most likely to encounter Roditis in an imported Greek wine, particularly through a specialist retailer, Greek restaurant, wine bar or focused distributor. Australian trade material has listed Roditis wines, although current availability can vary by importer, venue and vintage.

There is not enough verified evidence here to say that Roditis is widely planted or commercially produced in Australia. If you see the name on a local wine list or shelf, check the country and region on the label rather than assuming it was grown in Australia.

How should you serve Roditis?

Serve a fresh, dry Roditis chilled, around 8–10°C as a practical starting point. Very cold serving can mute its citrus and floral aromas. A standard white-wine glass is all you need.

Its freshness makes it a useful match for:

  • seafood and grilled fish;
  • salads and vegetables;
  • light poultry dishes; and
  • Greek appetisers or mezze.

The best pairing depends on the particular bottle. A fuller, more textured Roditis may suit richer food than a very light, crisp example.

Frequently asked questions

Is Roditis a white grape?

Roditis is pink-skinned, but it is most commonly used to make white wine. Skin colour and wine colour are not always the same thing.

Is Roditis sweet?

Usually, Roditis is made as a dry wine, meaning it is not noticeably sweet. Always check the individual label, as the grape variety alone does not guarantee sweetness.

Is Roditis the same as Sideritis?

No equivalence is established here. They should be treated as separate Greek grape varieties.

Is Roditis similar to Sauvignon Blanc?

Some individual Roditis wines may show a similarly fresh or aromatic impression, but Roditis is not Sauvignon Blanc and should not be defined by that comparison.

Is Roditis good for food?

A fresh, dry Roditis can work well with seafood, vegetables, salads, light poultry and Greek mezze. Its acidity and moderate weight make it a flexible table wine.
