Spice aromas in wine: what pepper, clove and liquorice mean

Learn what spice aromas in wine mean, from pepper and clove to liquorice and baking spice, and how grape variety, fermentation, oak and maturation can shape them.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Spice aromas are tasting descriptions for smells that remind us of pepper, clove, cinnamon, anise, liquorice or baking spice.
  • A “spicy” wine does not necessarily contain added spices.
  • Spice aroma is not the same as sweetness, chilli heat or alcohol warmth.
  • Grape variety, fermentation, oak and maturation can all contribute, often together.
  • Australian Shiraz is sometimes described as peppery, but pepper does not prove a wine is Shiraz.
  • Tasting notes are communication tools, not laboratory diagnoses.

Quick answer: what are spice aromas in wine?

Spice aromas in wine are smells—or flavour impressions created partly through aroma perceived in the mouth—that remind a taster of spices or spice mixtures.

The category can include black pepper, white pepper, clove, cinnamon, aniseed, liquorice and baking spice. These descriptions help communicate what a wine seems to smell like. They do not identify one chemical, grape, region or winemaking technique.

A wine may smell peppery without feeling hot. It may smell like cinnamon without being sweet. It may feel warm because of alcohol without having a spice aroma. And a spicy impression does not mean spices were added to the wine.

The most useful approach is to treat “spicy” as a starting point. Ask what kind of spice it suggests, how strong it is and whether the impression is fresh, dried, sweet-spiced, savoury or warm.

What can spice aromas smell like?

“Spice” is a broad family rather than one precise smell. Tasters might describe a wine as:

  • Peppery: like freshly cracked black pepper or white pepper.
  • Clove-like: aromatic, woody and sweet-spiced.
  • Cinnamon-like: gently sweet-spiced and familiar from baking.
  • Aniseed or liquorice-like: savoury, aromatic and slightly sweet-smelling.
  • Baking-spiced: a blend-like impression involving cinnamon, clove and other warm spices.
  • Fresh-spiced: bright, lifted and vivid.
  • Dried-spiced: more powdery, earthy or muted.
  • Savoury-spiced: closer to pepper, liquorice or dried spice than confectionery.
  • Warm-spiced: a general impression of clove, cinnamon or baking spice—not alcohol burn.

These categories overlap. You do not need to choose the “correct” spice if the wine reminds you of several things. “Pepper-like” or “possible clove” is useful, honest tasting language.

Aroma, flavour and mouthfeel are not the same

When you smell wine in the glass, you are assessing its aroma. When you taste it, some aroma travels from the mouth to the nose through the back of the throat. This is called retronasal aroma and contributes strongly to what we commonly call flavour.

Taste is different. It includes sensations such as sweetness, acidity, bitterness, saltiness and umami. Mouthfeel covers physical sensations such as warmth, burn, texture, weight and tannin.

That is why a wine can smell like pepper without being hot. Chilli heat is a burning sensation, while peppery aroma is a smell. Similarly, alcohol can create warmth on the palate, but warmth does not automatically mean the wine smells of warm spice.

Where do spice aromas come from?

There is rarely one simple answer. Several influences can overlap.

Grape variety and grape chemistry

Some spice aromas are linked to compounds that originate in the grape. One well-studied Australian example is rotundone, which can contribute a black-pepper aroma in some wines.

Rotundone is associated particularly with some Shiraz, although it can occur in other varieties too. Its presence varies between sites, seasons and growing conditions. Some people are also less sensitive to it than others.

So pepper can be a useful observation, but it is not a diagnostic test. A peppery wine is not automatically Shiraz, and a Shiraz without an obvious pepper aroma is not unusual.

Fermentation and yeast

Yeast and fermentation conditions can influence a wine’s aromatic profile. During fermentation, yeast can produce aroma compounds and help release or transform compounds from the grapes.

This does not mean that yeast universally creates spice aromas. The result depends on the yeast, the grapes and the conditions. It is safer to say fermentation may shape or reveal a wine’s spice character than to claim it has a single predictable effect.

Oak

Toasted oak can contribute aromas associated with toast, char and spice. An oak-derived impression may seem more like clove, baking spice or a warm, toasted seasoning than the fresh black-pepper character associated with some grapes.

This is one reason “spice” should not be treated as a shortcut to a particular cause. A wine’s spice impression may come from grape character, oak character, or a combination of both.

Maturation

As wine matures, its fruit, oak, savoury and spice impressions can change and become more integrated. However, individual aroma compounds do not all behave in the same way. Bottle age does not automatically make every wine spicier.

A mature wine might show a more blended or savoury spice impression, but that observation should be made from the glass rather than assumed from the wine’s age.

An Australian example: peppery Shiraz

Australian Shiraz is sometimes described as peppery or savoury, particularly when it shows black-pepper character associated with rotundone.

That description is a tendency, not a guarantee. Australian Shiraz varies with site, season, ripeness, winemaking and maturation. The same broad grape variety can produce wines with very different balances of fruit, spice, oak and savoury character.

A useful tasting note might be:

“Medium-intensity black pepper alongside dark fruit and savoury notes.”

That is more helpful than saying:

“This must be Shiraz because it smells of pepper.”

The first statement describes the wine. The second treats one aroma as proof of identity.

How to taste for spice aromas

Try this simple method:

  1. Smell before tasting. Notice the wine while the glass is still, then swirl gently and smell again.
  2. Start broad. Ask whether the impression seems fresh, dried, sweet-spiced, savoury or warm.
  3. Use familiar references. Smell black pepper, white pepper, cinnamon, clove, liquorice or a baking-spice blend if available.
  4. Separate aroma from palate sensations. Check whether the wine is also sweet, warm, tannic or hot. Do not use those sensations as substitutes for aroma.
  5. Judge intensity. “Light”, “moderate” or “pronounced” is often more useful than forcing an exact identification.
  6. Record uncertainty. “Pepper-like” and “possible clove” are perfectly valid.
  7. Compare wines side by side. Repeated comparison helps build aroma memory and shows how fruit, oak and spice can coexist.

What spice aroma does not mean

A spice aroma does not necessarily mean:

  • spices were added to the wine;
  • the wine is sweet;
  • the wine has chilli heat;
  • the wine is high in alcohol;
  • the wine has been aged in new oak;
  • the wine comes from one particular region;
  • the wine is made from one particular grape;
  • the wine has a fault.

A tasting descriptor is a way of sharing perception. It is not a laboratory analysis. When you describe a wine as peppery, clove-like or liquorice-like, you are saying what it reminds you of—not claiming that the wine contains that ingredient.

Frequently asked questions

Does a spicy wine contain spices?

Usually, “spicy” is a tasting description. It means the wine reminds someone of a spice or spice mixture; it does not, by itself, indicate that spices were added.

Is spicy wine sweet?

No. A wine can smell of cinnamon, clove or baking spice while being completely dry. Aroma and sweetness are different parts of the wine experience.

Is peppery wine hot?

Not necessarily. Peppery usually describes aroma. Chilli heat and alcohol warmth are palate sensations and should be described separately.

Does pepper prove a wine is Shiraz?

No. Some Shiraz can show black-pepper character, partly associated with rotundone, but pepper also varies with site, season, wine style and taster sensitivity. Other varieties can show pepper-like character too.

Does oak always make wine spicy?

No. Toasted oak can contribute spice-like impressions, but not every oaked wine smells spicy, and spice can come from sources other than oak.
