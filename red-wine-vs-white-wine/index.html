Red wine vs white wine: what’s the difference?

Understand how red and white wine are made, how they usually taste, and how to choose between them with confidence.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Red and white wine are broad style categories, not guarantees of sweetness, quality or body.
  • Red wine is usually fermented with grape skins, which provide colour and often tannin.
  • White wine is usually separated from the skins before fermentation, so it generally has less colour and tannin.
  • Red wine is not always heavy or dry, and white wine is not always sweet or light.
  • Acidity creates freshness; tannin creates a drying or grippy sensation.
  • Your best choice depends on the texture, body, fruit character and freshness you enjoy.

Quick answer

The main difference between red and white wine is usually how the grapes are processed.

Red wine is generally made from black grapes whose juice ferments with the skins. This skin contact extracts colour and phenolic compounds, including tannin. Tannin can make your mouth feel dry, firm or slightly grippy.

White wine is generally made by pressing grapes and separating the juice from the skins before, or early in, fermentation. It therefore usually has little skin-derived colour and less tannin.

That is the basic distinction, but it is not the whole story. Grape variety, climate, ripeness, fermentation, oak and maturation also affect a wine’s aroma, flavour, acidity, alcohol, body and texture. Colour does not tell you whether a wine is sweet, full-bodied, expensive or better made.

A practical way to choose is to ask what you prefer: freshness, fruit character, body or a drying texture.

How the winemaking differs

After grapes are harvested, the winemaker decides how much contact the juice should have with skins, seeds and sometimes stems. This is explained in more detail in How wine is made.

For a typical red wine, black grapes are crushed and fermented with their skins. During this period, colour and tannin move from the solid grape material into the fermenting juice. The length and intensity of this contact influence the wine’s colour, flavour and structure.

For a typical white wine, grapes are pressed and the juice is separated from the skins before fermentation. This limits extraction of colour and tannin. Some white wines may still spend time on their skins to add flavour, body or phenolic texture.

This is why a red wine is usually darker and more structured, while many white wines are lighter in colour and feel more refreshing. They are tendencies, not rules.

How red and white wine usually taste

Feature Red wine often shows White wine often shows
Colour Ruby, purple, garnet or brick shades Pale lemon, green-gold, gold or amber shades
Tannin More likely to be noticeable Usually low, although skin-contact whites can be grippy
Acidity Can be low, medium or high Often prominent in fresh styles, but can vary
Fruit character Red or black fruit, sometimes floral, savoury or earthy Citrus, orchard, stone or tropical fruit, sometimes savoury
Body Light, medium or full Light, medium or full
Texture Silky, firm, velvety or drying Crisp, creamy, oily or textured

A wine’s colour is only one clue. A pale red such as some Pinot Noir can be light-bodied and bright, while a Chardonnay can be full-bodied, rich and textured.

Tannin

Tannin is a group of phenolic compounds that can create a drying, puckering or grippy feeling in your mouth. It is not simply the same as bitterness. Tannin is usually more noticeable in red wine because red winemaking normally involves extended skin contact, but it is not exclusive to reds.

If you enjoy a firm, structured sensation, you might like a red with noticeable tannin. If you prefer a smoother or more immediately refreshing feel, try a low-tannin red or a white wine.

Acidity

Acidity gives wine freshness and a mouth-watering quality. It occurs in both red and white wine. A crisp white may make acidity particularly obvious, but some red wines are also high in acidity.

Body

Body is the wine’s apparent weight or fullness in your mouth. Think of the difference between skim milk and full-cream milk—not as a perfect comparison, but as a way to imagine light and fuller textures.

Both red and white wines can be light-, medium- or full-bodied. Colour alone does not determine body.

Dryness and sweetness

“Dry” means a wine has little perceived sweetness. It does not mean high in alcohol, high in tannin or red in colour.

Red wine can be sweet, and white wine can be completely dry. Sweetness is principally related to residual sugar and how the wine’s components balance on the palate—not to its colour.

Australian examples

In Australia, you may commonly encounter:

  • Red wines: Shiraz, Cabernet Sauvignon and Pinot Noir.
  • White wines: Chardonnay, Sauvignon Blanc, Riesling and Semillon.

These names identify grape varieties, not fixed flavour profiles. An Australian Shiraz may be light or full-bodied; Chardonnay may be crisp or creamy; Sauvignon Blanc may be lean and fresh or made in a richer style. Region, vintage and winemaking all matter.

How to serve them

Broad guidance is more useful than a rigid number.

  • Serve most white wines chilled.
  • Fuller-bodied whites are often better lightly chilled rather than extremely cold.
  • Light-bodied reds can be served slightly cool, especially on a warm Australian day.
  • Medium- and full-bodied reds are usually best cool rather than warm or hot.
  • If a wine is too cold, let it warm briefly. If a red is too warm, a short time in the refrigerator can make it feel fresher.

“Room temperature” is an imprecise traditional phrase. In a warm Australian home, it may leave red wine hotter than intended.

Which should I try?

Choose a fresh, crisp white if you enjoy citrus, green apple, lively acidity or a lighter-feeling wine.

Choose a richer white if you want more body, creaminess or a fuller texture. Chardonnay can be a useful place to explore, although styles vary.

Choose a light-bodied red if you want red-fruit character without a strongly drying finish. Pinot Noir is one possible example.

Choose a fuller, more structured red if you enjoy darker fruit, firm texture and noticeable tannin. Shiraz and Cabernet Sauvignon can illustrate this direction, but neither is always full-bodied.

If you prefer sweetness, read the label or producer description rather than choosing by colour.

The useful exceptions

Rosé is commonly made from black grapes with much shorter skin contact than red wine, giving it a paler colour.

Some white grapes are fermented on their skins for an extended period. These skin-contact, amber or orange wines can be deeper in colour and more tannic than conventional whites.

Sparkling wine can be white, rosé or red. Some red wines are also naturally pale and light-bodied.

The simplest explanation remains useful: red wine usually gets more colour and tannin from skin contact, while white wine usually does not. The more wine you explore, the more overlap you will find.

There is no universally better colour. Choose according to what you enjoy, what you are eating, the weather and the style described on the bottle.
