What is Greco?

Greco is a white Italian wine grape associated with Campania and Greco di Tufo. Learn the difference between the grape, the appellation and similar names.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Greco is a white Italian wine-grape variety, best known for dry white wines from southern Italy—especially Greco di Tufo DOCG in Campania’s inland Irpinia area.

The name can be confusing because Greco is the grape, while Greco di Tufo is also the name of a protected Italian wine designation. They are closely connected, but they are not interchangeable terms.

Highlights

  • Greco is a white wine grape associated mainly with southern Italy.
  • It is particularly linked with Campania, Irpinia and the province of Avellino.
  • Greco di Tufo is a protected Italian designation made mainly from Greco.
  • Greco wines are often dry, fresh and structured, but styles vary.
  • Typical descriptions include citrus, peach, floral, mineral, savoury and bitter-almond notes.
  • Greco should not be confused with Greco Bianco, Greco Nero or Grechetto.
  • Australian drinkers may encounter Greco on imported Italian bottles, restaurant lists and specialist retail shelves.

Greco is the grape; Greco di Tufo is the appellation

A useful way to remember the distinction is simple:

Greco is the grape. Greco di Tufo is a protected wine designation from Campania made mainly from that grape.

Greco di Tufo is a DOCG—Italy’s controlled and protected geographical designation—associated with the Irpinia area of Campania. Its still and sparkling wines must contain at least 85% Greco, with up to 15% Coda di Volpe Bianca permitted under the production specification.

That means a bottle labelled Greco di Tufo tells you about both its geographical origin and the rules governing the wine. A bottle labelled Greco, however, does not automatically mean it comes from Greco di Tufo. It may simply identify the grape variety.

Tufo is a locality within the Greco di Tufo production area, not another name for every Greco vineyard or wine.

What does Greco wine taste like?

Greco commonly produces dry white wines with noticeable freshness and structure. Depending on where it is grown and how it is made, a wine may feel light to full-bodied, firm or rounded, and crisp or more textural.

You may find aromas and flavours such as:

  • citrus and other white-fleshed fruit;
  • peach;
  • floral notes;
  • mineral impressions;
  • savoury character; and
  • a bitter-almond note, particularly on the finish.

These are broad tendencies rather than a tasting checklist. Ripeness, vineyard conditions, blending and winemaking can all change the result. Not every Greco wine will be full-bodied, mineral or almond-like.

Greco di Tufo is also made in sparkling versions, so the style is not limited to still white wine.

Where is Greco grown?

Greco is most strongly associated with southern Italy, particularly Campania’s Irpinia area, Avellino province and the Greco di Tufo designation.

The name should not be confused with Greco Bianco, a distinct grape associated with Calabria. Nor should Greco automatically be merged with Grechetto or Greco Nero: similar names do not necessarily indicate the same variety.

The relationship between grape, place and wine name is common in European wine. A grape can be grown in more than one place, while an appellation applies only to wine made within a defined area under its rules.

How might Australians encounter Greco?

In Australia, Greco may appear on an imported Italian wine label, a restaurant list or a specialist retailer’s shelf. Wine Australia recognises Greco as a grape-variety name for Australian wine labelling.

If you see Greco on an Australian label, read it primarily as a variety reference. If you see Greco di Tufo, it refers to the protected Italian designation—not simply to any Australian wine made from Greco. An Australian wine cannot use that Italian appellation merely because it uses the same grape.

Australian production and availability of Greco were not established here, so it is safer not to assume the variety is either widely planted or difficult to find.

What should you eat with Greco?

Greco’s freshness and structure make it a natural starting point for seafood, shellfish and grilled fish. It can also suit lighter savoury dishes. Treat this as a useful direction, not a rule: a richer or more textural example may work with more substantial food.

If you enjoy dry white wines with freshness, texture and more savoury character than a very simple light-bodied white, Greco is a variety worth exploring.

Frequently asked questions

Is Greco the same as Greco di Tufo?

Not exactly. Greco is the grape variety. Greco di Tufo is a protected Italian wine designation made mainly from Greco in Campania.

Is Greco the same as Greco Bianco?

No. Greco Bianco is a distinct Calabrian grape and should not be treated as a synonym for Greco.

Is Greco a sweet wine?

Usually, the styles discussed here are dry white wines. Greco di Tufo also includes sparkling expressions, but the grape name alone does not guarantee one particular sweetness or style.
