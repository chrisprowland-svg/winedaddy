Why does wine taste different the next day?

Opening a bottle, oxygen exposure, temperature and aroma release can all change how wine tastes the next day. Learn what to expect and how to store leftovers.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

Wine tastes different the next day because opening a bottle changes its contact with air, while overnight storage changes its temperature and aroma presentation.

Oxygen can make some wines seem softer or more expressive for a while. With continued exposure, it can also reduce freshness and alter aroma and flavour. Meanwhile, volatile aroma compounds may shift or dissipate, a colder or warmer serving temperature can change your impression of acidity and alcohol, and the wine may be tasted in a different glass, with different food and in a different setting.

So the next-day result is not automatically better or worse. It depends on the wine, how much remains, how it was resealed, how warm it became and how you taste it.

The key things to remember

  • Opening a bottle starts a new phase of contact with air.
  • Aeration and oxidation are related, but they are not the same thing.
  • A young, structured red may seem softer after some exposure; another wine may lose freshness.
  • Temperature can make the same wine seem sharper, heavier, warmer or more muted.
  • A half-full bottle usually has more air above the wine than a nearly full bottle.
  • Resealing and keeping wine cool can slow change, but cannot reverse oxidation.
  • Sparkling wine also loses carbon dioxide, so it behaves differently from still wine.
  • “Different” does not always mean “spoilt”.

What happens after you open a bottle?

When a bottle is opened, wine comes into contact with air during pouring, swirling and storage. The amount of air matters: a bottle with one glass left has more headspace—the air-filled space above the wine—relative to its contents than a nearly full bottle.

Oxygen can take part in chemical reactions involving wine’s phenolic compounds, sulphur dioxide and aroma compounds. These reactions develop over time and are influenced by the wine’s composition, temperature, previous oxygen exposure and closure.

The closure still matters to the wine’s history before opening, but after opening the practical factors are the quality of the reseal, the remaining fill level, storage temperature and time.

Aeration is not the same as oxidation

Aeration means exposing wine to air, such as by pouring or swirling it. This can change how aromas are released and how the wine feels in the mouth.

Oxidation refers to chemical reactions involving oxygen that alter wine constituents. It may contribute to loss of fresh fruit, browning and oxidative aromas.

These processes overlap, but they should not be treated as identical. A wine may smell more open after brief aeration without having become oxidised. Nor is leaving wine overnight the same as deliberately decanting it: a decanter creates a different exposure pattern for a different purpose.

Why oxygen can help one wine and hurt another

Some young, tannic red wines can seem softer or more integrated after limited exposure to air. Their aromas may also become easier to notice. This is one reason people sometimes say a wine “improved” overnight.

But oxygen is not a guaranteed improvement button. Continued exposure may flatten fruit, reduce freshness or make oxidative characters more noticeable. These can include bruised or cooked fruit, nuttiness, browning or aldehydic notes. The particular smell depends on the wine and the extent of the change.

Fresh whites and rosés are often valued for bright fruit, floral aromas and freshness, so a loss of those qualities may be especially noticeable. A full-bodied or oak-influenced white may respond differently. Older or delicate wines can have less sensory margin once opened, while some fortified wines are made in styles where oxidative characters are part of their intended profile.

There is no universal next-day rule. The same broad process can be welcome in one wine and undesirable in another.

Why aroma seems different

Wine aroma comes partly from volatile aroma compounds: compounds that can move from the liquid into the air and reach your nose.

Some aromas may become less vivid as the wine is stored. Oxygen may also transform or degrade aroma compounds, including some associated with fresh varietal character. Other aromas may become more noticeable as the wine warms, is swirled or is tasted.

Smell also reaches the nose from the back of the mouth while you drink. This is called retronasal perception. It means that a change in temperature, texture or mouthfeel can alter the flavour you experience, even when you are not consciously thinking about aroma.

Your surroundings matter too. The next day you might use a different glass, eat different food, or taste the wine under different lighting and with a different expectation. “It tastes different” can therefore describe both chemical change and a change in sensory context.

Temperature may be the main difference

Temperature is separate from oxidation, but it can strongly change your impression of the wine.

A colder wine may seem sharper, firmer or less aromatic. It can also appear less sweet and less full in texture. A warmer wine may seem broader, heavier, more alcoholic or less refreshing, while its aromas may be more obvious.

This is particularly relevant in Australian homes. A resealed bottle left on a warm kitchen bench, in direct sun, in a car, garage or shed may change differently from one kept somewhere cool, dark and reasonably stable. Summer heat can accelerate unwanted wine-quality changes, so “room temperature” should not be assumed to mean a cool cellar.

Before deciding that a wine has deteriorated, let it approach a suitable serving temperature and taste it again. Storage temperature and serving temperature are different questions: keeping wine cool can slow change, but chilling does not reverse oxidation.

How different wines may respond

Still red wine

A young, structured red may seem softer or more expressive after limited air exposure. Too much exposure can still reduce fruit and freshness.

White wine and rosé

Bright, fresh styles may show a noticeable loss of fruit, floral character or lift when exposed to too much oxygen. The speed and direction of change vary.

Sparkling wine

Sparkling wine has an extra issue: carbon dioxide loss. As the bubbles escape, the wine may seem flatter even if oxidation is not the main change.

A purpose-designed sparkling-wine stopper and cool storage can help, but ordinary resealing does not preserve carbonation indefinitely. The spoon-in-the-neck trick is not a reliable preservation method.

Fortified wine

Some fortified styles are intentionally made with oxidative characters, such as nutty or aldehydic notes. That does not mean every opened fortified wine is immune to change or remains stable indefinitely.

Older and delicate wines

Older or delicate wines may change noticeably after opening, but there is no guaranteed direction. Some may become more expressive; others may lose their remaining freshness quickly.

What should you do with leftover wine?

  1. Reseal it promptly after pouring.
  2. Keep it cool, dark and reasonably stable, rather than on a sunny bench or in a hot room.
  3. Minimise unnecessary headspace where practical. A nearly full suitable container leaves less air above the wine than a half-full bottle, but transferring wine is not a guarantee of preservation.
  4. Treat sparkling wine separately and use a proper sparkling-wine stopper.
  5. Taste before judging. Allow the wine to reach a suitable serving temperature and compare it with what you remember from the first day.
  6. Do not rely on refrigeration to restore wine. Cooling may slow further change; it cannot undo oxidation or bring lost bubbles back.

There is no universal drinking window that applies to every style, age or bottle. The best next-day decision is sensory: does it still smell and taste enjoyable to you?

Troubleshooting: what does the change mean?

“It tastes better after a night”

Brief aeration may have softened tannin or made aromas more expressive. That does not mean every wine chemically improves overnight.

“It tastes flatter or duller”

Possible explanations include reduced fresh aroma, oxidation, a warmer or colder serving temperature, or lost carbonation. This description alone does not identify one cause.

“It smells vinegary”

Vinegar-like character may indicate volatile acidity or another fault, but a sour impression by itself is not a chemical diagnosis.

“It smells musty”

Musty, mouldy or damp-cardboard-like aromas can be associated with cork taint, but mustiness alone does not prove cork taint. Storage or other environmental factors may also be relevant.

“It is fizzy when it should not be”

A still wine that has become noticeably spritzy is not simply showing ordinary overnight aeration. Retained carbon dioxide, refermentation or another condition could be involved. Treat it as a warning sign rather than a definite diagnosis.

The takeaway

Wine tastes different the next day because air exposure, temperature, aroma release, carbonation and tasting context can all change its sensory experience. Oxygen may briefly help some wines, while excessive exposure can reduce freshness. Cool, dark storage and prompt resealing can slow change, but no method guarantees that every wine will be better tomorrow.

Frequently asked questions

Does wine always improve overnight?

No. Some wines may seem softer or more expressive, while others become flatter, duller or more obviously oxidised.

Does putting opened wine in the fridge help?

Cool storage can slow further change, but refrigeration does not reverse oxidation. Taste the wine at a suitable serving temperature before judging it.

Does a half-full bottle go bad faster?

It may change more quickly because it contains more headspace relative to the amount of wine. The actual result still depends on the wine, temperature, closure and time.

Is overnight storage the same as decanting?

No. Decanting deliberately changes the wine’s exposure to air for controlled service. A resealed bottle left overnight has different exposure conditions and a longer storage period.
