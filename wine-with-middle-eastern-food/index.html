Wine with Middle Eastern food: how to choose a good match

Learn how to choose wine for varied Middle Eastern dishes, from hummus and mezze to grilled meats, chilli-rich stews and baklava.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • There is no single wine for all Middle Eastern food.
  • Start with the dish’s strongest feature: lemon, herbs, tahini, smoke, chilli, sweetness, grilled meat or richness.
  • Fresh, dry white wines are useful starting points for many dishes.
  • Sparkling wine and dry rosé are versatile choices for a shared mezze table.
  • Chilli heat can make alcohol and tannin feel stronger, so fresher, lower-alcohol or slightly sweet wines may work better.
  • Heavy oak and firm tannin can overwhelm delicate dips, herbs and spicy food.
  • Your guests’ preferences matter as much as any pairing rule.

The quick answer

“Middle Eastern food” describes a wide range of dishes and culinary traditions, so pairing works best when you focus on the food in front of you rather than the regional label.

Look first for the dominant flavour and texture. Is the dish fresh and lemony, creamy and rich, smoky, fried, herb-filled, grilled, chilli-hot or sweet? Then choose what you want the wine to do: refresh the palate, soften heat, complement aromatic spices or stand up to charred meat.

A fresh, dry, aromatic white—such as Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc or Pinot Gris/Grigio—is a strong place to begin. Sparkling wine and dry rosé can be especially adaptable across a table of mezze. For grilled meats, consider a lighter or medium-bodied red such as Pinot Noir or fresh Grenache, provided the wine is not too tannic or alcoholic. If chilli is prominent, an off-dry white with noticeable freshness can be more comfortable than a powerful dry red.

These are starting points, not rules. The recipe, sauce, heat level and your own taste can change the answer.

Why the dish matters more than the label

A mezze spread might include hummus, falafel, tabbouleh, olives, fried foods, roasted vegetables and grilled meat. These foods do not all create the same experience in your mouth.

Lemon, fresh herbs and sumac make a dish feel bright. Tahini, chickpeas and olive oil add richness and texture. Roasting creates smoke and savoury depth. Chilli brings physical heat, while cumin, coriander, cinnamon and cardamom bring aroma without necessarily making a dish hot.

That is why “wine with Middle Eastern food” is best treated as a decision-making question, not a single traditional pairing.

Match the wine to the main flavour

Fresh salads and herbs

Tabbouleh, fattoush and other lemony or herb-led dishes usually benefit from a wine with freshness of its own. Dry Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc or Pinot Gris/Grigio can echo the brightness without making the food feel heavy.

Be cautious with heavily oaked wines: their toast and weight may compete with fresh herbs and citrus.

Hummus, falafel and creamy dips

Hummus combines chickpeas, tahini, lemon, garlic and olive oil. Its creamy, nutty texture and richness make acidity useful. Sparkling wine, a fresh white or dry rosé can provide lift, while bubbles can be particularly appealing with fried falafel.

A very tannic red is rarely the most flexible choice for a table led by dips and fried mezze. It may taste more drying or bitter beside savoury, earthy foods.

Baba ganoush and roasted vegetables

Smoky aubergine, garlic, tahini and olive oil call for a wine with enough flavour to engage with the dish, but not so much oak that the smoke becomes harsh. Try a fresh white, dry rosé or lighter red.

Roasting can also bring out bitterness and char. Firm tannin may feel more noticeable alongside those flavours, so moderate structure is often easier to enjoy.

Kofta, shawarma and grilled meat

Charred or roasted meat can support a red wine, but the whole plate still matters. Herbs, yoghurt, pickles, chilli and salad may make a lighter-bodied red or dry rosé more useful than a big, high-alcohol style.

Pinot Noir and fresh Grenache are Australian-friendly style directions to consider. An unoaked or lightly oaked Chardonnay may also work when the dish is rich but not heavily spiced.

Aromatic stews and rice dishes

A stew’s name does not tell you enough. Consider whether the sauce is savoury, chilli-hot, fruit-sweet or rich with meat. An aromatic white, fuller dry rosé or lighter red may suit a moderately spiced dish. If chilli or dried fruit dominates, a fresher off-dry white can be worth trying.

Rice is similarly neutral until you consider what it carries: stock, herbs, meat, vegetables, nuts or sweet spices.

Chilli is different from spice

Cumin, coriander, cinnamon and cardamom contribute fragrance. Chilli heat creates a burning sensation, and it can make alcohol and tannin feel more intense.

With noticeably hot food, look for freshness and moderate alcohol. A wine with some perceptible sweetness—such as an off-dry Riesling—may soften the sensation of heat. That does not mean every spicy dish needs a sweet wine; acidity, sauce, aromatic intensity and personal preference still matter.

Powerful, high-alcohol reds and strongly tannic wines are the combinations most likely to feel uncomfortable with chilli.

What works best for a shared table?

When several dishes arrive at once, choose versatility over perfection. Dry sparkling wine, dry rosé or a fresh aromatic white can move comfortably between salty, oily, herb-filled and fried foods.

If the meal is strongly meat-led, add a lighter or medium-bodied red. If chilli is central, include a fresh off-dry white. Serving two contrasting styles is often more successful than searching for one wine that matches every plate.

Serve sparkling and sweet wines well chilled, whites and rosé chilled, and lighter reds slightly cool rather than warm. Wine is optional, so always respect guests who prefer not to drink, as well as cultural or religious practices.

What about baklava and other sweet finishes?

Dessert is a separate pairing. Syrup, pastry, nuts and dried fruit can make a dry wine taste austere and less fruity. As a general rule, the wine should be at least as sweet as the dessert if you want a harmonious match.

A sweet wine may work better with baklava or a date-based dessert. Sparkling wine can provide a refreshing contrast to richness, but a very dry style may taste stark beside a notably sweet finish.

A simple five-step method

  1. Name the main dish or feature: salad, dip, fried food, grilled meat, stew or dessert.
  2. Separate heat from aroma: chilli is not the same as cumin or cardamom.
  3. Choose the wine’s job: refresh, soften heat, complement aroma or match weight.
  4. Check structure: avoid excessive tannin, alcohol or oak when the food is hot, bitter, delicate or herb-led.
  5. Allow for preference: the best pairing is one your table actually enjoys.

Frequently asked questions

Is Riesling the best wine with Middle Eastern food?

Not universally. Dry Riesling is a useful starting point for fresh salads, herbs and lemon, while an off-dry version may suit chilli. Other wines may be better for smoky aubergine, grilled meat or sweet pastry.

Can you drink red wine with mezze?

Yes, particularly when the table includes grilled or roasted meat. Choose a lighter or moderate-tannin red if dips, herbs, yoghurt or chilli remain prominent.

Is white wine better with hummus?

Fresh white wine is often a practical choice because its acidity can balance richness from tahini and olive oil. Sparkling wine and dry rosé can also work. There is no absolute rule.

What Australian wines can I look for?

Consider styles such as dry Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Gris/Grigio, unoaked or lightly oaked Chardonnay, sparkling wine, dry rosé, Pinot Noir and fresh Grenache. Choose by style rather than assuming a particular region or label will guarantee a match.

What should I serve if everyone orders something different?

Start with dry sparkling wine, dry rosé or a fresh aromatic white, then add a lighter red or fresh off-dry white if the menu is heavily meat-based or chilli-hot.
