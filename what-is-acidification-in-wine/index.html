What is acidification in wine?

Acidification is the controlled addition of acid to grape juice, must or wine. Learn why winemakers use it, how pH differs from titratable acidity, and how it differs from malolactic fermentation and deacidification.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Acidification is the controlled addition of acid to grape juice, must or wine.
  • It usually increases titratable acidity and lowers pH.
  • The aim is balance and stability—not simply making wine taste sour.
  • pH and titratable acidity are related, but they are not the same measurement.
  • Tartaric acid is the main practical example, although other acids may be used under applicable rules.
  • Acidification is different from malolactic fermentation, deacidification and blending.
  • In Australia, permitted treatments and conditions depend on the current Food Standards Code and the wine category.

Quick answer

Acidification in winemaking means adding a permitted organic acid to grape juice, fermenting must or wine to increase its acidity and generally lower its pH.

Winemakers may consider it when grapes or wine have insufficient natural acidity. This can happen because of grape ripeness, climate, fruit composition or changes during winemaking. A well-judged addition can help restore balance, support a more stable wine environment, influence colour behaviour and improve the conditions for some other winemaking operations.

It is not automatically good or bad. The decision depends on measured chemistry, the intended style and the rules that apply to that wine.

Why acidify wine?

Acidity gives wine freshness and shape. It also affects more than taste. Wine pH influences microbial stability, the effectiveness of sulphur dioxide, tartrate stability, colour behaviour and several other chemical processes.

Acidification may therefore be considered when a juice or wine seems too soft or lacks sufficient natural acidity. It can help bring the wine into better balance, but adding too much acid may create an overly sharp or unbalanced result.

This is why winemakers do not normally rely on taste alone. They measure the wine, consider its intended style and reassess the result after a controlled addition.

pH and titratable acidity: what is the difference?

These two measurements are often discussed together, but they answer different questions.

pH relates to the activity of hydrogen ions in the wine. In practical winemaking, it is particularly important for microbial stability, sulphur dioxide effectiveness and colour chemistry.

Titratable acidity, often shortened to TA, estimates the amount of acid that can be measured by titrating a sample to a defined endpoint. It is more closely related to the wine’s overall acid structure, but it does not tell you the pH by itself.

Two wines can have a similar TA but different pH. Their acid types, mineral composition and buffering capacity may differ. So “high acidity” and “low pH” are not interchangeable descriptions.

A useful way to remember it is:

  • pH helps describe how chemically active the acidity is.
  • TA helps describe the measured quantity of titratable acid.

Both matter when deciding whether acidification is appropriate.

Which acids are used?

Tartaric acid is the principal example in wine acidification. It is naturally present in grapes and is widely used in oenological practice.

Other acids, including malic, lactic or citric acid, may appear in technical or regulatory discussions. They are not interchangeable, however. They behave differently in wine and may have different sensory and legal implications.

Malic acid also needs particular care because it can be affected by later malolactic fermentation. Citric acid should not be assumed to be freely permitted in every wine or jurisdiction.

The relevant question is not simply “Which acid makes wine sharper?” It is which permitted treatment is technically suitable for that juice or wine, at that stage, under the applicable rules.

When can acidification happen?

Acidification may be considered in:

  • Grape juice, before fermentation;
  • Must, meaning crushed grapes and their juice, skins and seeds during production; or
  • Wine, after fermentation or at another later stage.

The best timing depends on the wine and the result sought. Fermentation, skin contact, blending, tartrate precipitation and other processes can change pH and TA. An addition that looks suitable at one stage may not produce exactly the same outcome later.

A broad cellar approach is to analyse the material, calculate a proposed addition, trial it where appropriate, mix thoroughly and test again. The wine is then reassessed for both chemistry and sensory balance. The correct amount cannot be safely chosen from a general table for every wine.

Acidification is not malolactic fermentation

These terms describe different processes.

Acidification adds acid to the juice or wine.

Malolactic fermentation is a biological process in which lactic-acid bacteria convert malic acid into lactic acid. This generally reduces titratable acidity and raises pH, making it a potential deacidifying influence rather than an acidification treatment.

Acidification is also different from deacidification, which aims to reduce acidity or raise pH. Deacidification can involve malolactic fermentation, blending with a less acidic wine or other technical methods.

Acid blending means combining juices or wines with different acidity profiles. It changes the overall composition, but it is not the same as adding acid directly.

The broader phrase acid adjustment can mean either increasing or reducing acidity. It is useful only when the direction of the adjustment is made clear.

What does Australian law say?

In Australia, acid additions and related wine treatments must comply with the applicable Food Standards Code, particularly Standard 4.5.1, which covers wine production requirements and permitted substances.

The exact permission, conditions, compositional limits and any labelling implications depend on the current standard and the product category. International oenological guidance is useful for understanding the concept, but it should not be treated as Australian law.

For commercial production, the current Food Standards Code and competent technical advice should be checked before a treatment is used.

Frequently asked questions

Does acidification make wine taste sour?

It can make acidity more noticeable, but the purpose is not simply to create sourness. A measured addition may restore freshness and balance. An excessive or poorly judged addition can make the wine taste sharp.

Does lower pH always mean higher acidity?

No. pH and titratable acidity measure different aspects of wine chemistry. A lower pH does not automatically mean a wine has a higher TA.

Is acidification a sign of poor-quality grapes?

Not necessarily. Acidification is a technical adjustment used under various production conditions. Its appropriateness depends on the fruit, wine chemistry, intended style and applicable rules.

Can acidification be detected from the label?

Usually, a consumer cannot determine from a standard wine label whether acidification was used. Labelling requirements depend on the relevant rules and product category.

Is acidification the same as adding tartaric acid?

Tartaric acid is a common example of an acid used for acidification, but the terms are not synonyms. Acidification describes the treatment; tartaric acid describes one possible material used in that treatment.

Can a home winemaker acidify wine by taste alone?

Taste can be useful, but it is not a substitute for reliable analysis. Acidification changes pH and TA as well as flavour, so treatment decisions should be measured, controlled and made with appropriate technical guidance.
