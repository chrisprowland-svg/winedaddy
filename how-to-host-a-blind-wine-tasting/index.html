How to host a blind wine tasting

A practical Australian guide to planning, serving and discussing a fair, inclusive blind wine tasting at home.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Blind tasting means hiding a wine’s identity before people taste it.
  • You do not need to be a sommelier—or correctly identify every bottle—to enjoy one.
  • Four to six wines is a practical starting point for a casual home tasting.
  • Consistent glasses, pours and temperatures make comparisons fairer.
  • Ask guests to record observations before making guesses.
  • Preference is just as interesting as accuracy.
  • Water, food, non-alcoholic options and a safe trip home matter as much as the wine.

Quick answer

To host a blind wine tasting, choose a clear theme, decide how many wines to serve, conceal the bottles and pour each wine under a number. Give guests time to taste and write down what they notice before revealing the answers.

A simple tasting might compare one grape variety from different Australian regions, several wine styles, or a group of sparkling, white or red wines. Four to six wines is usually enough for a relaxed gathering. That is a practical recommendation, not a universal limit: professional sensory panels work under different conditions, while a home tasting also includes conversation, food and alcohol.

The aim is comparison and curiosity—not proving who has the best palate. A guess about grape variety, region, vintage or price is a hypothesis, not a fact. A wine tasting is successful when people feel comfortable describing what they notice and discovering what they prefer.

1. Choose the format before guests arrive

First decide what you want the tasting to do. It could be a learning exercise, a comparison of styles, a social activity or a themed challenge.

Then choose:

  • the number of wines;
  • the theme;
  • whether the tasting is fully or partially blind;
  • the serving order;
  • how guests will take notes;
  • whether guesses are optional;
  • how and when the reveal will happen.

In a fully blind tasting, guests do not know the wines’ identities or the intended answer set. In a partially blind tasting, they might know the grape, region, country or number of wines, but not which bottle is which. The second format can be friendlier for beginners.

Keep the theme focused. You might compare one grape variety from different regions, one region made in different styles, or several broad styles within white or red wine. Avoid making the event depend on obscure technical knowledge.

2. Assemble the essentials

You do not need specialist equipment. Prepare:

  • clean, similar glasses;
  • opaque bags, foil, bottle covers or a separate pouring area;
  • numbered labels;
  • a private answer sheet for the host;
  • paper or digital tasting sheets;
  • pens;
  • water;
  • plain food such as bread or crackers;
  • a spittoon, disposable cup or another discreet disposal option;
  • a way to keep wines at suitable, consistent temperatures.

One glass per wine makes comparison easier, but it is not essential. If glasses are reused, rinse them consistently and avoid leaving strong aromas in them. The important principle is consistency: similar glassware, pour sizes and serving conditions for everyone.

A helper can conceal and pour the wines while the host joins the tasting. If you are hosting and tasting, keep your answer sheet private and avoid giving clues through comments, expressions or the order in which you discuss the wines.

3. Conceal the clues

Cover labels, capsules and distinctive packaging where practical. Number each sample and record the order privately. A helper can pour from concealed bottles, or the wines can be transferred to identical vessels if appropriate.

Do not assume decanting is automatically fairer. It can change a wine, and some wines may respond differently to air. If one sample is opened earlier or handled differently, record that as a limitation rather than claiming perfect comparability.

Blind tasting can reduce the influence of labels, prices and reputation. It does not remove every bias. Guests can still be influenced by serving order, glass differences, food, colour, group discussion, personal preferences and previous experience.

4. Plan a sensible serving order

A useful starting order is:

  1. sparkling or lighter wines;
  2. fresh whites or rosé;
  3. fuller or oak-influenced whites;
  4. lighter reds;
  5. fuller or more tannic reds;
  6. sweet or fortified wines, if included.

This is a convention, not a rule. A randomised order may suit a more controlled comparison, particularly if you want to reduce expectations about which wine should come first. Very sweet, intensely aromatic, high-alcohol or tannic wines can dominate the samples that follow, so place them thoughtfully.

Keep the wines at suitable and reasonably consistent temperatures. There is no need to turn the event into a temperature experiment; just avoid asking guests to compare one noticeably warm wine with one noticeably cold wine.

5. Run the tasting

A simple hosting sequence works well:

  1. Explain the theme and reassure guests that this is not an examination.
  2. Pour the first sample without revealing its identity.
  3. Give everyone a quiet moment to look, smell and taste.
  4. Ask guests to write observations before discussing guesses.
  5. Invite optional guesses about broad style, then grape or region if people are interested.
  6. Pause between samples and offer water.
  7. Collect notes or record rankings before the reveal.
  8. Reveal the wines one at a time.
  9. Compare observations and preferences.
  10. Finish with questions rather than declaring a winner.

Useful prompts include:

  • What do you notice in the aroma?
  • Does it seem dry or sweet?
  • How fresh does it feel?
  • Is it light-, medium- or full-bodied?
  • Is there tannin or alcohol warmth?
  • What texture and length does it have?
  • Would you choose to drink it again?

An observation might be “fresh acidity” or “firm, drying tannin”. A guess might be “South Australian Shiraz”. Encourage the first kind of note before the second.

6. Keep scoring simple

For beginners, preference ranking is often more useful than a 100-point score. Ask guests to rank the wines from favourite to least favourite, or simply record whether they would drink each one again.

Another easy format has three parts:

  • Observation: What did I notice?
  • Confidence: How sure am I?
  • Preference: How much did I enjoy it?

If you use points, explain what they mean and keep the system modest. A score is a personal summary, not an objective measurement of quality. Someone can enjoy a wine without identifying its variety, region or price.

7. Handle a suspected fault fairly

If a wine seems musty, mouldy, vinegary, oxidised, reductive or otherwise unusual, pause before revealing it. Ask whether other guests notice something similar, and compare it with a clean glass or a second bottle if one is available.

Set the sample aside rather than forcing everyone to score it. Use language such as “suspected fault” or “unusual character”. A casual group cannot confidently diagnose every wine problem, and sensitivity varies between people. The Australian Wine Research Institute describes faults and taints including cork taint, oxidation, volatile acidity, Brettanomyces and reduction.

Do not assume every sulphury, earthy or musty note proves a fault. Some aromas are stylistic, and one person’s concern may be another person’s acceptable character.

8. Make the event inclusive and safe

Normalise spitting, declining a sample, taking a break and choosing a non-alcoholic drink. A blind tasting should never be organised around finishing every pour.

Provide water, food and non-alcoholic options. Do not include shots, drinking games, sculling or rapid-consumption challenges. NHMRC’s national guidance says healthy adults should have no more than 10 standard drinks per week and no more than four on any one day. That is general health guidance—not a target or allowance for your event. Water helps with pacing, but it does not cancel alcohol’s effects or make driving safe.

Ask guests to plan their trip home before the tasting. Public transport, a taxi, rideshare, a designated driver who does not drink or staying overnight may all be appropriate. Anyone who has been drinking should not drive.

Rules concerning alcohol service vary between Australian states and territories and between private homes, clubs and licensed venues. This guide offers general safety guidance, not licensing or legal advice.

You can also make participation easier by providing large-print or digital notes, good lighting, comfortable seating and a quiet tasting period. Avoid scented candles, perfume and strongly flavoured food. Identify ingredients and allergens in anything served. Respect guests who are abstaining, pregnant, taking medication, recovering from alcohol dependence or simply do not want to drink.

9. End with the reveal, not a verdict

The reveal is most interesting when it explains differences rather than ranking people. Ask:

  • Which observations matched the revealed wines?
  • Did any wine surprise you?
  • Did knowing the identity change your impression?
  • Which wine did you prefer?
  • Would you choose differently next time?

The best result is not a perfect list of identities. It is a group of people who feel more confident noticing, describing and discussing wine.

Suggested FAQs

How many wines should I serve at a blind tasting?

Four to six is a practical range for a casual home event. There is no universal limit, so shorten the lineup if the group is small, the wines are intense or the event includes substantial food and conversation.

Can beginners host a blind tasting?

Yes. The host needs a clear format, concealed bottles, consistent serving and a welcoming attitude—not formal wine qualifications.

Should guests guess the grape or region?

Only if they want to. Record sensory observations first, and treat guesses as hypotheses rather than proof of expertise.

What should I do if one wine seems faulty?

Pause, compare impressions and set it aside if necessary. If possible, compare another bottle. Avoid diagnosing confidently from one person’s reaction.

Does water make drinking wine safer?

Water can help pace the event, but it does not remove alcohol from the body or make driving safe.
