What Is Sagrantino? A Beginner’s Guide

Sagrantino is an Italian red grape known for deep colour, firm tannin and fresh structure. Learn the difference between dry Secco and sweet Passito, plus what Australian drinkers can expect.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Sagrantino is a red wine-grape variety, not a general wine category.
  • It is particularly associated with Montefalco in Umbria, central Italy.
  • Wines made from it are often deeply coloured, tannic, structured and full-bodied or substantial.
  • Acidity can give Sagrantino freshness alongside its weight and tannin.
  • Young examples may feel firm or austere, while bottle age can make the structure feel more integrated.
  • Sagrantino can be made as a dry red wine or as a traditional sweet wine called Passito.
  • The variety is also grown in Australia, although Australian examples may differ significantly from wines made in Umbria.

Quick answer: what is Sagrantino?

Sagrantino is a dark-skinned red grape variety best known through the wines of Montefalco in Umbria, Italy. Its typical wines combine deep colour, substantial body, high tannin and refreshing acidity. You may find flavours and aromas suggesting cherries, forest fruits, mulberries and violets, although the finished wine depends on climate, vineyard site, ripeness, extraction, oak and bottle age.

The name can describe the grape, but it also appears within the protected regional designation Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG. That denomination is specifically associated with Montefalco and includes two important expressions: the dry Secco and the sweet traditional Passito.

For an Australian wine drinker, the simplest expectation is a red wine with plenty of structure. If you enjoy wines with firm tannin, deep colour and the ability to develop in the bottle, Sagrantino is a variety worth exploring.

Sagrantino, Montefalco and Umbria: what is the difference?

It helps to separate three names:

  • Sagrantino is the grape variety.
  • Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG is a protected Italian wine denomination.
  • Umbria is the broader Italian region in which Montefalco is located.

Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG is made from 100% Sagrantino. This is different from every wine labelled simply with a broader geographic name. For example, Montefalco Rosso is a separate denomination in which Sangiovese is the base grape and Sagrantino may be included as part of the blend.

So, a wine from Umbria is not automatically Sagrantino, and a wine from Montefalco is not automatically 100% Sagrantino. The wording on the label matters.

What does Sagrantino taste like?

Sagrantino is commonly associated with:

  • deep colour;
  • firm, abundant tannin;
  • good acidity;
  • substantial body;
  • dark and red fruit flavours;
  • floral notes such as violet;
  • a long, structured finish.

The word tannin describes the drying, gently gripping sensation that can make your mouth feel firm or puckered. You can learn more in our guide to what tannins are.

Sagrantino’s tannin is one of its defining features, but the grape is not simply about power. Its acidity can provide lift and freshness, helping the wine feel more balanced rather than merely heavy. Alcohol, ripeness and winemaking still vary from bottle to bottle, so the grape name does not guarantee one fixed level of richness.

Oak may contribute flavours such as vanilla and can change how the tannins feel, but oak is a winemaking influence rather than an inherent Sagrantino flavour.

Why can young Sagrantino taste austere?

Young Sagrantino can feel firm because its thick skins and naturally high polyphenolic content contribute colour and tannin. Winemakers may also use extended contact with the grape skins to extract more structure.

That does not mean every Sagrantino needs years of ageing, or that every young bottle will be difficult to enjoy. Extraction, fruit ripeness, acidity, oak and the quality of the tannins all matter. With bottle age, tannins may feel more integrated and the wine may show greater complexity.

Many examples have the structure to mature well, but there is no universal drinking window. Storage conditions, vintage and winemaking decisions make a considerable difference. If a young wine tastes especially firm, decanting may be worth considering; our guide to when wine should be decanted explains the broader principle.

Is Sagrantino dry or sweet?

It can be either, depending on the style.

Montefalco Sagrantino Secco is the dry red expression. It is typically a substantial, tannic wine suited to rich savoury food.

Montefalco Sagrantino Passito is a traditional sweet expression made from selected grapes that have been dried for at least two months before fermentation. Passito is not a different grape. It is a different way of making wine from Sagrantino.

Passito’s sweetness should not be confused with fruitiness. A dry wine can smell and taste of ripe fruit while containing little perceptible sweetness. Conversely, a sweet wine can still have enough tannin and acidity to feel structured.

How is Sagrantino grown and made in Australia?

Australian Wine Research Institute material reports Sagrantino plantings in the Murray–Darling, Swan Hill, Heathcote, King Valley, McLaren Vale, Eden Valley and Granite Belt. The variety is recognised in Australian industry resources and has been described as suited to hot, dry conditions.

These plantings should be kept in perspective: Sagrantino is an alternative variety in Australia, not one of the country’s major mainstream grapes. Australian Sagrantino may also taste different from Montefalco examples. Climate, soil, irrigation, crop level, ripeness and cellar technique can affect tannin, alcohol, fruit weight and freshness.

A bottle labelled Sagrantino therefore tells you the grape, but not the entire style. As with any wine, tasting the finished bottle matters more than relying on the variety name alone. Our guide to how to taste wine can help you assess it confidently.

What food goes with Sagrantino?

Start with the wine’s structure rather than following a rigid list of rules. Dry Sagrantino’s tannin generally works well with protein-rich and richly textured foods, while its acidity can refresh the palate.

Useful starting points include:

  • grilled or braised meat;
  • game;
  • slow-cooked dishes;
  • mature hard cheese.

Passito can work with aged cheese, dry biscuits, desserts and dark chocolate. With sweet wine, choose food that is at least as sweet as the wine so the wine does not taste flat or harsh.

Serve a dry Montefalco Sagrantino at around 18°C and Passito cooler, around 12°C, according to regional serving guidance. A large red-wine glass can make it easier to notice the wine’s aromas, but the glass is less important than serving the wine at a sensible temperature and tasting it without rushing.

Frequently asked questions

Is Sagrantino the same as Sangiovese?

No. They are different Italian red grape varieties. Sagrantino is associated particularly with Montefalco and is known for its firm tannin and deep colour. Sangiovese is the principal grape in several other Italian wines and may form the base of Montefalco Rosso.

Is Sagrantino always full-bodied?

It is often substantial and structured, but the finished style varies with climate, ripeness, extraction, oak and winemaking. The grape name indicates a tendency, not a guarantee.

Is Sagrantino always sweet?

No. Dry Secco is an important expression. Passito is the traditional sweet style, made from partially dried grapes.

Is Australian Sagrantino the same as Italian Sagrantino?

It is the same grape variety, but the wine may differ. Australian climate, site and winemaking choices can change the wine’s ripeness, tannin, alcohol and freshness.
