How to send wine back at a restaurant

Learn what the restaurant wine tasting is for, how to raise a concern about a faulty bottle, and why personal preference may be handled differently in Australia.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

If a restaurant opens a bottle of wine for you to taste, that small pour is generally a check that the wine is the correct bottle and appears sound—not a test of whether you happen to like its style. If something seems wrong, you can politely explain what you have noticed and ask the server, sommelier or manager to assess it.

Highlights

  1. Check the bottle before it is opened.
    Make sure the producer, wine, variety and vintage match what you ordered, where those details apply.

  2. Taste the sample.
    Smell it and take a small sip. You do not need to identify the region, judge its quality or give a formal tasting note.

  3. Describe the problem, rather than announcing a diagnosis.
    You might say:

    “I’m not sure this bottle is sound. It smells quite musty and muted. Would you mind having someone taste it?”

    Or:

    “I think this may be oxidised, but I’m not certain. Could you please check it?”

  4. Let the restaurant assess the bottle.
    The server may involve a sommelier or manager, where available. If the restaurant agrees that there is a problem, ask what replacement options are available.

  5. Be clear if it is simply not your style.
    A wine can be perfectly sound but still feel too dry, sweet, acidic, tannic, full-bodied or unfamiliar for your taste. You can ask courteously whether another option is possible, but a replacement in this situation may depend on the restaurant and the circumstances.

What might indicate a faulty wine?

A musty, mouldy or damp-cardboard-like smell can be associated with cork taint. Pronounced oxidation can make a wine seem unusually dull, flat or bruised, with cardboard-like or sherry-like characters.

These are examples, not a diagnosis test. Unusual does not automatically mean faulty: some wines have deliberately distinctive aromas, and people vary in their sensitivity to different wine characters. If you are unsure, describing what you smell or taste is more useful than confidently calling the wine “corked”.

The Australian Wine Research Institute notes that wine faults can be misdiagnosed and that tasters do not all perceive them in the same way. You do not need to get the technical label right before asking for help.

Fault, serving issue or preference?

It helps to separate three situations:

  • A possible fault: the wine itself seems affected, perhaps by cork taint or excessive oxidation.
  • A serving issue: the bottle is not the one ordered, or the wine has been presented in an unsuitable condition.
  • A preference issue: the wine is sound, but you do not enjoy its style or chose something different from what you wanted.

Raise a possible fault or serving issue promptly, ideally before the wine is poured for everyone. Keep the explanation factual and respectful. Hospitality staff can investigate the bottle more effectively when they know exactly what you noticed.

Is the restaurant required to take it back?

Restaurant practice varies between Australian venues. The tasting custom is not the same thing as a statutory guarantee, and there is no simple universal entitlement to reject any wine merely because you dislike it.

Australian Consumer Law can provide remedies when goods or services fail applicable consumer guarantees, but whether those guarantees apply—and what remedy may be available—depends on the particular problem and circumstances. This article is not legal advice. For an ordinary change of preference, any replacement is likely to be at the restaurant’s discretion.

The best approach is simple: speak up early, explain what seems wrong, ask staff to check it, and accept that a sound wine you dislike may be treated differently from a faulty or incorrect bottle.

Frequently asked questions

Can I send wine back if I do not like the taste?

You can ask politely, but disliking the style is different from identifying a fault. Whether the restaurant offers an alternative will depend on its policy and the circumstances.

Do I need to know what fault the wine has?

No. Describe the aroma or flavour that concerns you and ask the restaurant to assess the bottle.

What if the server says the wine is sound?

You can calmly explain why it seems unsuitable to you. If the issue is preference rather than a fault or serving problem, the restaurant may decline to replace it.
