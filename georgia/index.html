Georgia: A Beginner’s Guide to Georgian Wine

Understand Georgia’s place in wine: its early archaeological evidence, qvevri tradition, major regions, native grapes, amber wines and useful Australian label context.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Georgia is the country in the South Caucasus, not the US state.
  • It has some of the earliest known archaeological evidence of grape wine, dating to about 6000–5800 BC.
  • Qvevri is a traditional egg-shaped earthenware vessel used for fermenting, maturing and storing wine.
  • Not all Georgian wine is made in qvevri, and not every qvevri wine is amber-coloured.
  • Kakheti is Georgia’s principal wine region, but Kartli and Imereti show how varied the country’s wine culture is.
  • Saperavi and Rkatsiteli are two important native varieties to know.
  • Georgian amber wine overlaps with what Australians often call orange wine, but the terms are not exact synonyms.
  • Georgian labels may emphasise grape, appellation, method or sweetness differently from familiar Australian labels.

Quick answer: what should an Australian wine learner understand about Georgia?

Georgia is a country in the South Caucasus, between the Black Sea and the Caucasus mountain systems. It shares land borders with Russia, Azerbaijan, Armenia and Turkey. This is Georgia the country—not the US state.

Georgia matters in wine because it combines a very old archaeological record with a living winemaking tradition. Evidence from two Neolithic sites indicates grape wine production there around 6000–5800 BC. That makes Georgia one of the earliest known centres of grape winemaking. It does not, however, prove that Georgia was the single birthplace of wine or that modern techniques have continued unchanged for 8,000 years.

The country is especially associated with qvevri: large, egg-shaped earthenware vessels that are commonly buried or installed below ground. Grapes may ferment and mature in contact with their skins, seeds and stems, producing wines with deeper colour, more texture and sometimes noticeable tannin. But qvevri is a vessel and method, not a synonym for Georgian wine. Georgia also produces modern European-style wines in contemporary vessels.

For a first taste of Georgian wine language, begin with three ideas: Kakheti, Saperavi and Rkatsiteli. Kakheti is the major eastern wine region. Saperavi is an important red variety, while Rkatsiteli is a leading white variety. Both can be made in more than one style, so the grape name alone does not tell you exactly what will be in the glass.

Where is Georgia in wine terms?

Georgia’s clearest geographic description is South Caucasus. Its western edge meets the Black Sea, while the country extends through valleys, foothills and mountainous areas. That varied geography helps explain why Georgian wine should not be reduced to one national flavour profile.

The most useful regional starting point is Kakheti in eastern Georgia. Its important vineyard areas include the Alazani and Iori basins. Kakheti is strongly associated with Saperavi, Rkatsiteli and traditional qvevri wines, although both traditional and modern European-style wines are made there.

Kartli, in central Georgia, is associated with the Mtkvari and its tributary valleys. Its wines include classical European-style and sparkling wines, as well as varieties such as Chinuri, Goruli Mtsvane, Tavkveri, Shavkapito and Saperavi.

Imereti, in western Georgia, is another important region. Its traditional method generally uses a smaller proportion of grape skins and stems than the fuller, more extraction-driven Kakhetian approach. A western Georgian qvevri-type vessel may also be called a churi.

Racha-Lechkhumi and other western areas add further regional diversity. Protected names such as Tsinandali, Mukuzani, Kindzmarauli and Khvanchkara are useful examples of Georgian wine geography. These names should not automatically be treated like broad regions: some refer to tightly defined appellations or microzones.

Why is Georgian wine history important?

Archaeological research at Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora found chemical and botanical evidence consistent with grape wine from approximately 6000–5800 BC.

That is a strong basis for saying that Georgia has some of the earliest known archaeological evidence of grape wine. It is more careful—and more accurate—than saying simply that Georgia is “the birthplace of wine”.

Archaeology can show that wine was made at particular places and times. It cannot, by itself, prove an uninterrupted chain of identical methods from the Neolithic period to today. Nor does it show that all modern Georgian wine is made according to ancient techniques.

Qvevri winemaking is also culturally significant in its own right. UNESCO inscribed the Ancient Georgian traditional qvevri wine-making method on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013. The practice remains connected with families, communities, celebrations and everyday cultural life.

That recognition is evidence of a living cultural tradition. It is not a judgement that qvevri wine is automatically better, more authentic or more natural than wine made in another way.

What is qvevri winemaking?

A qvevri is an egg-shaped earthenware vessel used for fermentation, maturation and storage. It is commonly buried in the ground or installed below ground level, helping to moderate temperature.

In a traditional process, grape juice or must may be placed in the vessel with grape skins, seeds and sometimes stems. Fermentation takes place, followed by further contact with the grape solids. The vessel may then be sealed while the wine matures and is stored.

This contact with skins, seeds and stems can change the wine’s texture and structure. White grapes may produce wines that are deeper in colour and more phenolic—the word used for the compounds that can contribute bitterness, grip and tannin. Red wines may also gain colour, structure and a distinctive texture.

The details vary. Traditional Kakhetian wines commonly use a substantial amount of grape solids, while Imeretian methods generally use less. These are regional tendencies, not rules that describe every bottle.

Most importantly, not all Georgian wine is qvevri-made. Georgian winemakers also use modern European-style methods and vessels. Saperavi, for example, can be made in both qvevri and modern styles.

It is also a mistake to assume that every qvevri wine is amber or orange. The vessel, the grape variety, the length of skin contact and the winemaking choices all matter.

Which Georgian grapes should beginners know?

Saperavi

Saperavi is a leading Georgian red grape, particularly associated with Kakheti. It can be used for dry red wines, as well as rosé and sparkling styles, and can be made using qvevri or modern European-style methods.

It is often associated with deeply coloured and structured wines, but there is no single fixed Saperavi taste. Vineyard site, ripeness, extraction, vessel and maturation can all change the result.

Rkatsiteli

Rkatsiteli is a leading Georgian white grape, strongly associated with Kakheti. It can be made as a conventional white wine without extended skin contact, or as a skin-contact wine in qvevri.

The difference can be striking. A fresh white Rkatsiteli and a skin-contact Rkatsiteli may share a grape name but have very different colour, texture and structure. In the latter style, contact with skins and seeds can contribute amber colour, phenolic grip and savoury or dried-fruit notes.

Other varieties—including Mtsvane Kakhuri, Chinuri, Goruli Mtsvane, Tsitska, Tsolikouri, Krakhuna, Aleksandrouli and Mujuretuli—help show Georgia’s indigenous diversity. You do not need to memorise a catalogue to understand the country. Saperavi and Rkatsiteli are useful anchors; the wider variety of grapes is a reminder that Georgia is not a one-grape wine culture.

What styles of Georgian wine are produced?

Georgian wine includes:

  • Dry reds, especially Saperavi-based wines made in modern or qvevri styles.
  • Dry whites, including Rkatsiteli and Mtsvane Kakhuri made without extended skin contact.
  • Amber or skin-contact whites, where white grapes spend time with skins and seeds.
  • Semi-sweet reds, including wines associated with protected names such as Kindzmarauli and Khvanchkara.
  • Sparkling and rosé wines, which show that Georgian production extends beyond still reds and qvevri whites.

The word dry means that the wine has little residual sugar; it does not mean flavourless. A dry Georgian wine may still be aromatic, richly textured or fruit-driven.

Is amber wine the same as orange wine?

The terms overlap, but they are not perfectly interchangeable.

Internationally, orange wine commonly describes white grapes fermented or macerated with their skins. The resulting wine may be darker in colour and more textural than a conventional white. Amber wine is often used for a similar category, particularly when discussing Georgian traditional wines.

But “amber” can describe colour or a Georgian style, while “orange” is a broader contemporary international term. A Georgian amber wine may be made in qvevri, but qvevri wines are not all amber. Likewise, a skin-contact white wine is not necessarily Georgian or qvevri-made.

There is an additional Australian wrinkle. Wine Australia notes that Orange is also a registered geographical indication for a New South Wales wine region. As a result, “orange wine” has specific implications on an Australian label and cannot simply be used as an unrestricted style description when the fruit does not come from the Orange GI. “Amber” or carefully worded descriptions may be used instead, provided the presentation is not misleading.

For Australian learners, the practical lesson is simple: in tasting conversation, “orange wine” may mean skin-contact white wine; on a label, geographical names and style descriptions can carry different meanings.

How might a Georgian label differ from an Australian label?

Australian drinkers are used to seeing a grape variety and region presented prominently together—for example, a variety followed by an Australian place name. Georgian labels may foreground a different combination of information:

  • the grape variety;
  • a protected appellation or locality;
  • qvevri or another production method;
  • a style such as dry or semi-sweet.

Do not assume that a word such as “traditional”, “amber”, “white” or “qvevri” maps exactly to an Australian category. Look for the named grape, place, appellation and method together.

A protected name such as Mukuzani or Tsinandali is not simply a poetic description. It refers to a defined geographical designation. Some Georgian protected names are closely bounded appellations, so they should not be treated as interchangeable with a large regional label.

The useful takeaway

Georgia is best understood as a diverse wine country with three connected stories: very early archaeological evidence of grape wine, a living qvevri tradition and a modern industry that also uses contemporary European-style winemaking.

When you encounter Georgian wine, ask:

  1. Which grape is named?
  2. Is a region or protected appellation identified?
  3. Was qvevri or skin contact used?
  4. Is the wine dry or semi-sweet?
  5. Is “amber” describing colour, style or both?

Those questions are more useful than assuming every Georgian wine will be ancient, rustic, amber-coloured or made in qvevri. Georgian wine becomes much easier to understand once tradition is treated as part of the picture—not the whole picture.
