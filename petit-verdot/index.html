What Is Petit Verdot? A Beginner’s Guide

Learn what Petit Verdot is, how it tastes, why it is used in blends, how it differs from Petite Sirah and where it fits in Australian wine.

Foundation guide · Beginner friendly · Australian context

Highlights

  • Petit Verdot is a red wine grape variety, not a wine style or sweetness category.
  • It is traditionally associated with Bordeaux, where it is usually used in blends.
  • The grape is valued for deep colour, firm tannin, fresh acidity and intense aromas.
  • It ripens late, so the vineyard site and growing season matter.
  • In Australia, it appears in both blends and varietal wines.
  • Petit Verdot is not the same grape as Petite Sirah. In Australian wine usage, Petite Sirah generally refers to Durif.

Quick answer: what is Petit Verdot?

Petit Verdot is a black-skinned Vitis vinifera grape used to make red wine. It originated in France and is best known for its traditional role in Bordeaux-style blends, where it can add colour, tannin, acidity, spice and aromatic depth.

Although it is often blended with varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot, Petit Verdot can also be bottled on its own. These varietal wines tend to show the grape’s structure and intensity more directly, although the final style depends on climate, ripeness, harvest decisions and winemaking.

Petit Verdot is a late-ripening variety. It needs a suitable site and growing season to reach full maturity, which helps explain why it is used in different ways in different regions.

What does Petit Verdot taste like?

Petit Verdot commonly makes deeply coloured, structured red wines. Typical tendencies can include:

  • firm or pronounced tannins;
  • fresh, vibrant acidity;
  • medium-full to full-bodied texture;
  • dark or red fruit;
  • spice and liquorice;
  • violet or other floral notes; and
  • savoury aromas such as cocoa, coffee, leather or tobacco.

These are useful recognition cues, not a fixed recipe. A warm-climate wine may taste riper and fuller, while a different site, vintage or winemaking approach may produce a fresher or more restrained expression.

Tannin is the drying, gently grippy sensation that can make your mouth feel firmer after a sip. Petit Verdot’s tannins can be especially noticeable, so a wine made largely from the variety may feel more structured than a softer red. You can learn more about this sensation in our guide to tannins in wine, as well as our explanations of wine body and acidity.

Why is Petit Verdot used in blends?

In a blend, Petit Verdot can work like a structural seasoning. Even when it is not the main variety, it may contribute:

  • darker colour;
  • tannin and shape;
  • acidity and freshness;
  • spicy or floral aromas; and
  • extra complexity.

This is why it has traditionally been associated with blends based on Cabernet Sauvignon and Merlot. It does not mean that every Bordeaux blend contains Petit Verdot. Rather, the variety can be included when its particular colour, structure and aroma are useful.

A varietal Petit Verdot gives those characteristics more room to speak. Expect the grape’s colour, tannin and aromatic intensity to be more prominent, but do not assume that every bottle will be equally powerful, alcoholic or ripe.

Why does ripening matter?

Petit Verdot ripens later than many familiar red grape varieties. In a short or cool growing season, reaching full ripeness can be more challenging. In a suitable warm site, it can develop the concentration and balance needed for a varietal wine.

That does not make the grape automatically unsuitable for cooler regions. Site, season, crop levels, harvest timing and winemaking all influence the result. The practical lesson is simple: Petit Verdot does not have one universal style.

This is also why climate can help explain differences between wines. Our guide to how climate affects wine explores the broader relationship between growing conditions and flavour.

Petit Verdot in Australia

Australia grows Petit Verdot, and Australian drinkers may encounter it either as part of a blend or as a wine labelled primarily with the variety’s name.

Wine Australia’s 2023 Petit Verdot snapshot reported 16,122 tonnes crushed in 2023. It also reported that the largest shares of that crush came from the Riverland, Riverina and Murray Darling–Swan Hill areas, with smaller reported shares from regions including Coonawarra and Margaret River.

The same snapshot recorded 1,120 hectares planted in Australia, but that planting figure is based on the ABS 2015 dataset. It should not be read as a current 2026 planting total.

Australian Petit Verdot is not one single taste. Climate, site, season, ripeness, harvest decisions, extraction, oak and maturation can all change the finished wine. A bottle from Australia may therefore be richly coloured and full-bodied, or show a fresher and more tightly structured style.

How might it appear on a label?

When “Petit Verdot” appears prominently on a label, it tells you the wine is identified primarily by that grape variety, subject to applicable labelling rules. It may be a varietal wine or part of a broader blend whose composition is explained elsewhere on the label or producer information.

The word “dry” is separate from the grape name. Dry means the wine has little residual sugar; it does not mean that the wine lacks fruit flavour. A dry Petit Verdot can still smell or taste of ripe berries, dark fruit, spice or liquorice.

That distinction is useful whenever a wine tastes fruity but is not sweet.

Is Petit Verdot the same as Petite Sirah?

No. Petit Verdot and Petite Sirah are separate grape identities.

In Australian wine usage, Petite Sirah generally refers to Durif, a different variety. The names sound similar, but they should not be treated as synonyms. If you are comparing labels, look for the exact grape name rather than relying on the first word.

Petit Verdot is also distinct from varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Shiraz, even though it may be blended with them. You can explore those grapes in our guides to Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot and Shiraz.

Petit Verdot FAQs

Is Petit Verdot sweet?

Not inherently. Petit Verdot is a grape variety, not a sweetness level. A wine made from it may be dry, off-dry or sweet depending on how it is made. Fruit aromas and ripe flavours do not necessarily mean the wine contains much sugar.

What is Petit Verdot known for?

It is best known for deep colour, firm tannin, fresh acidity, late ripening and intense spicy, floral or dark-fruited aromas. In blends, it can add structure and aromatic depth.

Is Petit Verdot common in Australia?

It is an established Australian variety with recorded plantings and crush. Wine Australia’s data shows that it is grown across several regions, but the available figures should be read with their dates and dataset limitations rather than as a claim that it is one of Australia’s dominant varieties.

Is Petit Verdot a Bordeaux grape?

It is traditionally associated with Bordeaux and has a long blending role there. It is not a Bordeaux appellation or a wine style, and it is now grown in other wine regions, including Australia.

The takeaway

Think of Petit Verdot as a dark, structured red grape that can bring colour, tannin, acidity and aromatic intensity. In a blend it may provide backbone and detail; as a varietal wine, those traits are more likely to take centre stage. Its late-ripening nature means place and season matter—and Australian examples can show more than one expression.
